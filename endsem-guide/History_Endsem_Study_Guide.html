SESSION12

Mariners, Merchants and Monks in the Indian Ocean: From Connected Space to Connected Lives

Readings: Ranabir Chakravarti, "Indic Mercantile Networks and the Indian Ocean World: A Millennial Overview (c. 500–1500 CE)", in A. Schottenhammer (ed.), Early Global Interconnectivity across the Indian Ocean World, vol. I (2019), ch. 8, pp. 191–224 (RB5) · Mansur al-Sharida, "Merchants without Borders: Quṣmān Traders in the Arabian Gulf and the Indian Ocean, c. 1850–1950", Arabian Humanities 17 (2023) (OT8) · Lecture 12 PPT
Where this fits / why it matters Session 11 gave us the space (the Indian Ocean as one connected arena). This session asks who made that space work. The answer is a human-centred one: mariners (who mastered routes and winds), merchants (who organised exchange and risk) and monks and religious communities (who carried texts, rituals and patronage). Two readings from two very different periods let us see continuity and change "in the same breath": Chakravarti covers the deep medieval foundations (c. 500–1500), al-Sharida a modern transformation (c. 1850–1950).

12.1 The lecture's framing Must know

The big lecture argument The Indian Ocean was connected by human mobility, not geography alone.
  • Mariners: mastered routes, winds and shipping across huge distances.
  • Merchants: linked ports to hinterlands; organised exchange and risk.
  • Monks and religious communities: carried texts, ritual, patronage and cultural forms.
  • Trade was never only economic: it produced diasporas, multilingual settings and shared institutions of trust.

Key idea: sacred geography and commercial geography overlapped constantly.

Subrahmanyam's "connected space", in simple words
  • Places were linked by movement of people and goods, not by fixed borders.
  • Regions were shaped by encounters; contact left lasting marks.
  • Local histories sit inside larger processes; no region stands fully apart.
  • Connections were structural and repeated, not occasional accidents.

Why read the two articles together? Chakravarti gives the pre-1500 monsoon world; al-Sharida gives the 1850–1950 imperial-commercial world: "two phases of one phenomenon". The same ocean kept rewarding mobility and trust, while scale, technology and imperial structures changed.

12.2 Chakravarti: why the Indian Ocean, why South Asia, why 500–1500 Must know

The ocean's shape

  • About 20% of the earth's maritime space; the only enclosed ocean; the longest continuous history of seafaring (at least five millennia).
  • Monsoons do not operate south of 10° S, so "Indian Ocean history" really means its northern part.
  • The Java Sea, Gulf of Siam and South China Sea are geographically Pacific but historically part of the same world, an "Indo-Pacific rim".
  • Few straits (Hormuz, Malacca; Palk unusable), so islands, deltas and estuaries served as strategic points instead.

The historiographical gap

  • Most writing focuses on 1500–1800/1900 because the Portuguese Estado da Índia and the three East India Companies left huge "official" records and serial data.
  • Michael Pearson: those centuries are only a tiny part of a much longer history.
  • Much is written on 1st c. BCE–3rd c. CE (the misnamed "Indo-Roman" trade, really a Red Sea trade with many Asian and African participants).
  • That leaves "a yawning gap" of more than a millennium. Chakravarti fills it.

Why South Asia is central

  • Not an Indocentric view replacing a Eurocentric one, but a geographical fact: the subcontinent and Sri Lanka sit at the centre of the enclosed ocean.
  • No voyage between the western and eastern ends could be finished in one monsoon season, so India's two long coasts became stopovers, gateways and transshipment points.
  • Lecture: South Asia was "a hinge, not an edge", linking Red Sea and Gulf circuits to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and China.
Sources and two clear messages

Textual (Indic and foreign), epigraphic, coins, archaeology and images, plus two newer sources: the Cairo Geniza letters of Jewish "India traders" (S.D. Goitein's term; Friedman calls the India trade "the backbone" of the medieval international economy) and shipwreck archaeology. They are mostly qualitative and scattered, but together they let us "hear many voices".

  1. Indic peoples did go to sea, against the old idea of a religious taboo on sea voyages.
  2. Trade carried daily necessities in bulk, not only luxuries.
Lecture: "segmented mobility"

Connectivity was built in stages, not as one straight voyage: predictable monsoons set the calendar; goods stopped at stopover ports and were transshipped; journeys went ship → port → caravan → hinterland. Chaudhuri dates "emporia trade" and "segmented voyages" to after 1000 CE. The Belitung wreck (9th c.) is the opposite case: one stitched ship sailing all the way from the western Indian Ocean to China and back.

The same logic continued later: goods reached Gulf ports by sea, then went inland by camel caravan to al-Qasim and Najd (al-Sharida).

12.3 Chakravarti, 500–800 CE: the western and eastern seaboards Supporting

Western seaboard

  • Socotra inscriptions (1st–5th c., Brahmi script, Sanskrit; Ingo Strauch): Indian visitors from Gujarat ports Bhrigukachchha (Broach) and Hastakavapra; sailors (nāvika) and navigators (niryāmaka); drawings of plank ships stitched with coconut coir, single and double masts (like ships on Satavahana coins and in the Aurangabad caves).
  • Cosmas Indicopleustes (late 6th c.): Sopara, Kalyan and Chaul on the Konkan.
  • Charter of Vishnusena (592 CE) at Lohatagrama (Kathiawad): privileges for the merchant body vaniggrāma (the later manigramam).
  • Byzantine coins at Pattanam (Muziris): the network continued after the Roman trade faded.
  • Big shift: the Persian Gulf regained importance over the Red Sea (Sassanian–Byzantine rivalry). Chalukya Pulakeshin II (610–642) captured Puri (Elephanta), poetically "the goddess of fortune of the western sea"; al-Tabari records diplomacy with Khosrow II.

Eastern seaboard

  • Kaveripattinam in the Kaveri delta peaked in the 5th–6th c. (Tamil epics Silappadikaram, Manimekalai).
  • Tamralipta (Tamluk), the outlet of the landlocked Ganga valley: Faxian sailed from it to Sri Lanka; Yijing records a Malay Peninsula–Tamralipta network and the role of Srivijaya; Dandin calls it a velākula (port).
  • Yijing's "Ho-lai-ka-lo" = Harikela (Chittagong), near Arakan, a door to mainland Southeast Asia. Xuanzang notes Samatata (Comilla) linked to six Southeast Asian places.
  • Buddhagupta, a master mariner (mahānāvika) of Raktamrittika (Bengal), 5th/6th c. inscription in the Malay Peninsula.

12.4 Chakravarti, 800–1500 CE: ports, Geniza merchants and Chola power Must know

(a) The western ocean

  • Arabic and Persian writers (Sulayman 851, Ibn Khurradadhbih, al-Masudi, Buzurg ibn Shahriyar, Hudud al-Alam 982, al-Biruni, al-Idrisi, Ibn Battuta) describe Indian ports. Caution: most never visited India (only al-Masudi, al-Biruni and Ibn Battuta did), so accounts can be stereotyped.
  • Abbasids (750–870) + the great port of Siraf → Gulf dominant (good for Konkan and Gujarat). Fatimids in Egypt (969) + Siraf's decline → the Red Sea revived, with Egypt as a "hinge" to the Mediterranean. From the 13th c. Hormuz revived the Gulf.
  • Cambay replaced Broach as Gujarat's chief port, helped by Ghogha and Somnath. Somnath inscriptions of 1264 and 1287: the shipowner (nakhuda) Nuruddin Firuz of Hormuz; a merchants' quarter called "Sikottari-mahajanapalli" (linked to Socotra). Al-Biruni linked Somnath to Zanzibar.
  • Konkan: indented coast ideal for ports (Sanjan, Thana, Sopara, Chaul) under the Silaharas; agrarian growth fed maritime prosperity.
  • Malabar: Quilon from the 9th c.; Calicut rises in the early 14th c. (Ibn Battuta: Chinese junks and western ships meet there for transshipment).
The Cairo Geniza: the ocean as "a world of letters" (lecture)

Merchant letters preserved by chance in the Ben Ezra synagogue, Fustat (Old Cairo). Route: Jewish traders from Tunisia → Fustat → Red Sea ports Qus/Aidhab → Aden → Malabar.

  • Madmun b. Hasan-Japheth (d. c. 1151), representative of merchants (wakil al-tujjar) at Aden, in a shipping partnership with Aden's sultan Bilal b. Jarir.
  • Abraham Ben Yiju (d. c. 1156), from the Maghreb; in Malabar 1132–1149 at Mangalore, where he ran a bronze factory; married a local Tulu woman, Ashu (formerly his slave), and had three children. His agent Bama (Tulu, once called "Sheikh") travelled to Egypt.
  • Indic shipowners rarely appear elsewhere but do here: Fatanswami (Pattanasvami), Ali b. Fawfali (an areca-nut dealer turned shipowner), Pdyr (Padiyara).
  • Itinerant names: Joseph al-Adani al-Mamsawi (Aden → Morocco), Abu Zikri Cohen Sijilmasi.
  • Information mattered as much as goods: "One who is present can see what is not seen by one who is absent" (Joseph b. Abraham to Ben Yiju).
  • Pepper: one sale at Aden 510½ dinars; customs tithe 155⅓ dinars; prices 34 vs 23 dinars per bahar (≈100 lb). A ship of Fatanswami sank at Bab-el-Mandeb c. 1138: "The pepper was a total loss."
  • Sri Lankan cinnamon (60 bags), red cloth (lalas, "Kulami lalas"), and iron from Malabar in five grades (ingot "eggs", refurbished, standard, shiny, smooth). Only the Geniza tells us about this iron trade.
  • Gifts: fine Egyptian paper sent to Ben Yiju, a poet with a fine hand.
  • Trust across religions: in 1145 Judah ha-Kohen told his brother-in-law, robbed by pirates near Thana, to seek help from the Hindu shipowner Tinbu, with whom he had "inseparable bonds of friendship and brotherhood".
A multilingual market (Unniyaticharitam) Merchants gathered at Kandiyur in Kerala from Kanara, Malwa, Gujarat, Telugu lands, Orissa, Konkan, "Yavana" lands, China and "Turk" lands, sitting on black carpets with heaps of coins. One boasted that an Arab horse would fetch 2,000 coins in the Chola country. Lesson: bilingualism and multilingualism marked the cultural plurality of trade.
Horses: the great import India lacked the best warhorses. Central Asian "Tatari" horses came overland; from the 2nd millennium, Arabian and Persian horses came by sea ("bahri", sea-borne). Marco Polo: 10,000 a year to the Pandya kingdom alone from Shihr, Kish, Hormuz and Aden, each worth about 220 gold coins; landed on the Malabar coast and handled by Tamil horse-dealers (kudirai-chettis).

(b) The eastern ocean: the Bay of Bengal

  • Names for the Bay: Vangasagara (inscription of 971), bahr Harkal ("sea of Harikela", Hudud al-Alam), Sanskrit Purvasagara.
  • The Cholas (strongest c. 985–1150): premier port Nagapattinam (replacing Kaveripattinam); Leiden grants of Rajendra and Kulottunga; inland trade through nagaram networks at the nadu (locality) level.
  • Merchant bodies: nanadesi, manigramam, anjuvannam and the ainnurruvar ("the 500 svamis of Ayyavole"), an umbrella organisation (Karashima & Subbarayalu). They gave charities to shrines.
  • Chola embassies to the Song court: 1012, 1033, 1077.
  • Kadaram campaign (1025–26): the Chola fleet attacked 12 places including Srivijaya (Palembang), Takuapa, the Nicobars and Kadaram (Kedah). Not annexed. Purpose: to cut Srivijaya out as middleman with Song China.
  • Tamil merchant inscriptions (9th–13th c.) in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Sumatra and China. Barus 1088: merchants, shipowners, captains and sailors at the camphor port. They lack the royal preamble usual in India.
  • Vengi (Andhra): Visakhapatnam renamed Kulottungacholapattanam (1068–1090), the only port renamed after a reigning Chola king. Kulottunga remitted tolls (sunka) to attract trade. A 1090 inscription records an Anjuvannam merchant arriving from Sri Lanka, another from Pasai (Sumatra).
  • Kakatiya Ganapati (1199–1261): Motupalli charter (1245), a "charter of security" (abhayaśāsana) for voyaging merchants: protection from pirates and only fixed, listed duties, since for merchants "wealth is dearer than life". Marco Polo praised "Mutfili".
  • Bengal: Tamralipta fades after the 8th c.; Samandar (Chittagong) rises: Marco Polo's "Bengala", Ibn Battuta's "Sudkawan". Forest goods from Kamarupa (aloes wood, yak tails, rhino horn); fine textiles.
  • Cowries and rice: Bengal's currency was cowrie shells imported in shiploads from the Maldives, paid for with rice: cheap bulk goods (Ma Huan; Suma Oriental).
  • Harikela silver coins (7th–12th c.): non-dynastic, carrying only a territory's name, modelled on Arakan's Chandra coins.
  • Pala Buddhism: the Sailendra king of Java asked Devapala for land for Nalanda; Atisha trained in Suvarnadvipa (maritime Southeast Asia), sailing both ways.

12.5 Plurality, and an ocean without navies Must know

Ports as "social laboratories" (lecture)
  • Under the Rashtrakutas, Muslims from Siraf, Baghdad and Oman built mosques and appointed qadis at Saymur and Sindan.
  • The Saiva Chola kings granted land for the Buddhist Chudamani vihara at Nagapattinam at a Srivijayan king's request.
  • Kerala grants to a church and a synagogue, witnessed in Pahlavi, Hebrew and Arabic.
  • Somnath 1264: a bilingual Sanskrit–Arabic inscription records a mosque (mijigiti) built by Nuruddin Firuz with land obtained with help from Hindu "righteous friends" (dharma-bāndhava), endorsed by the Saiva priest and the Chaulukya government. Allah is called Viśvarūpa (universal) and Śūnyarūpa (formless).
William of Adam's crusade plan (c. 1316/17)

A Dominican friar's tract How to Defeat the Saracens, written after the fall of Acre (1291), saw India as the source of Mamluk Egypt's wealth. His body metaphor: goods start at the Indian sea (head), pass through Aden (throat) to Egypt (stomach). Plan: blockade Aden with galleys based at Socotra and Hormuz, diverting trade through Ilkhanid Iran. Never carried out.

Chakravarti's contrast: the Mediterranean was mare nostrum, a sea for navies. Indian Ocean rulers looked to land; the sea was "an arena for merchants, sailors, pirates, fisher-folk". Before 1500 it was an open sea (mare liberum), not a closed one (mare clausum). The Chola raid is the exception that proves the rule.

12.6 al-Sharida: the Qusman traders, c. 1850–1950 Must know

Context: a changed ocean, the same logic After the 1820 treaty with the Gulf's Arab rulers, Britain controlled India–Arabia trade and offered "commercial security"; Gulf protectorates were run from India. Steamships and the Suez Canal (1869) reintegrated the Gulf into the world economy, so pearls and dates reached Europe and North America. India became the most attractive trading centre, and Arabia–India trade "fully recovered" from the disruption caused by the Portuguese in the 16th century. Lecture: new conditions (British control, steam, Suez, colonial port rules) but continuity: dispersed nodes and trust-based networks, combining sea and land routes.

Who were they?

The Qusman (singular Qasimi) were people of al-Qasim, one of three districts of Najd (central Arabia), with towns Buraydah and Unayzah. Najd was poor in farming and industry, so it imported almost everything.

Two trades: the al-Uqaylat (overland camel exports to Iraq, Syria, Egypt, from the 18th c.; already studied) and the al-hadrat / ahl al-hadra ("people of downward", linked to Gulf ports and India; under-studied, al-Sharida's subject).

Central intervention

Inland traders were fully part of oceanic commerce. Their network ran from al-Qasim to Kuwait, Bahrain, Basra and on to Bombay, Calcutta, Karachi, Oman and East Africa (also Cairo, Damascus, Jerusalem, Baghdad, Mecca, Jeddah).

Following Peter Valenti: by finding markets for local goods and bringing in goods and foreign currency, al-Qasim became an entrepôt in Najd, showing Najd was in the global economy by the 19th c., "if not even earlier". This breaks the land/sea divide.

How goods moved

India Qusman → Bahrain Qusman → al-Jubayl / al-Uqayr → camel caravan → al-Qasim. Caravans made 3–4 round trips a year, about 20 days one way; a camel carried about 440 lb (two big bags of sugar); in the late 1920s a camel cost 15–20 Maria Theresa dollars. Example: al-Suhaymi of Unayzah at al-Jubayl received goods from the Bahrain merchant Abd al-Aziz al-Bassam.

Commodity chains: "diversification, not specialisation, was the key to survival"

Lecture link to Chakravarti: as in the earlier Indian Ocean, this was never trade in luxuries alone; everyday and strategic goods moved together, and merchants switched goods quickly as demand changed.

Family firms, agents and trust (lecture)
  • Kinship networks and agents in many cities (sons sent to India almost every year).
  • Letters and identity substituted for formal contracts; trading marks like "SS" and "HMB".
  • Repeated circulation between homeland and port, not one-way migration.
  • Profits were kept as credit with agents; rice profits split 50/50 (al-Dhukayr and al-Assafi).
  • Key families: al-Bassam, al-Assafi, al-Dhukayr, al-Mushayqih, al-Khunayni.
  • "Family and reputation were the real infrastructure behind Qasimi trade."
al-Sharida's sources and conclusion

The al-Assafi commercial records (Imam Muhammad b. Saud University, Riyadh), family letters (over 300 in al-Umari's collection), oral interviews (2018), and travellers Doughty (1877), Philby (1918), Burckhardt, and Lorimer's Gazetteer.

Conclusion: poverty pushed generations of Qusman to leave home; they built networks from Egypt to India, endured "the absence of fathers", and helped found some of the region's leading companies.

12.7 Monks, merchants and moral worlds; continuity and change Must know

Religion and commerce braided together (lecture)
  • Economic networks supported belief: pilgrimage, endowments, monastery patronage.
  • Stronger in Chakravarti: Hindu rulers supporting Buddhist and Islamic institutions in ports (Chudamani vihara, Kerala church and synagogue grants, Somnath mosque, Konkan mosques).
  • More indirect in al-Sharida: no monks, but merchant mobility linked holy cities (Mecca, Madinah) with port cities.
  • A shared vocabulary of gift, obligation and reputation ran through both commerce and religion.

13.2 Ray: textiles, "Warp and Weft" Must know

Ray's argument in three parts
  1. India produced many varieties of textile, but only some travelled across the sea (fine, patterned, high-value cloth travelled best).
  2. The organisation of trade (guilds, merchant bodies, temples) changed between the 1st and 14th centuries, but not simply from "kinship" to "big states".
  3. Cloth had multiple uses inside the subcontinent: clothing, gifts, ritual, hangings, even writing material.

The bigger point: textiles were a means of communication across a shared Indian Ocean culture, markers of identity used to build social relationships. They were not a mere luxury that Romans, Arabs or Europeans bought to pay for spices.

(a) A very old cotton tradition

  • Cultivated cotton (Gossypium arboreum, tree cotton, and G. herbaceum) at Mehrgarh (Baluchistan), 5th millennium BCE.
  • Mohenjo-daro: silver vases wrapped in red-dyed cotton (Marshall). Jute cloth at Harappa (2200–1900 BCE).
  • Dorian Fuller: spinning increased in peninsular India from the 2nd millennium BCE with growing social complexity.

(b) Cloth at home: identity, rank, ritual

  • Later Vedic texts: cotton for daily wear, wool shawls (kambala) for winter, fine linen (kśauma) for elites.
  • Arthashastra (50–125 CE): the king's treasury lists woollens, linen, cotton and silks: wild silks (patrorṇa, kauśeya) and Chinese silk (cīna).
  • Buddhism: cloth was one of only four gifts allowed to monks (robes, food, shelter, medicine). At first only cotton, so monks looked different from other ascetics. King Pasenadi's gift of fine linen to a nun caused a dispute; silk was later allowed, an example of the "middle way". A silk piece in a relic casket at Devnimori (Gujarat); a Kanheri inscription (854 CE) of a Bengali donor endowing 100 silver coins for monks' clothing.
  • Tamil Sangam poetry (2,381 poems by 473 poets): white cloth covers a dead hero; the king gives embroidered silks to a bard; a woman spins cotton by firelight. Cloth links to kingship, labour and mourning.

(c) Colour rules and indigo

  • Indigo, the oldest organic dye of the region: the Periplus lists it as an export from Barbarikon; Greek indikon melan ("Indian black"); Sanskrit nīla, which spread to Southeast Asia; Arabic an-nīla.
  • Dharmasutras (3rd–2nd c. BCE): students must avoid dyed cloth; Vasistha assigns white to brahmins, madder red to kshatriyas, yellow silk to vaishyas; Manusmriti forbids a brahmin in hardship to trade in dyed cloth or indigo.
  • Buddhist monks wore reddish brown; indigo, turmeric and patterned cloth were forbidden to them.
  • But art tells another story: patterned cloth on Bharhut and Sanchi sculptures; Ajanta (5th c.) shows tunics with geese, rosettes and chevrons; a Mathura donor was a dyer's wife. The Panchatantra's Blue Jackal falls into an indigo vat.
  • Philosophers of the Nyaya-Vaiseshika school (by the 10th c.) argued with Buddhists about the colour of multicoloured cloth and called it citra ("variegated").
The technology behind demand (lecture + Ray) Cotton does not take dye easily, so a mordant (fixative) was painted with pens (qalams) or stamped with wooden blocks. Resist dyeing covered areas with wax or mud so they stayed white. Block printing, resist dyeing and mordant work gave Indian textiles recognisable regional identities. Indigo produced durable, desirable blue.

(e) How the textile trade was organised

  • Periplus: no state control over maritime trade.
  • Nasik inscription (year 42): Usavadata, son-in-law of the satrap Nahapana, invested 3,000 kahapanas with weavers' guilds at Govardhana; only the interest was spent, as "cloth money" for monks. Guilds handled production and finance.
  • Vishnusena's charter (592): 72 trade rules; lower taxes on goods for religious use; a tax on pressing indigo.
  • The Geniza business house of Ibn Awkal (980–1030) shipped indigo to Egypt.
  • Merchant bodies Ainnurruvar, Manigramam, Nanadesi, Anjuvannam had their own armies and gave to temples and water tanks. Copper plates gave the Jewish merchant Joseph Rabban the title Anjuvannam (early 11th c.).
  • Anjuvannam = West Asian sea-going merchants, mostly Muslim by the 11th c. (Subbarayalu).
  • Tamil inscriptions in Southeast Asia (8 known, 9th–13th c.): Takua Pa (a tank and a military camp), Lobo Tuwa near Barus 1088 (a tax on ship captains and crews), north Sumatra, and a Tamil–Chinese one at a Siva temple in Quanzhou.
  • Java and Bali: local guilds called banigrama (seven Javanese inscriptions, 902–1053).
  • Java land-grant ceremonies gave 200 pieces of cloth; about 70 kinds are named, chosen by the recipient's status and sex.
Cloth as paper Cotton cloth was a writing material. Nearchos (326 BCE) saw Indians writing on beaten cotton; Kerala traders kept accounts on cloth; Karnataka used blackened kaditam. When Ben Yiju had no paper in India, he wrote on cloth; two such pieces survive in the Geniza.
Ray's conclusion: avoid generalisations Textiles were integral to the maritime network from the start of the Common Era, from coarse cotton to fine silk. Their value varied by time and place. Example: it is often said Southeast Asians stored wealth in cloth while Indians stored it in gold jewellery; but Javanese inscriptions mention cloth because of their land-grant rituals, not because Indians valued cloth less. So textiles must be studied at both the micro (local) and macro (oceanic) levels. She disagrees with John Guy's view of export cloth as made just "to the requirements of a foreign consumer".
Textiles at home and abroad (lecture) Within South Asia: cloth marked caste, monastic discipline and rank; Buddhist and Brahmanical rules shaped colours; Tamil poetry links cloth to kingship and mourning.
Across the ocean: fine cloth travelled as gifts and prestige cargo; Geniza muslins (lanas/lalas) were mostly presents to wives, partners and religious leaders; cotton was traded in quantity but was "secondary", since only precious cloth was worth the cargo space.
The futa example The futa (an untailored waist cloth) went from Egypt to Aden and on to India, for foreigners living in India (linen or silk), while Indians wore cotton. Market demand varied by consumer.

13.3 Spices: more than flavour Must know

  • Pepper, cloves, cardamom and cinnamon were portable, valuable and durable: a high value-to-weight ratio, a long shelf life, elite demand and medicinal uses.
  • They travelled as food, medicine and luxury markers; their rarity came from ecology and distance.
  • Remember the technology limit: early navigation was fragile. From al-Sirafi: if winds were too strong, merchants had to sell wherever they landed to avoid a loss, so ports mattered greatly.
  • China wanted frankincense, ivory and copper from the Arabs; the Arabs wanted spices, hence their interest in India as "an accessible gateway".
Abu-Lughod and Khatun on the system

Janet Abu-Lughod (Before European Hegemony: The World System A.D. 1250–1350, 1989): the Red Sea was the preferred route to the "Orient"; trade and politics were intertwined but not identical. After Rome fell, smaller lords controlled routes and trade adapted rather than stopped. She uses al-Muqaddasi to argue for globalisation before European hegemony.

Khatun (2025): Arab traders inserted themselves into existing Asian markets. Cabotage (short coastal hops) linked many markets. There was no single hegemonic group and no sudden "explosion"; perfumes and spices drove demand but were not solely responsible. Distance made them symbols of luxury; when their medicinal properties were studied, their value rose further.

13.4 Perfumes and aromatics as political goods Must know

  • Musk, ambergris, camphor, sandalwood and aloe wood signalled prestige.
  • They appeared in tribute, diplomacy and court ceremony ("luxury + ritual, a dual function").
  • They blurred the line between luxury, medicine and ritual, and turned ecological rarity into political capital.
  • Khatun: in Arab accounts, Indian Ocean perfumes became "a byword for wealth and power" in the Caliphate.
Why Sind mattered (Khatun) The Azd 'Uman governed areas such as Sind, which the Caliphate needed for perfumes, pearls, spices, cloth, beads and metalwork from India via Oman. Controlling Sind cut out middlemen. Goods also moved on to Egypt and, by cabotage across the Mediterranean, to the Carolingians (Charlemagne's court) from c. 700.
Display and the Belitung wreck (Khatun)

Expansion and taxation brought the Caliphate's elite huge amounts of gold. At Harun al-Rashid's wedding the bride received very large pearls and ruby-studded tunics; guests received silver, gold, ceramic bowls and perfumes in glass vessels. Writing about such gifts helped advertise foreign goods.

The Belitung shipwreck carried at least 60,000 Chinese ceramics, a sign of the market's size. All the goods on display (camphor, ceramics, pearls, Indian gold) had come through Asia.

13.5 Comparing the three, and what they changed Supporting

Shared features

All needed specialised knowledge and regional ecologies; all moved through port networks and merchant intermediaries; all carried social meanings beyond price.
vs

Different logics

Textiles showed identity and status on bodies and in rooms. Spices were concentrated, mobile value. Perfumes suited court ritual and diplomacy.
Connecting Europe and Asia through demand
  • Red Sea and Gulf routes linked Indian goods to Mediterranean consumers.
  • Islamic merchants connected Indian Ocean and European luxury markets.
  • Europe entered older exchange systems rather than creating them (a pre-European connected world).
  • Consumer desire helped bind distant regions into one commercial field.
  • The Caliphate as a hinge: Baghdad absorbed and redistributed goods to Egypt and Europe; tribute, gifts and trade reinforced one another; expansion intensified but did not invent these networks.
What these commodities changed
  • They shaped tastes and consumer cultures.
  • They encouraged merchant diasporas and port settlements.
  • They transmitted techniques, words and aesthetics (nīla → an-nīla).
  • They made the Indian Ocean "a lived material world, not just a map".

Lecture synthesis: the ocean connected Europe and Asia "not only through ships and sailors, but through fabrics worn on bodies, spices used in kitchens and medicines, and perfumes burned in courts and rituals."

Islam and the Indian Ocean World: From Maritime Mobility to Muslim Communities, Networks and Political Imaginaries

Reading: Geoff Wade, "Islam Across the Indian Ocean to 1500 CE", in A. Schottenhammer (ed.), Early Global Interconnectivity across the Indian Ocean World, vol. II (2019), ch. 5, pp. 85–138 (RB5) · Lecture 14 PPT (which also draws on Mahmood Kooria's microhistory of Ponnāni)
Where this fits / why it matters Session 10 showed religions adapting locally; Session 12 showed monks and merchants on the same routes. This session follows one religion across the whole ocean. Central question: how did the Indian Ocean shape the making of Islam in Asia? The lecture's arc runs from merchants → ports → communities, then to networks → political authority → localised Islam → maritime conflict. The key shift: from asking "how did Islam spread?" to asking "how did Muslim communities become embedded and reshape their identities?"

14.1 Framing: the monsoon makes the network Must know

The "Indian Ocean world" (Pearson)

Not a body of water but a connected space made by people (merchants, sailors, migrants, scholars, pilgrims), goods (spices, textiles, horses) and ideas (religion, law, knowledge, political concepts). Michael Pearson treats the ocean as an arena of connection, not an obstacle. For Malabar, the most meaningful frontier was not inland India but the ocean itself.

Wade's framework

Seasonal winds → repeated voyages and long stays → merchant settlements, intermarriage, religious institutions. The pattern: "visitor becomes resident, resident becomes community, community becomes institution". Route: Persian Gulf → Quilon → Sri Lanka → Kedah → Sumatra → Java → China.

"Islam does not travel alone"

People, commerce, languages, institutions, practices and political ideas move as one bundle. Method: replace "Islam spread to India" with "Who brought Islam, and through what relationships?" Carriers: Arab and Persian merchants, Gujarati and South Indian Muslims, Sufis, scholars, sailors, refugees.

Wade's sources and the trading background
  • Islam is harder to track than Buddhism or Hinduism because it has no iconography. Sources: a few texts, shipwreck finds (jewellery moulds, prayer beads), tombstones, and newly used Chinese texts.
  • Baghdad (762) and Basra drove Gulf–East Asia trade; under the Buyids (932–1044) Siraf led. Hourani: the Umayyad and Abbasid empires coexisting with Tang China helped long voyages (about six months to Canton).
  • The 878 massacre of foreign merchants at Canton ended direct voyages → segmented trade (Arabian Sea / Bay of Bengal / South China Sea). Gulf hubs moved Siraf → Kish (11th–13th c.) → Hormuz (14th–15th c.).
  • Egypt rose from the 10th c. (al-Maqdisi, 985: Fustat "like the Baghdad of old"); Aden was the key port; the Karimi merchants, organised in guilds, ran much of Egypt's Indian Ocean trade.
  • Eaton: by the late 7th c. Muslims saw themselves as carriers of a global civilisation; Arabic and Islam gave "civilizational coherence".

14.2 Merchants to communities in South India Must know

Muslim presence did not require Muslim rule André Wink: outside conquered Sind, Makran and Punjab, permanent Muslim communities before the 11th c. were found only in coastal towns, from Gujarat and Konkan to Malabar, Coromandel, Sri Lanka, Bengal, Southeast Asia and China, and "everywhere their raison d'être was trade". A coastal town could combine a non-Muslim ruler, Muslim merchants, a mosque and Muslim legal institutions. So: does Islam require a Muslim state? No.

Gujarat and Konkan

  • Al-Masudi: about 10,000 Muslims at Saymur (Chaul), four Friday mosques; the local king appointed a Muslim leader to judge Muslims' affairs.
  • Ibn Hawqal (976): at Rashtrakuta ports "Muslim institutions of law are practiced openly".
  • Bayasira = Muslims born in India of Muslim parents.
  • Chalukya king Siddharaja (1094–1143) fostered coastal Muslim communities (later legend credits him with founding Bohra and Khoja groups).
  • By the 12th c., trade east of India was controlled by Indian Muslims; Gujarati merchants, Muslim and Hindu, were prominent. Hinterland cotton, indigo and saltpetre. Ibn Battuta: foreign merchants at Cambay built mansions and mosques.

Sri Lanka and Coromandel

  • Sri Lankan "Moors"; a commercial triangle of pepper (Malabar), cinnamon (Sri Lanka), pearls (Coromandel).
  • Ma'abar (Coromandel): Rashid al-Din called it "the key to India"; horse imports from Kish; the al-Tayyibi family from Fars governed three ports. Madurai Sultanate (1335–78).

Calicut: a Muslim commercial world under a Hindu ruler

  • The dominant Malabar port from the 11th c., ruled by the Zamorin (Samudri raja).
  • Mutual dependence: the Zamorin protected Muslim merchants because they were economically indispensable; merchants even supplied ships and military service.
  • Ma Huan (early 15th c.): the great chiefs are Muslims, 20–30 mosques. Abd al-Razzaq (1442): Muslims dominate the town, yet it is "situated on a hostile shore".
  • Coastal Muslims followed the Shafi'i school and tied themselves to other Shafi'i ports across the ocean, unlike the Hanafi inland "Tartars". Pearson: Shafi'i law was more relaxed about living under non-Muslim rulers.
  • Only Cannanore and Honavar had Muslim rulers in the 15th c.
Who were the Mappilas? Malabar Muslims = Pardesi ("foreign") and Mappila (local converts). Gaspar Correia (16th c.): low-caste Malabaris who converted could "go freely where they wished", outside the caste rules enforced by the Nayars. Coromandel groups: Marakkayar and Kayalar (Shafi'i, traded with Southeast Asia), Rawther and Labbai (Hanafi).

14.3 Why did people convert? Must know

Eaton: no single cause Many overlapping factors: commerce, migration, marriage, political patronage, social mobility, Sufi networks, local adaptation. Conversion was gradual and socially negotiated; elites often moved first. Eaton also argues that temple desecration under Turkish sultans was political (aimed at an enemy king's state deity), not proof of a theology of iconoclasm. Nehemia Levtzion stresses assimilation over imposition; David Parkin prefers "acceptance of Islam", an additive process.
Timing and evidence
  • Pearson notes a burst of mosque-building along the Indian coast in the 12th c., probably a wave of conversions.
  • Michael Feener: quantitatively significant local conversion in Southeast Asia appears only from the late 13th c., among political and mercantile elites.
  • Conversion legends: a Cheraman (Chera) king converted after a dream (Mappila tradition).

Diaspora: when does it stop being "foreign"?

A merchant diaspora connected home port, family, partners, religious institutions and rulers. It could become economically powerful, politically influential, institutionally autonomous and culturally embedded (Gujarat, Malabar, Southeast Asia, China). Engseng Ho's question: a diaspora stops being simply "foreign" when later generations stay locally embedded while keeping transregional ties.

14.5 Southeast Asia and China Must know

Southeast Asians were not passive recipients They actively remade Islam within their own political worlds. Wade: Muslim communities in Southeast Asia by the 10th c.; significant local conversion later. Key sites: Samudera-Pasai (Aceh, Sumatra), Malacca, Java, Champa.

Early phase (10th–11th c.)

  • Envoys to the Song court from the Arab lands, Srivijaya, Champa and the Cholas had Muslim names, "surnamed" Li (= 'Ali) or Pu (= Abu). The Li names may point to Alid/Shi'a refugees (a 14th-c. source: Alids fleeing the Umayyads settled in Champa).
  • Kalah (Kra/Kedah area): "the rendezvous of the ships of Siraf and Oman" with ships of China; tin.
  • Barus (camphor): a glass seal reading "Allah Muhammad" (10th–11th c.).
  • 986–88: Muslims from Champa fled to Hainan and Guangzhou (Sanya community survives today).
  • The Chola raids (1017, 1025) disrupted these links: Li-named envoys vanish.

First Muslim rulers (13th c.)

  • Lamreh gravestone (1211, disputed); Marco Polo (1290s): Perlak is Muslim; Sultan Malik al-Salih of Samudera (d. 1297). The title al-Malik al-Zahir echoes the Mamluk sultan Baybars.
  • Why then? Probably the Chola decline and the end of the Tamil guild economy opened new avenues.
  • Ibn Battuta (1345–46): the Samudera sultan was Shafi'i, loved jurists and waged jihad on neighbours who paid jizya; graves of "martyr" sultans; Muslim rulers at Aru (1411) and Lambri (1412).

Malacca: Islam meets state formation

  • Path to a Muslim sultanate: strategic location + international commerce + Muslim merchants + political patronage.
  • Founder Parameswara kept an Indic title but used Muslim envoys. Iskandar Shah converted after marrying into the Pasai ruling family (Tomé Pires says at age 72).
  • Islamic rule may have been backed by the Ming base there; by the 1450s Sultan Muzaffar Shah minted coins. Malacca became a centre of commerce, learning and religion. Anthony Reid places it in Southeast Asia's "Age of Commerce".
  • About 1,000 Gujarati merchants lived in Malacca when the Portuguese arrived.

Quanzhou: Islam at the eastern end

  • Mosque reputedly built 1009/10; earliest gravestone 1173. More than half of foreign trade was in Muslim hands.
  • Pu Shougeng: maritime trade supervisor (1274), joined the Mongols (1276), ordered to build 200 ships (1281).
  • Read a tombstone as a map: a person's nisba (name of origin) shows the world a community imagined itself in: Isfahan, Shiraz, Tabriz, Bukhara, Qazvin, Yemen. Most point to Iran.
Migration, refugees and the "accidental" spread of Islam Conflict → flight → settlement → new networks. The 1357–66 "Isfahan rebellion" in Quanzhou (Persian commanders against Pu Shougeng's descendant) was followed by a purge of Muslims. Refugees fled by sea, and within a decade Muslim tombs appeared at Trowulan (the Majapahit court, from the 1370s, with Saka dates and Arabic texts), along with Demak, Gresik (1,000+ "Chinese" families) and Barus (tomb of 1370). Concept: forced migration creates new religious geographies, not only missionaries and merchants.
Zheng He and Ma Huan The Muslim eunuch admiral Zheng He (voyages 1405–35; hundreds of ships, up to 30,000 troops) served the Yongle emperor's need for legitimacy. The emperor's 1407 edict protected Muslims. Ming bases at Malacca and Samudera. Ma Huan (Yingyai shenglan) saw three groups in Java: western Muslim merchants, Chinese (many Muslim), and locals not yet converted. Malacca's king and people were Muslim.

14.6 Ponnāni and the ribāṭ: from networks to maritime conflict (Kooria) Must know

  • Microhistory: start with one locality and reconstruct the larger ocean through it.
  • Ponnāni was a port, military centre, naval headquarters, educational centre, commercial hub and political frontier: the Zamorin's second capital (his principal military capital after Calicut).
  • Actors: the Zamorin, Muslim merchants, the 'ulamā', the Makhdūms, the Kuññāli Marakkārs, the Portuguese, Mamluks and Ottomans.
  • 1498: Vasco da Gama reaches Calicut. The Portuguese were "not simply more merchants"; they sought to control commerce militarily, threatening both the economy and the religious institutions of Malabar Muslims.
  • The key shift: commercial competition became political conflict, and political conflict became religiously articulated.
What is a ribāṭ? Originally a frontier defence post; also a fortification, religious community, mosque, marketplace, hospice, settlement and school. Its meaning shifted across time and place. Kooria's proposal: an Indian Ocean ribāṭ was a coastal community organised around defence, religion and maritime life. Treat it as an analytical concept, not just a building.

Who made the Ponnāni ribāṭ? Not "Muslims vs Christians", but a coalition:

  • the Zamorin: sovereignty, revenue, trade;
  • the 'ulamā': framed the conflict as jihad and ribāṭ;
  • the Kuññāli Marakkārs: naval warfare for the coalition;
  • the Māppiḷas: trade, fishing, labour and finance.
Religion, economy, politics and military power came together; none explains the ribāṭ alone.
Conclusion: from "spread of Islam" to "making an Indian Ocean Islam"

Wade's arc: mobility → trade → settlement → networks → Islamisation. Kooria's arc: settlement → local institutions → maritime conflict → religious reinterpretation → political mobilisation. Together: Islam was made, remade and localised through maritime networks. Islam became an Indian Ocean religion because mobility created communities, institutions and networks that local societies and changing political economies repeatedly reshaped.

Deep history of Arabian Sea shipping

  • The oldest evidence of shipping anywhere: Magan ships trading to Oman c. 3000 BCE (probably reed boats).
  • The Periplus (1st c. CE): local boats were sewn or lashed. Strabo tells of an Indian sailor who showed Eudoxos the route to India.
  • al-Sirafi (916): Omanis went to "the islands" (Lakshadweep or Maldives) to build ships of coconut timber with coir rope and frond-matting sails.
  • By the 12th c. three segments: Arabs and Persians in the Arabian Sea; Indian Muslims, especially Gujaratis, across the Bay of Bengal; the South China Sea beyond.
  • Agius: when the Portuguese came, Indians were "masters of the Indian Ocean triangle trade" (India–Arabia–East Africa), as pilots and merchants.
Sewn-plank: engineering without nails
  • Planks joined by rope stitching, first documented by al-Sirafi (916) for Omani shipbuilders.
  • Belitung wreck (9th c.): the only excavated example; a 15.3 m keel, sharp V-shaped hull, zero nails or dowels; held by stitching (four stitches per hole) and internal frames. Its frames also disprove the claim that these ships had no frames before Europeans.
  • Often assumed frail, but the replica Jewel of Muscat (Oman → Singapore, 2010) showed no distortion when craned out: the hull acts as a monocoque (a single shell, like an egg).
  • Trade-off: stitching must be replaced every few years, and size is limited unless reinforced.
  • Other replicas: Sohar (Oman → China, 1980); INS Kaundinya (stitched ship, Porbandar → Oman, Jan 2026).
Rudder innovation Sternpost-hung rudders appear in Arabian Sea pictures by the 12th c., arguably before Europe's first known example (the Winchester Cathedral font, c. 1180, using iron hinges). Without iron hinges, shipwrights extended the sternpost with extra timber (the fashin), cheek timbers and rope lashings. The rudder worked in "clean water" below the hull, its loads did not strain the sewn hull, and it could be lifted for beaching.
Gujarati and Parsi shipwrights: "not just imitators"
  • c. 1500: the Portuguese rated Gujarati shipwrights the most skilled in the region; ships of 300–800 tons, larger than rivals. The Gujarat sultan's ship Meri, taken at Hormuz in 1510, was up to 800 tons.
  • Technique: rabbeted seams (vadhera) allowed bigger, longer-lived hulls than plain sewing.
  • 1736: the EIC recruited Parsi shipwrights from Surat, led by Lovji Nusserwanjee Wadia ("wadia" means shipwright in Gujarati), to run its Bombay yard.
  • They adopted British designs and improved on them: HMS Trincomalee (1817) is still afloat; HMS Cornwallis (1813) lasted 144 years. Grose: "incomparably the best ships in the world for duration".
  • Point: European companies sought out this expertise, which complicates any simple story of European technical superiority before steam.

Southeast Asian lashed-lug ships and the "vanishing jong"

  • Planks hewn in mirror-image pairs, joined with wooden dowels; lugs carved inside the planks are lashed to the frames. Fully developed by the 3rd–5th c. CE (Pontian boat, Malaya) and used locally into the 20th century.
  • Borobudur reliefs show double outriggers, tanja sails and quarter rudders.
  • 1512: the Portuguese met jong, Southeast Asian cargo ships larger than their own. Albuquerque's fleet fired for two days at a jong from Pasai; its four layers of planking were too thick for Portuguese cannon; the Portuguese poop deck barely reached the jong's waist. Jongs carried nutmeg, cloves, textiles, ceramics and metals between Java, Malacca, India and Arabia.
  • Late 16th c.: sustained Portuguese naval disruption made the large jong disappear, replaced by smaller vessels.
Why the jong matters European dominance did not begin with superior technology. It began with military coercion of trade routes, centuries before steam gave Europe a real technological edge.

15.3 The pivot: two mechanisms of European dominance Must know

16th century: COERCION

Portuguese naval power disrupts routes and attacks vessels. Technology is not yet decisive: the jong was structurally superior. Power comes from military force, not engineering.
→

19th century: COMPETITION

Steam, iron and the telegraph give Europe a genuine, growing edge. Displacement comes from economic and technical competition: lower costs, faster transit, centralised control. Structural economic advantage, not direct force.

15.5 Survivals and synthesis Must know

The dhow beneath the steamship The dhows and katias of the 20th-century "unorganised sector" are the same sewn-plank and lashed-lug vessels Burningham traces back to 916 CE and the 3rd century CE. Steam pushed the old technology to the economic margins but left it structurally and technically intact. (Hornell: on the west coast, baggalas and pattamars were so cheap that European "true ships" could not compete. Barendse, S20: small-scale dhow trade prospered into the 20th century.)
The argument to take away
  1. Deep time, plural technologies: no single "best" technology; traditions coexisted, cross-pollinated and sometimes rivalled Europe (Gujarati ships).
  2. Two mechanisms of dominance: 16th-c. coercion (the vanishing jong) vs 19th-c. competition (steam, iron, telegraph); different tools, same outcome.
  3. Survivals are not accidents: dhows are living engineering traditions.
  4. Technology as politics: from solving a beaching problem to centralising an empire in London.

  • A major Mughal port (the "commercial hinge of western India", c. 1540–1750) linking inland production to overseas trade.

17.3 Roy: colonialism and development, 1860–1920 Must know

Roy's argument Roy challenges a simple "drain and deindustrialisation" story and asks how empire actually changed India's economy. His starting point is market integration through institutional and technological change: railways, telegraphs, ports and legal uniformity linked markets. The colonial state was poor in revenue but strong in legislation, so its effects were mostly indirect: it created enabling conditions for private enterprise. Outcomes were uneven across regions and sectors.
The debate Roy enters
  • Hobson and Lenin: capitalism reached for the periphery to avoid falling profits at home.
  • Marxists (Paul Baran, Walter Rodney, André Gunder Frank): imperialism caused underdevelopment.
  • Indian orthodoxy (Bagchi, Irfan Habib, Aditya Mukherjee) is "fundamentally neo-Marxist": deindustrialisation, the drain, an exploitative class structure, and export crops "forced" on peasants.
  • Roy's reply: peasants responded to profit; measuring the drain is crude; and the real puzzle is that India forged ahead of the colonised tropics, with big factories, one of the largest rail and telegraph systems, and good banks, ports, universities and hospitals. "Far from deindustrializing, India industrialized." The so-called drain was "the price paid for the gains derived from market integration", mainly payments for services to trade, business, education and health.
A poor but legislating state
  • In the 1820s tax per head in the three presidencies was under one-tenth of England's and half or less of other colonies'. Land tax dominated.
  • A "night-watchman" state (Morris D. Morris) that legislated "to a degree unknown in Indian history".
  • It offered one umbrella of property and contract law, one official language, one leading currency, and uniform channels of knowledge.
  • A "failure" in direct development, but a "success" as a modern state creating the institutions that global capitalism needs.

Agriculture: uneven commercialisation

  • 1765 diwani; land gave over 80% of revenue. Cornwallis's Permanent Settlement (1793): zamindari rights to the old revenue elite; ryotwari (peasant owners) in the west, south and north.
  • Critics: ownership over user rights, absentee landlords, debt traps. Roy: low investment was mostly due to scarce water; canals worked only in the Ganges and Indus plains; less than 10% of land was irrigated; rain-fed yields up to four times lower.
  • At its peak only 10–15% of cropland grew non-food exports, in a few districts. Gains came only where land, clear rights, cheap water and railways coincided. "Uneven" is the key word.

Industrial growth under empire

  • Factory jobs: 100,000 (1860) → 2 million (1940), about 4% a year, concentrated in Bombay and Calcutta.
  • Bombay cotton mills: a Parsi merchant's mill of 1854; railways cut cotton transport from three months to one day; the 1860s cotton boom funded mills; 10 mills (1865) → 271 (1914), 260,000 workers. China was the main buyer of Bombay yarn until Japan took over.
  • Calcutta jute: George Acland's mill (1855); 5 mills (1869) → 64 (1913); a European enterprise for world packaging (capital from London), while cotton was Indian (personal capital).
  • Limits: regional concentration, reliance on imported machinery, persistent inequality.

Technology and knowledge transfer

  • Empire moved machinery, engineers, medicine and skills: the Botanic Garden, Fort William College, the Asiatic Society; Roxburgh, Prinsep, Piddington's "law of storms"; tea found in Assam by Robert Bruce (1839).
  • Learning happened in mills, railway workshops, arsenals, surveys and colleges. Europeans among mill supervisors: 100% → under 30% by 1925.

Empire mattered through law, language (English as lingua franca) and knowledge. It let Mauritian planters reach Indian workers and Bombay mill-owners reach Lancashire foremen; "India's precocious industrialization cannot be explained otherwise". The cost was an explosion of contract disputes, and a deep contradiction: markets need freedom, while empire means subjection (exposed by the Great Depression).

18.1 Furber: the age of calm and the rise of tea (1713–44) Supporting

  • After the Peace of Utrecht (1713), three decades of calm in the eastern seas; Anglo-Dutch wars became "unthinkable". A long background trend: ever-rising China trade (tea, porcelain, chinoiserie).

Plassey (1757): more than a battlefield

  • The nawab Siraj-ud-daula; news of Anglo-French war (1757); Admiral Watson took French Chandernagore; the Afghan Ahmad Shah Abdali threatened from the west.
  • The Company's Select Committee resolved to depose the nawab (April 1757). Bengal's non-Muslim elites (Hindu, Parsi, Sikh), especially the Seth bankers, saw the British as "a means whereby they could gain more power".
  • Conspirators chose Mir Jafar. Amin Chand (Omichund) demanded 5% of the treasure, so a fake treaty was drawn up with Watson's signature forged, with Clive's connivance.
  • Battle, 23 June 1757: Clive's ~3,000 men vs the nawab's ~50,000. Rain silenced the nawab's guns; Major Kilpatrick's initiative; Mir Jafar's troops held back; the nawab fled on a camel. "Less fighting... than at any other 'decisive battle'."
  • Rewards: Clive about £234,000; councillors £12,000 each; a subaltern £3,000.
Why Plassey mattered (lecture + Furber)
  • It involved military force and political factionalism and conspiracy.
  • Indian bankers and political elites mattered.
  • Company officials and private interests expected rewards.
  • It sped the move from commercial presence to territorial power.

Furber: after Plassey the Europeans "could not actively intervene in Indian politics and yet remain mere traders". Several European powers could not all be "country powers"; one had to stop the others. The Dutch learned this in 1759 ("Dear Forde, fight 'em immediately"). The French were defeated at Wandiwash (1760) and Pondicherry (1761); Treaty of Paris 1763.

1765: revenue transforms the Company

  • Mir Jafar was replaced by Mir Kasim, who resisted Company servants' abuse of trade passes. At Baksar (Buxar), 1764, Hector Munro defeated Mir Kasim and the nawab of Awadh: Bengal's independence ended.
  • 1765: Clive obtained from the Mughal emperor Shah Alam the diwani of Bengal, the right to collect its revenue: "the beginning of British rule in India".
  • Territorial revenue could fund both commerce and politics; the Company increasingly acted as a governing institution. But territory brought huge military and administrative costs.
  • Westminster stepped in: Lord North's Regulating Act (1773) (governor-general, Supreme Court at Calcutta); Warren Hastings (governor from 1772) acted like the ruler of a "country power"; Pitt's India Act (1784) placed the Company under state control.
The Company paradox (lecture) Political power (territory, revenue, army, administration) grew while commercial monopoly (eroded by private traders, country trade, agency houses) weakened. "An imperial government could become stronger as the old trading monopoly weakened." Furber: by the 1790s the EIC "as a government" went "from strength to strength", but "as a company" it was "in eclipse". The era ended symbolically at Seringapatam (1799), where Tipu Sultan fell to Wellesley's forces.

18.3 Furber: tea, India and China as one Asian system Must know

  • Tea imports to London: 2.5 m lb (1760) → 9 m (1769–70) → down during the American War → the Commutation Act (1784), Pitt's cut in tea duty to beat smuggling → 9 m (1784–85), 14 m (1785–86), 23 m lb by 1800.
  • The more bills of exchange on London that Canton supercargoes sold to country traders (who had earned silver selling Indian goods in China), the less Company silver they needed: negligible in the 1750s → 1 million taels (£333,000) by 1781–82 → £500,000 a year by 1800.
  • Bengal opium exports in country trade: Rs 125,000 (1774–75) → over Rs 1 million (1786–87).
  • Net China-trade profits: £234,000 (mid-1770s) → £418,000 (1783–84) → £800,000–900,000 by 1800.
  • So: opium and cotton moved east; tea and silk moved west; India supplied goods and became the base for country trade.
Agency houses and private fortunes
  • Agency houses (growing after 1763) managed Company servants' savings at 9%+ interest, linking British power on land and at sea.
  • Servants sent fortunes home through French, Dutch and Danish companies and "clandestine" voyages under foreign flags; 100–150 such voyages in 1783–93 were "British in all but name". Hastings himself bought Dutch bills.
  • Wars were profitable: in the war with Tipu (1789) Madras borrowed at 8–12% from Indians, agency magnates and even church wardens.
  • 70 British country ships called at Cochin in one season (1791–92); Penang was founded in 1786 as a free "emporium".
  • Furber: the gains of Company servants were "a catalyst", and individual enterprise was "destroying the world of mercantilism and monopoly, making way for the world of free trade".

18.4 Bowen: follow the silver (1760–1833) Must know

Bowen's question and method He reconstructs British silver flows to, around and from Asia to test the belief that bullion exports simply stopped after the conquest of Bengal. Method: rebuilding fragmented evidence from Company invoice data (customs ledgers were partly destroyed by fire). Finding: exports were at times very large, especially after 1785, and were used for war and debt relief as well as trade; private flows mattered; and after 1810 silver began to flow back to Britain.

The debate he resolves

  • Before 1760: K.N. Chaudhuri showed treasure exports rising to about £750,000 a year in the 1750s (65–85% of Company exports).
  • Irfan Habib: silver to India stopped after 1760 (removing an inflationary force). Rajat Datta: it continued and rose after 1785. Bowen: Datta is right, but his figures need correction (the Company counted Mocha, Benkulen and Balambangan as "India").

Phase 1 (1760–84): almost nothing to India

  • Under £2 m in total, mostly to Canton; only two shipments to India (Madras 1773, 1776).
  • Why? Directors: more British goods, treaty payments by Indian rulers, and above all private fortunes paid into Indian treasuries in return for bills of exchange on London.
  • After 1765, silver was sent from India to Canton to buy tea, until Bengal was "drained of silver"; then the "country supply" of the Canton treasury (Indian goods sold in China plus private deposits) took over.

Phase 2 (1785–1820): exports resume

Lecture figures: more silver went to India than to China.
  • Trade: the 1784 Commutation Act raised tea demand → silver to China; silver to Bombay to buy raw cotton for Canton; lumps of fine silver (sycee) for Chinese duties.
  • War: by 1793 Calcutta's treasury had no silver; Wellesley's wars (1798–1805) forced big shipments (over £1 m to Bengal in 1802 and 1804). China-bound silver was seized en route: £70,000 at Madras in 1790 for the war against Tipu; £202,281 at Madras in 1805; 505,298 dollars at Penang in 1806.
  • Debt: after the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–18), bulk shipments in 1816–20 to reduce Indian debt (sinking fund).
  • Silver was only 18.6% of Company export value in 1760–1829 (peak 47.9% in 1785–89), as directors pushed British manufactures.
Private flows and circulation inside Asia
  • Private silver: ship commanders, crews, passengers, interlopers; diamond merchants; General Claude Martin of Lucknow paid for indigo in silver (70,000 dollars in 1795–96); Parsi shipbuilder Pestonji Bomanji. Private exports to India 1791–1806: about £2.67 m.
  • 1802–11: of India's silver imports, Britain supplied only 43.8%, other European countries 16% and America 40.2%.
  • Silver circulates within Asia: almost £2 m moved from Canton to India in 1792–1809; later the opium and cotton trades created a "golden current" from Canton to India (about £681,577 a year in 1810/11–1827/28).
After 1800: the direction reverses
  • After the Company lost its India trade monopoly (1813) and its textile imports fell, it paid its "home charges" partly with bullion shipped from Asia to London.
  • About 1.5 million dollars from China in 1811; an "extraordinary remittance" of £2 m ordered in 1823; banker Nathan Rothschild bought the bullion.
  • Net bullion received in London 1811–33: about £8.1 m. Britain probably became a net importer of silver from India: "a far cry from the one-way traffic in bullion for goods".
  • Private flows remain hard to measure. The shift reflected mounting Company financial pressure.
Bowen: silver moved to, around and from Asia; the "bullion for goods" model breaks down.

Silver, Tea and Opium in the Imperial Expansion II: Opium, Empire and the Global Political Economy

Reading: Carl A. Trocki, Opium, Empire and the Global Political Economy: A Study of the Asian Opium Trade, 1750–1950 (1999), ch. 5, "The Most Gentlemanlike Speculation", pp. 93–108 (R15; not in the folder, so this chapter follows the Lecture 19 PPT, which also cites Trocki's "Drugs, Taxes, and Chinese Capitalism in Southeast Asia" (2000) and Klimburg's critique (2001))
Where this fits / why it matters Session 18 showed tea pulling silver into China and opium and cotton moving east. This session puts one commodity, opium, at the centre and asks what it reveals about empire. The analytical move: from "drug history" to political economy. "The history of opium is also a history of empire, capitalism and Asian interconnection." It links back to S13 (commodities as actors), S18 (silver) and forward to S20 (port cities like Singapore and Hong Kong).

19.1 Why study opium? Must know

Commodity

Opium was not a marginal product. It linked production, transport, taxation, finance and consumption across Asia.

Empire

Colonial states regulated production and distribution and used opium revenue to strengthen their fiscal and administrative power.

Global economy

India, Southeast Asia and China formed one interconnected commercial system, not isolated national economies.
Trocki's central thesis "Without opium there would have been no empire." This is a deliberately provocative formulation, best treated as a discussion proposition.
  • Opium generated revenue, profits and commercial leverage.
  • Imperial power depended on controlling commodity circuits, not only on conquering territory.
  • The opium system tied colonial India to Southeast Asian ports and the China trade.
  • So Trocki puts opium at the centre of Asian imperial political economy.

Britain's China problem and the "solution"

Problem

Britain wanted Chinese goods, especially tea (and silk, porcelain), but China had little demand for British manufactures, so silver drained out.

Solution

Opium was a commodity for which there was strong demand in China.

Consequence

Silver and purchasing power were redirected through the opium trade: British India → opium → China → silver → tea → Britain.

19.3 India: the colonial production zone Must know

  • The EIC turned opium into a regulated fiscal commodity.
  • Production was concentrated in Bengal and Bihar under Company control.
  • Peasant cultivation was organised through contracts and cash advances.
  • Processing happened in Company factories (e.g. Patna), where quality and packing were standardised.
  • Finished opium went to auctions (Calcutta) and export networks.
  • Paradox: colonial "free trade" coexisted with tightly controlled production and monopoly. Ask: who controlled the chain, and at which stage?
The opium factory as an imperial machine

Poppy cultivation → collection and contracting → factory processing → auction and export.

  • State power: contracts, inspection and processing turned a crop into a fiscal commodity, made legible to the colonial state.
  • Labour: large numbers of workers in repetitive processing and handling.
  • Global reach: the product left India as part of an interregional maritime network.

The commodity chain: cultivation (countryside) → processing (Company factories) → finance (Calcutta merchants) → shipping (Indian Ocean ports) → distribution (Southeast Asian and Chinese markets). "Value was created across a chain of unequal relationships." Follow the commodity, not the nation.

The maritime journey: opium clippers Calcutta/Hooghly → Bay of Bengal → Strait of Malacca → Singapore → South China Sea → Lintin (the receiving depot near Canton) → Canton/Pearl River (and Macao). Fast, sophisticated opium clippers such as Red Rover (Jardine Matheson), Sylph (1831), Falcon, Water Witch (built at Kidderpore, Calcutta, 1831) carried the trade; the Parsi Cowasjee family were shipowners. Images in the lecture: the opium warehouse and examining hall at the Patna factory.

19.4 Southeast Asia: the crucial middle Must know

  • Trocki's intervention is especially important for Singapore, a major post between India and China.
  • Revenue: colonial governments licensed private monopolies to sell opium.
  • Networks: Chinese commercial organisations handled distribution.
What was an "opium farm"? A fiscal institution, not an agricultural farm. The colonial state sold a licence (a legal monopoly to sell opium) to a farmer/licensee, who sold to consumers. Revenue flowed upward to the state; commercial risk and local market knowledge stayed decentralised with the farmer.
Chinese commercial networks: the kongsi

Opium farms were often run by Chinese kongsi (shareholding partnerships).

  • Colonial authority: granted the monopoly and collected predictable revenue.
  • Chinese enterprise: kongsi and other groups organised labour, finance and distribution.
  • Embedded economy: opium became intertwined with wider urban commercial life.

Trocki: "Opium was the trap in which the kongsi economies were captured by colonial capitalism."

19.5 China: consumption, finance and war Must know

Not a simple "addiction = demand" story
  • Demand developed within urban, commercial and social settings.
  • Consumption varied by class, occupation and locality.
  • Opium could be embedded in sociability and business life (19th-c. opium culture).
  • The real question: how did supply, demand, state power and social change interact?
  • Chinese consumers were not only passive victims: analyse agency and social context alongside coercive imperial supply.
  • Note the imagination of China: Thomas Allom's image of an opium den in Canton.
The commodity circuit was also a financial circuit

India → (opium) → sea ports → China; China → (silver/payment) → back; tea and goods → Britain.

  • Revenue: opium created taxable income and commercial profits.
  • Liquidity: payments circulated through merchants, ports and colonial institutions.
  • Empire: fiscal resources strengthened colonial states.

Link to S18: this is Bowen's "golden current" of silver from Canton back to India.

Britain

British merchants sought protection for their interests; military force secured access and favourable terms.

"Was this a war about drugs, or about who had the right to regulate trade?"

  • Treaty of Nanking (Nanjing), 1842: five treaty ports opened; Hong Kong ceded to Britain; an indemnity; limits on Chinese tariffs; new extraterritoriality and consular jurisdiction.
  • The British government claimed opium was "irrelevant" to the war, so the opium question was not solved.
  • Second Opium War (1857–60) → Treaty of Tianjin: opium trade legalised in China.
  • Afterwards European middlemen were squeezed out and Asian traders grew stronger; after 1860 Europeans strengthened their hold on the Calcutta and Bombay auction markets.
Trocki's lens Military victory translated commercial power into imperial privilege.

The central paradox: "free trade" through monopoly. Empire could liberalise trade externally (open markets, commercial access, freedom of trade as rhetoric) while controlling markets internally (monopoly contracts, regulated production, state-backed fiscal extraction). "The question is not whether capitalism was 'free' or 'unfree', but who had the power to define the rules."

Interconnected Port Cities in Colonial Asia: From the Gulf and Red Sea to Bombay, Madras and Calcutta

Schottenhammer (ed.), Early Global Interconnectivity across the Indian Ocean World, vol. I (2019), ch. 14, pp. 343–374 (RB5) · Meera Kosambi & John E. Brush, "Three Colonial Port Cities in India", Geographical Review 78:1 (1988), pp. 32–47 (OT10) · Lecture 20 PPT
Where this fits / why it matters Port cities are where every endsem theme meets: merchants (S12), goods (S13), faith (S14), ships (S15), companies (S17), silver and opium (S18–19). Why port cities matter (lecture): they linked sea routes, inland markets and imperial states; they concentrated merchants, migrants, soldiers and labourers; they connected regional exchange to global commerce; and they reveal both Asian agency and colonial power. 20.1 Barendse: "the last stand of Asian autonomies" Must know

Barendse's central claim The "long eighteenth century" (c. 1720–1840) in the Arabian seas was what Anthony Reid calls the last stand of Asian autonomies: despite European colonialism, Asian trade remained equal or expanded. European power mattered but did not erase Asian commercial agency. The major rupture came later than colonial narratives suggest: the 1860s–70s.
Long 18th century Strong continuity in Asian shipping, trade and finance
Early 19th century Existing networks remain active across the Gulf, Red Sea and India
Mid-19th century Cotton boom (US Civil War), steam shipping and Suez accelerate change
Late 19th century British firms gain stronger structural advantage

Why the break came late

  • The great transformation: the 1860s cotton boom (financed by the same Bania merchants who had traded to Persia, the Red Sea and Africa; profits went into Bombay mills); steam (P&O; Arab and Indian shipowners could not compete; Raj contracts went to British firms); and Suez (1869), after which the Red Sea dominated and the Gulf became "a sleepy, placid backwater", a mare britannica.
  • Irfan Habib warns against studying a colonial economy "without perceiving colonialism"; but British residents in the Gulf overrated their own influence, so the European role is easily overvalued.
  • The 18th c. is now seen as an age of prosperity in the Middle East and India, not a "dark age" (Iran is the exception: the 1722 Safavid collapse and Nadir Shah's wars). In Bengal and Java, though, booming shipping came with falling living standards.
  • The critical events in the region were not European: the Safavid fall (1722), Oman's civil war (1720s–30s), Qajar rule, Wahhabi raids on Muscat (1820s, pushing the imams to Zanzibar), and Mehmed Ali's Egypt in the Hijaz and Yemen.
  • Rulers like Oman's imams and the Qajars played Europeans off against each other. Indian traders under the Union Jack were "easy hostages"; the British consuls' daily work was to intercede for them.

What port cities were like

  • Small: Basra 40–60,000 (the Gulf's largest); Muscat ~30,000; Jeddah ~40,000; Mocha (al-Mukka) ≤10,000; Kuwait 5–6,000; Aden only ~3,000 when Britain took it (1839), "a bunkering station in the middle of nowhere". Inland cities were far bigger (Isfahan ~200,000).
  • Reasons: food supply (Muscat's rice came from Kanara, later from British-controlled Bengal, so Oman had to stay friendly with Britain), epidemics, and heat (Bushire "the most detestable city I visited in all of Asia").
  • Some were shifting fairs tied to inland roads (Hudayda for the coffee fairs, Bandar Lingeh), but most had strong civic identity and ancient roots (Suhar, Aden, Bahrain). Hormuz (1620s) was not "blown away"; its people moved to Bandar Abbas.
  • Kuwait rose suddenly in the 1750s with a new caravan route to Aleppo and a sulphur mine (ballast).
Port cities as commercial nodes (lecture)
  • Ports mattered for distribution, information and credit as much as for size.
  • They linked hinterlands to oceanic markets and coordinated storage, pricing and re-export ("staple markets": one place sets prices for others; Gulf prices differed by only 4–5%).
  • Their importance lay in connections, not population: a node, not just a city.
  • Central rulers (Safavid, Ottoman) charged higher customs but protected roads; small sheikhdoms charged less. Merchants used strikes and bribed sheikhs against greedy officials.
  • Legal pluralism worked best in city-states where the ruler was a merchant (the al-Sabah of Kuwait).

Who made interconnection possible? People in motion

  • Indian merchants: Banias (most Gulf Banias were from Sind, not Gujarat), Bohras, Khatris, Marwaris, Parsis. They were the richest group in Mocha; temples were allowed in Iran but only private worship in Yemen.
  • Arab merchants, shipowners and coastal rulers (desert warlords turned shipowners); seafaring tribes like the al-Huwaila.
  • Iranian, Armenian, Jewish, Greek and other intermediaries (Greek firms had agents in both Bahrain and Leipzig).
  • Sailors, brokers, migrants, labourers, translators; Aden in 1830 was half Jewish; Mocha had Somali and Ethiopian merchants and slaves.
Infrastructure of connection: finance made trade work
  • Bills of exchange and interlocking banking lowered transaction costs: by the 1780s Pune firms drew bills directly on Istanbul (in the 16th c. this needed several stages: Pune → Cambay → Basra → Aleppo). By the 1730s the EIC ran its Mocha trade entirely on bills endorsable in Surat (Islamic bills and Indian hundis).
  • Insurance and credit made trade flexible: insuring cargo was rare in the 16th c. but common by the mid-18th.
  • Warehousing, road protection and harbour access shaped success.
  • Legal pluralism let merchants operate across communities.
What drove the Asian boom?
  • More farmland and rural demand (Egypt, Iraq, Iran's recovery); cheaper transport; a longer sailing season; lower interest rates and insurance.
  • China's economic expansion (demand for Gujarat cotton, Malabar pepper, Makassar trepang and opium; China was ~20% of Bombay's customs revenue by 1800).
  • New American demand, above all for slaves (Cuba, Brazil, USA): East African slave exports rose from ~3,000 a year to 20–30,000, emptying the hinterland; Zanzibar became a clove and nutmeg island using slave labour.
  • Mostly, middle-class demand: Red Sea trade quadrupled from the 17th to the 18th c. (Indian textiles, tobacco).
"Interconnection was not simply cosmopolitan exchange"

Lecture synthesis: "It also depended on unequal labour systems, coercion, and the violence of slavery." African slaves were preferred in the Gulf as supposedly "more resistant" to disease; seasonal workers rotated between highlands and ports.

Capital stayed in Asian hands: Chinese kongsi and the rise of Singapore ("as much a Chinese commercial enclave as a British one"); Zanzibar trade run by Bania families from Kutch Mandvi, Bhavnagar and Porbandar; Portuguese East Africa run by Kathiawar Banias and Goan firms.

Country trade and the "Anglo-Bania alliance"

  • Until the 1880s it makes sense to speak of an Anglo-Bania alliance and an "Anglo-Indian empire" in the Arabian seas.
  • By 1780 most Indian Ocean shipping was British-operated, but often financed and owned by Indian, Armenian and Arab consortia, insured by Indian underwriters, with Arab and Iranian freight (S18 country trade from the other side).
  • Later, Company officials' savings in agency houses (e.g. Forbes & Co., ~30 ships from Bombay) made British trade self-financing, remitted to London by bills: "the infamous 'drain'".
  • Why did British country trade rise? Lower customs, Royal Navy protection (cheaper credit), lower profit rates. The British campaign against the Qawasim ("pirates" of the Trucial Coast) mainly protected Bombay's Bania and Parsi ships.
  • The bulk of Gulf and Red Sea trade stayed local (about one long-distance ship to 20–30 short-haul ones); the dhow trade prospered into the 20th c.
Barendse's conclusion: beware both long continuities and sharp breaks K.N. Chaudhuri's Asia before Europe stops the Indian Ocean's history at 1757; that is wrong. Barendse agrees with Sugata Bose that in some ways the ocean became more of a unity under colonialism: mass Indian emigration (Mauritius as "little India", East Africa, Southeast Asia), Indian-manned garrisons and navy, and Gulf and East African agencies run from India until the 1880s. "The Indian Ocean became far more 'Indian'… than it ever was before." In the Gulf, the real "high tide of empire" after the 1870s was Ottoman, not British.

20.2 Kosambi & Brush: the colonial port-city model Must know

Their argument Madras, Bombay and Calcutta share a distinctive urban morphology (physical form) from their colonial past, not found in other Indian cities. 17th-c. factories inside existing Indian cities had little effect on them; but these three began as fortified factories at undeveloped village sites, fully controlled by the EIC, and grew into hybrid towns where Indian merchants, drawn by trade under English protection, lived segregated from Europeans. They became the bases for expansion inland (other colonial forms: cantonments, civil lines, hill stations).
Fort by the water, an open esplanade, segregated sectors, a CBD, and peripheral industry and military zones.

Three phases of colonial urban development

  1. Fortified factory town with "white" and "black" towns. The Indian town was walled (Madras) or ditched (Calcutta); in Bombay one wall enclosed both. The European town always lay south of the factory, perhaps for escape to ships.
  2. Expansion (mid/late 18th c.): stronger forts (ramparts, bastions, moats); esplanades cleared for gunfire ("defensive emptiness around power"); Europeans moved to suburbs 2–5 miles out (by horse, carriage and palanquin), copying suburbanisation in Britain. Fast in Madras and Calcutta, delayed in Bombay.
  3. CBD and industrial fringe (19th–early 20th c.): a Western-style central business district on the earliest English site; crowded Indian towns; an intermediate zone (Parsis, Asian immigrants, Eurasians); grand public buildings and railway stations on old esplanade land; coal-powered mills and slums on the fringe; troops moved to cantonments.

21.1 Chapter 10: the colonial state after 1857 Must know

Company Raj → Crown Raj (1858)

  • The Governor-General became the Viceroy.
  • A Secretary of State for India in London became the central authority.
  • The British Indian Army was reorganised; the colonial bureaucracy strengthened.
  • New mechanisms for cultivating Indian collaborators.
What stayed the same The fundamental imperial objective: (1) economic: India as a resource within the international capitalist economy; (2) strategic: India as a military resource for imperial defence and expansion. "1858 is institutional reorganisation, not an end to colonial exploitation."

The British Indian Army: an imperial institution

  1. Imperial: defended and expanded the empire abroad: Sudan, China, South Africa, Egypt, Afghanistan, Burma, Tibet, the Persian Gulf.
  2. Economic: shaped regional investment and recruitment (e.g. Punjab's irrigation canals).
  3. Domestic: maintained colonial order inside India.
"Martial races" and governance Recruitment favoured Sikhs, Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims and Pathans; Bengalis and Tamils were labelled "less martial"; regiments were deliberately mixed so no community dominated. Chain: military recruitment → state patronage → regional investment → uneven development. Discussion: how can a military classification become an economic policy?

India in the colonial world economy

India exported raw materials and farm goods (cotton, jute, tea, coffee, wheat, oilseeds) to Britain's industrial manufacturing, and manufactured goods came back as imports. Central question: was integration into the world economy the same as economic development? (Compare Roy in S17, who stresses gains from integration.)

The "drain of wealth"

Transfers included home charges, pensions, military spending, railway interest, debt interest, private remittances, merchant profits, shipping, banking and insurance.

  • Nationalist view (Dadabhai Naoroji, R.C. Dutt): these transfers were investible surplus lost to India.
  • British counter-view: some payments were legitimate returns on capital or services.
  • Reorganise the question: ask through what institutions and mechanisms surplus was transferred, not just whether wealth was "stolen".

Agriculture, markets and vulnerability

Cash crops (cotton, jute, indigo) → export markets → merchant credit → indebtedness → exposure to global price swings. The paradox: integration produced both boom and vulnerability: the cotton boom during the American Civil War then a sharp downturn; the jute boom in eastern India (1906–13); severe agrarian distress in the late 19th c. Lesson: integration did not necessarily mean economic security.

Railways: modernisation or colonial infrastructure?

They moved troops, distributed British goods, carried raw materials from hinterland to port, and paid guaranteed returns to British capital, even when unprofitable. The contradiction: technologically modern infrastructure could still serve an unequal economic structure. Ask: who designed it, who financed it, whose interests did it serve?

Governing through collaboration
  • The Raj relied on princes, landlords, local elites and nominated representatives.
  • Limited representation: 1861 Indian Councils Act; 1882 Ripon's local-government reforms; 1892 Indian Councils Act; 1909 Morley–Minto reforms.
  • Pre-colonial sovereignty was layered and shared; colonial rule imposed unitary sovereignty.
  • Colonial India held mostly subjects, not citizens. Core contradiction: participation expanded without full political sovereignty.
Census, community and colonial knowledge Caste, religion and community were classified → political patronage → separate representation → separate electorates (1909). The Morley–Minto reforms institutionalised separate electorates for Muslims and some other groups. Food for thought: did the colonial state merely record pre-existing identities, or also reorganise and politically institutionalise them?

22.2 Aspengren's article: the note of caution Must know

Question and answer

Author: Henrik Chetan Aspengren, Uppsala University (2014). Subjects: Virendranath Chattopadhyaya and Lala Har Dayal in Sweden.

Question: what does recent global scholarship on exile leave out?
Answer: the long periods of silence and disconnection.

"By omitting them, one risks romanticising exile."

Why historians over-connect
  • There is a temptation to celebrate exile as creativity and connectivity.
  • Every new place seems to supply new contacts.
  • Yet exile was often restricting, painful and insecure (Edward Said).
  • Historians "connect the dots" with hindsight.
  • Better terms: "silence" or "disconnection", rather than only "connectivity".

Two men, one route

Virendranath ChattopadhyayaHar Dayal
OriginHyderabad; father principal of Nizam CollegeDelhi; University of the Punjab, brilliant results
In BritainFailed the ICS exam; law at Middle Temple; quiet at firstGave up an Oxford scholarship (1907); joined India House
AfterwardsParis, then BerlinParis, Algiers, Martinique, San Francisco (helped found Ghadar), then Berlin (1914)

Both men were radicalised in England, not in India.

From London to Berlin

  • Both were indirectly financed by the German Foreign Ministry.
  • Plans: arms from the US, agitation in Punjab, passage through Afghanistan, an office in Constantinople.
  • Failures: British intelligence, German–Indian disconnect, infighting, and an India less ready than hoped.
  • Outcome: the spectacular ventures failed; revolution was not imminent.

Why Sweden?

  • Berlin's interest cooled as Germany's war fortunes turned.
  • Both were barred from India and at risk anywhere in the empire. Chattopadhyaya: "There is no country except Sweden where I can safely reside."
  • Sweden was neutral, with links via German contacts.
  • The planned Stockholm Peace Conference (1917) (socialists of neutral, Allied and Central powers were to negotiate peace) drew Chattopadhyaya.

Chattopadhyaya in Stockholm: lost platform

  • Arrived 12 May 1917; opened a bureau at Artillerigatan 28 B.
  • The peace conference never opened, so he lost his platform.
  • His contacts were conservative, pro-German, anti-socialist editors.
  • He protested that for Western social democrats "humanity" meant Europe only.
  • "No conference, no audience, no allies."

Pressure to fall silent

  • German funding became uncertain after the war; contacts urged him to earn money; police advised less politics.
  • He turned to translation, articles and an import agency; the bureau closed (propaganda "not desirable at present").
  • Silence imposed by money, police and legal insecurity.
Har Dayal: arrival, two letters, a changed mind
  • Stuck a year in Vienna; Swedish visa October 1918; stayed in a sanatorium. Swedish authorities questioned his links to Chattopadhyaya.
  • December 1918: he wrote that he would take his own life if forced to leave Sweden; the next day he wrote that revolutionary propaganda against England was "foolish and useless". He then disowned Chattopadhyaya and the Indian National Committee. (Handle with care: the record shows distress and strategy together.)
  • After 1920 he criticised German foreign policy and called for autonomy within the British Empire; the New York Times noted the "remarkable effect" of displacement.
  • He turned to Swedish adult education (temperance and study circles, Uppsala), met Agda Erikson, later wrote Hints for Self-Culture, and never rejoined the movement.
A pattern, not an exception; and Aspengren's conclusion

Algiers, 1910: after quarrelling with Krishnavarma, Har Dayal wrote of illness and strain, found "relief in crying" and took up the mandolin on medical advice. He had withdrawn dramatically before. Such episodes "existed, so they matter": they were a decisive part of the reality of exile.

  • Connectivity is the historian's retrospective construction.
  • In the moment, events looked bleak and chaotic.
  • Silence may also have meant introspection that reshaped later thought.
  • Aim: a theoretically informed yet humane approach to displaced people: "moments of anxiety and despair", not only creative ones.

22.3 Impact, deliberation and memory Must know

What it meant for the movement at home

  • Repression: Defence of India Act (1915), conspiracy trials, the Rowlatt Report (1918) and Rowlatt Acts (1919).
  • The Rowlatt Acts triggered Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha (link to S21).
  • Symbols: Dhingra, Kartar Singh Sarabha, later Bhagat Singh.
  • Limits: the plots failed; Gandhi's mass politics set the mainstream.
  • Takeaway: revolutionaries shaped the climate more than the outcome.

Separate events or one phenomenon?

Separate

Different triggers: London students, Pacific-coast racism, Germany's war aims, Singapore's sepoys; rival ideologies and personal feuds.
vs

Connected

The same people in many places; shared journals, routes and patrons; British officials treated them as one conspiracy.

Third view: connected but fragile, and perhaps connected most of all by the empire's surveillance.

Three things to remember (lecture)
  1. Concepts shape what historians see ("connectivity" can hide silence).
  2. Sources are never neutral: even silence reaches us through surveillance archives (police, intelligence, immigration files).
  3. Writing history is an ethical act (Har Dayal's letters of distress).

S.5 Short-answer plans (10 marks each, about 250–350 words)

1. Why does Chakravarti place South Asia at the centre of Indian Ocean history? Thesis: geography and evidence, not Indocentrism. Points: enclosed ocean; no single-season voyage, so the coasts became stopovers and transshipment points; two seaboards plus deltas; Geniza and Chola evidence. But: he warns against replacing Eurocentrism with Indocentrism. Conclude: "a hinge, not an edge".
2. What do the Cairo Geniza letters reveal about Indian Ocean trade? Trust and information ("one who is present can see…"); goods (pepper, iron, cinnamon, cloth) and prices; risk (shipwreck at Bab-el-Mandeb); cross-cultural life (Ben Yiju's marriage, Tinbu's friendship). But: chance survival, a Jewish merchant viewpoint.
3. How did textiles connect the Indian Ocean world? (Ray) Deep cotton history; only some cloth travelled; Berenike Z-spun evidence, Fustat, Newberry (Gujarat); guilds and charters; Java gift rituals; cloth as writing material. But: values differed by place; avoid generalising.
4. "Islam does not travel alone." Discuss. (Wade/lecture) Bundle of people, trade, law, language; carriers (merchants, Sufis, refugees); khutba networks; Calicut under the Zamorin; conversion of Malacca's ruler by marriage. But: Sufi role before 1500 unproven. Conclude: Islam was "made and localised".
5. Compare the two mechanisms of European maritime dominance. 16th-c. coercion (jong destroyed by disruption, not inferiority) vs 19th-c. competition (steam, iron, telegraph, Suez). Evidence: Burningham, Bhattacharya. But: dhows survived. Conclude: technology is politics.
6. How did Indian merchants shape European expansion in Surat? (Nadri) Credit and supply; Virji Vora's monopsony; broker dynasties (Parekhs, Kishandas, Parsis); 18th-c. change (split roles, marfatia, protection). But: companies used violence and later gained leverage. Conclude: interdependence then hierarchy.
7. Why did silver keep flowing to Asia after 1760? (Bowen) Tea after 1784; cotton for Canton; war (Wellesley, Tipu); debt relief; private flows; then reverse flows after 1810. Habib vs Datta. Conclude: silver became a flexible imperial resource.
8. What was an opium farm, and why does it matter to Trocki? Licence/monopoly; revenue upward, risk downward; Chinese kongsi; Singapore. Shows empire as fiscal circuits; "free trade through monopoly". But: Klimburg's critique.
9. Describe the morphology of colonial port cities. (Kosambi & Brush) Fort, esplanade, white/black towns, CBD, intermediate zone, industrial and military fringes; three phases; Madras, Bombay, Calcutta differences. But: built on Asian commerce (Barendse).
10. Why does Aspengren say focusing on connectivity can romanticise exile? Retrospective construction; Chattopadhyaya's lost platform; Har Dayal's letters and turn to adult education; Said; surveillance sources. But: exiles shaped the climate (Rowlatt). Conclude: history as an ethical act.

S.6 Essay plans (15 marks, about 600–900 words)

Essay 1. "The Indian Ocean was connected by human mobility, not geography alone." Discuss with reference to merchants, mariners and religious communities. Intro: monsoon as condition, people as agents. Body: mariners (Socotra visitors, sewn ships, al-Sirafi); merchants (Geniza, ainnurruvar, Qusman families); religious communities (Chudamani vihara, Somnath mosque, khutba and Kazaruni networks); rulers as patrons (Motupalli). But: geography still set the rhythm; connection could be unequal (slaves, coercion). Conclude: geography made connection possible; people made it real, continuing from 500 to 1950 in changing forms.
Essay 2. "European expansion in South Asia was negotiated before it was institutionalised." Discuss. Intro: process not event. Negotiated: Surat interdependence, Mughal farmans, Virji Vora, brokers (Nadri); Plassey as conspiracy with Indian bankers (Furber). Institutionalised: diwani, Regulating Act, law and standardisation (Roy), forts and esplanades (Kosambi & Brush), Crown Raj (S21). But: violence existed early (Middleton 1612), and Asian autonomy lasted in the Gulf (Barendse). Conclude with the lecture's line "trade, law, technology and local agency all belong in the same story".
Essay 3. How did commodities (silver, tea, opium) make British power in Asia? Tea demand and the Commutation Act; country trade and bills on London (Furber); silver for trade, war and debt, then reverse flows (Bowen); the opium circuit and opium farms, the Opium Wars, "free trade through monopoly" (Trocki); port cities as nodes (S20). But: territorial revenue and the army also mattered; Klimburg's critique. Conclude: empire as commodity circuits plus territory.
Essay 4. Was colonial rule in India "modernisation, exploitation, or both"? Roy: market integration, industrialisation, artisan reinvention, knowledge transfer. Bose & Jalal: drain, railways guaranteeing British returns, vulnerability of export agriculture, famine as entitlement failure, unitary sovereignty without citizenship. Bhattacharya: peripheralisation through steam. Conclude: both, unevenly; ask whose interests institutions served.
