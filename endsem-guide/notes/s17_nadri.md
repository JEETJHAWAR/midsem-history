# S17 Ghulam A. Nadri, "The English and Dutch East India Companies and Indian merchants in Surat in the 17th & 18th centuries: Interdependence, competition and contestation" — ch 5 in Clulow & Mostert eds, The Dutch and English East India Companies: Diplomacy, Trade and Violence in Early Modern Asia (AUP 2018) pp125–149 [R13]
- Thesis: EIC & VOC depended heavily on Indian merchants (bankers, brokers, suppliers, buyers); relationship = INTERDEPENDENCE & COMPLEMENTARITY, also competitive & contested. Older view: European domination/Indian subordination; since 1970s partnership & reciprocity (Kling & Pearson "Age of Partnership"; Ashin Das Gupta; Om Prakash). New: long trajectory incl 18th c; genealogy of brokers. "Indian merchants" = Gujarati Bania, Muslim, Parsi; "Asian merchants" = Armenian, Jewish, Turkish etc.
- Unlike Portuguese (cartaz system: Asian ships must buy Portuguese licence; violent reprisal) English & Dutch had mercantile tradition, pragmatic; got permission for factories. Exports: indigo, cotton & silk textiles. EIC came 1600s, VOC 1610s. EIC: precious metals for textiles, indigo, pepper. VOC intra-Asian: imported fine spices (cloves, nutmeg, mace), cinnamon, Japanese gold/silver/copper, Java sugar, ivory, sappanwood; surpluses remitted to other Indian establishments/Batavia. VOC Surat exports 340,000–1.08m guilders/yr (1672–1792); imports 390,000–1.16m (1711–93).
- Subordinate factories: Ahmadabad, Baroda, Broach, Cambay, Agra. Mughal support: Thomas Roe's embassy; Jahangir's farman (remission of customs, exemption from inland transit duties); Shahjahan's 1630s farmans to Dutch. Companies used naval violence sometimes (first petition & negotiation). Mughal officials traded to Red Sea/Gulf → competitors: 1619 Surat governor ordered EIC brokers not to buy for Mocha & Aceh; 1620s–30s indigo monopoly attempts. Broker Tapidas 1622 promised to get Baroda permission or secretly supply. Maritime violence: 1612 Henry Middleton plundered ~15 Surat vessels; VOC & EIC captured Red Sea ships 1620s; late 17th c European pirates in Gulf of Aden → companies blamed, forced to give convoy protection; 2nd half 18th c EIC tried to monopolise freight to West Asia.
- Brokerage changed from commercial intermediation (commission) to privileged office with social & political significance (Das Gupta: "enormous prestige"); families held it for generations.
- VIRJI VORA: richest merchant of Surat to early 1660s; not formal broker but principal buyer of EIC/VOC imports — MONOPSONY; lent money (VOC 36,000 mahmudis; EIC letters of credit 20,000 Rs 1628, 25,000 1630; loan 30,000 Rs 1634); 1629 EIC bought 20,000 maunds pepper; engrossed Deccan pepper; 1625 haggle 16 vs 16¼ mahmudis/maund + coral; 1634 "sole monopolist of all European commodities"; forced companies to give Surat merchants freight to Mocha, Jeddah, Gombroon, Basra (Hart diverted to Persia 1628). Edward Knipe 1643: Vora buys Calicut pepper with cotton & opium, sells to EIC at double; "Virge Vora... must not bee displeased in any case"; "so long as Virge Vora is so much our credittor, little or no proffitt". Maratha plunder of Surat 1663/64 hit him; recovered.
- PAREKH family (EIC): Tapidas (broker/house shroff 1609–1660; allowance 500 mahmudis), Tulsidas (shroff 1636–67), Bhimji Parekh (d.1686; family fortune >£1m ≈ 8m Rs, 1682; led Bania community — withdrawal of ~8,000 Banias from Surat over forced conversion), brothers Kalyandas, Kisso, Vithaldas; joint shares. Lost to Parsi Rustamji Manikji (broker for New EIC; replaced Banwalidas Parekh 1712 after 1709 merger); Laldas Parekh regained after 1719 (patrons Robert Cowan gov. of Bombay, Henry Lowther); feud with Naoroji Rustamji (went to London, won compensation + revived 150,000 Rs claim vs Parekhs); Jagannath Laldas imprisoned 1736, fled to Marathas 1738, comeback 1740s as MARFATIA (agent) until 1761 — role in English takeover of Surat castle 1759. By 1740 EIC downgraded broker → marfatia.
- VOC: Mohandas Naan (early 17th c); Kishandas 1659–1686 (shipowner, principal buyer); grandsons Risikdas & Bhagwandas (monopoly; VOC's attempt to sell small lots in late 1680s–90s failed); co-brokers Risikdas & Wanmalidas 1715–27; Rudraram Raidas & Govindram + Parsi Mancherji Khurshedji 1749–mid-1780s; Manik Dada modi (provisions); after 4th Anglo-Dutch War (1781–83) Prem Shankar with co-brokers; office in family till VOC dissolved 1795.
- PARSIS (Zoroastrians from Persia) rose late 17th c, challenged Bania brokering: Rustamji Manikji; Mancherji Khurshedji (half a dozen ships; monopsony over VOC imports — spices, sugar, copper, tin, tusk, lead, alum; ignored contract terms, huge debt — VOC tried public auctions, failed); Dhanjishah Manjishah; Sorabji & Ratanji (VOC suppliers); Dadabhai Manikji & Edul Dada (EIC textile contractors).
- CHANGE in 18th c: companies split broker's portfolio — broker (makelaar) sells imports; suppliers (leveranciers) provide exports; another for local admin; 2+2 joint; curtailed broker authority; brokers more dependent on companies, diversified portfolios; PROTECTION contractual: low customs at company ports, protection of person/wealth. 1768 VOC protected Mancherji from Dhanjishah. 1771 Portuguese at Daman seized Dhanjishah's ship from E Africa (no Portuguese pass/EIC pass) → EIC: he is our marfatia, our subject; Goa governor invoked Catherine of Braganza marriage treaty (Bombay dowry) & "ancient rights"; Nov 1772 EIC got nawab of Surat to detain Portuguese property 48,808 Rs (then 61,517 Rs 1773) → jurisdiction & passes discourse.
- Conclusion: 17th c Indian merchants better placed (companies short of funds, urgent need to sell/buy before ships sailed); companies tried violence but couldn't tilt; 18th c qualitative change — companies asserted position, but still interdependence.
