# S15 Nick Burningham, "Shipping of the Indian Ocean World" (Schottenhammer ed. vol II ch 6, pp141–201) [Western Australian Maritime Museum]. + S. Bhattacharya "The IO in the 19th & Early 20th Centuries" (in Satish Chandra ed. 1987, pp302–316) — only via lecture.
- Arabian Sea tradition (= "Arabo-Indian", "western IO") — Arabo-Indian obscures Persian contribution; broad heterogeneous class foreigners saw as one — loosely "dhow" (Manguin). Geographic: Arabian Peninsula, Red Sea, Gulf, Iran, Pakistan, India to Malabar.
- al-Sirafi (Abu Zaid Hasan) 916: Omanis went to "the islands" (Lakshadweep/Maldives) to build sewn-plank ships of coconut timber, coir twine & rope, frond-matting sails.
- Oldest shipping anywhere: Magan ships to Oman c.3000 BCE (reed boats; Marsh Arabs). Periplus: local craft sewn/lashed. Strabo/Poseidonius: shipwrecked Indian sailor showed Eudoxos route to India. Ammianus Marcellinus (4th c): Gulf coast throng of ships. Persian ships to China by 8th c; Chinese terms Bosi (Persia) & Dashi (Arabia). After 878/79 Guangzhou massacre ships loaded Chinese goods at Isthmus of Kra.
- 12th c three segments: Arabian Sea (Arabs/Persians), Bay of Bengal (Indian Muslims esp. Gujaratis), SCS. Range: Kra/Andaman/Nicobar to E Africa; India–Arabia–E Africa triangle. Photos of Mombasa & Muttrah: Indian vessels carried much trade. Agius: Indians "masters of the IO triangle trade not only as pilots but also as merchants" when Portuguese came.
- Gujarati shipwrights most proficient per Portuguese; average Gujarati ship 300–600 tons (Manguin); Sultan of Gujarat's Meri taken at Hormuz 1510 up to 800 tons; Cabral chronicler: no nails, "very well built... nicely sewn with ropes" → rabbeted seams (vadhera). Bhuj bas-relief (palliya stone, 14th/15th c). EIC recruited Parsi shipwrights from Surat 1736 led by Lovji Nusserwanjee Wadia ("wadia" = shipwright in Gujarati) → Bombay yards; Maconochie: bottoms half as thick as English, rabbeted; Grose: "incomparably the best ships in the world for duration" up to 1000 tons; HMS Trincomalee (1817) still afloat; HMS Cornwallis (1813) broken up 144 years later sound. Large 19th c dhows with ornamented sterns based on Anglo-Parsi Bombay designs.
- Features: straight raked stem, straight keel, straight vertical sternpost; angular profile; single mast near midships; settee sail (not true triangular lateen — recent development); garboard on top of keel → sharp bottom; plank-first construction; frames exist (Belitung frames ~330 mm apart — refutes "no frames before Europeans"); Correia: Angediva & Cannanore sewn ships with few frames sewn to planks.
- Sewing: original = wadding inside & outside, identical pattern (Belitung; now only S India); later wadding inside only. al-Wasiti (1237) Maqamat of al-Hariri illustrations show paired stitches. Portuguese noted no iron fastenings early 16th c; some nailed ships by 15th–16th c.
- Belitung (9th c, c.826, Arab dhow from China with Changsha ceramics): 15.3 m keel, sharp V hull, NO nails or dowels; four stitches per hole → monocoque. Replicas: Sohar (1979–80, Oman→China, now on roundabout); Jewel of Muscat (Oman→Singapore 2010) craned with no distortion. Drawback: coir must be replaced every few years; size ceiling.
- Rudders: sternpost-hung rudders in Arabian Sea iconography by 12th c, arguably before Europe's earliest (Winchester Cathedral font c.1180, iron pintle & gudgeon). Arabian: no iron hinges — sternpost extended with fashin, cheek timbers, ropes; loads not transmitted to sewn hull; blade in "clean water", lift for beaching.
- Other Indian traditions (S & E India, Bay of Bengal): east coast lee shore in NE monsoon, cyclones, tsunami destroyed Chola Pumpuhar (Kaveripattinam) 11th c, silting; Visakhapatnam only natural harbour. Bharhut boats: double-dovetail ("butterfly") timber straps; Andhra coins ships two equal masts; Ajanta three masts. Masula surf boats flexible, few frames, sewn, easily disassembled. Jaffna dhonis; brigs, barques & dhonis until steamers; flat-bottomed; later hybrid Anglo-Indian. Hornell: baggala & pattamar so cheap European "true ships" couldn't compete on west coast.
- Swahili tradition (Kenya, Somalia; Lamu archipelago, Bajun people): mtepe & dau la mtepe; edge-dowelled + sewn; planks split from green mangrove; keelson (shared with Belitung & 10th c Cirebon wrecks); triangular cross-section; Vasco da Gama on Mozambique ships: no nails, cords, palm-mat sails, Genoese needles, quadrants, charts; fast & weatherly.
- Maldivian: edge-dowelled, closer to SE Asia than to neighbours — "geography does not predict technology".
- SE Asian lashed-lug: planks with carved lugs lashed to frames, edge-dowelled; Pontian boat (3rd–5th c CE) fully developed; 9th c large lashed-lug wreck central Vietnam (keel >22 m); Borobudur reliefs: double outriggers, tanja sails, bipod masts, quarter rudders; Paya Pasir/Kota Cina (12th–14th c); Java Sea wrecks (Intan, Karawang, Cirebon). JONG: 1512 Portuguese found jong larger than their largest ship; Albuquerque's fleet fired 2 days on a jong from Pasai — 4 layers of planking too thick for largest cannon; Portuguese poop barely as high as jong's waist; disabled steering. Perreira 1582: Javanese jongs no iron, wooden dowels. Hybrid SE Asian–Chinese tradition (South China Sea) after Ming 1371 ban on private trade. 16th c Portuguese disruption → large jong disappeared, replaced by smaller vessels.
# Lecture 15 (Bhattacharya part): sail→steam slow; 1770s French marine steam engines; 1830 Bombay–Suez mail steamer (state-subsidised); 1840s P&O, British India Steam Navigation Co., Messageries Maritimes; timber still 90% of tonnage; 1869 Suez decisive; 1825 crossings ~25% longer by early steam, refuelling issues; dhows & katias survived in "unorganised sector" of dual economy (Boeke). Iron hulls → compass deviation; Flinders' corrections & charts cut England–Australia ~20%. Submarine telegraph from 1870: Britain–India–Australia/Africa; homogenised prices; centralised administration in London more than post-1857 reforms. British merchant marine 2.5 m tons (post-Napoleonic) → 9.3 m by 1900; shipping income covered >half of Britain's trade deficit 1825–50; raw-material import price index 215 (1811–15) → 94 (1846–50) (1880=100). Obsolescence of timber shipbuilding → dependency; enabled mass indentured migration after abolition of slavery. World-systems frame: IO incorporated as periphery. Two mechanisms: 16th c COERCION vs 19th c COMPETITION. INS Kaundinya (stitched ship, Porbandar→Oman Jan 2026).
