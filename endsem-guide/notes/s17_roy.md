# S17 Tirthankar Roy, India in the World Economy (CUP 2012) ch 7 "Colonialism and Development, 1860–1920" pp181–209 [RB6]
- British Empire a watershed; processes obscured by ideological debates; START from MARKET INTEGRATION via institutional & technological change.
- Theories: Hobson & Lenin — capitalism reached out to periphery to avert falling returns at core; critics: attraction of periphery. Marxists (1950s–60s): Paul Baran, Walter Rodney, André Gunder Frank — imperialism cause of underdevelopment (exchange relations or class); rejected modernization theory. Indian orthodoxy "fundamentally neo-Marxist": deindustrialization, DRAIN, exploitative class structure (Bagchi, Irfan Habib, Aditya Mukherjee); export crops "forced" on peasants by moneylender–landlord–revenue officer alliance.
- Roy's critique: peasants responded positively to profitability; drain measurement crude; puzzle — India forged ahead among colonised tropics: large factory industry, one of biggest railway & telegraph systems, best banks, ports, universities, hospitals; "Far from deindustrializing, India industrialized"; artisans not so bad. "So-called drain was not a payment for empire; it was the price paid for the gains derived from market integration" — services for trade, business, education, health.
- Tech/science pessimists: Headrick Tools of Empire (bias to technologies useful to imperialists), Baber, Inkster ("transfer of loyalties"), Deepak Kumar ("Science, modernization and domination all marched together"), Nandy. Roy: too restrictive — public-goods character (railways for defence used for profit too) + private choices driven by profit.
- Colonial state one of POOREST: 1820s tax per head in 3 presidencies <1/10 of England's, half or less of other colonies; worse vs Japan/Russia in early 20th c; land tax main; "night watchman" (Morris D. Morris); LEGISLATING state "to a degree unknown in Indian history"; enabling conditions: single umbrella of property & contract law, one official language, one leading currency, uniform channels of knowledge → "failure" in direct change but "success" as modern state creating institutions for global capitalism. Railways = private enterprise under state guarantee; poor long-run infrastructure record.
- AGRICULTURE: 1765 diwani; land >80% of revenue; 1793 Cornwallis Permanent Settlement (zamindari) — proprietary right, revenue legal-contractual; awarded to old tax-collector elite → peasants tenants. Ryotwari in west, south, north (peasant gets right). Critics: ownership over user rights, absentee landlords, debt trap. Roy: caution — ryotwari areas investment & dynamism; low investment due to scarce WATER (canals feasible in Ganges/Indus snowmelt plains, not Deccan; wells risky). Commercialisation limited: peak only 10–15% cropland for nonfood exports; few districts; gains only with conjunction of land, property rights, cheap water, railways; <10% irrigated; rain-fed vs irrigated yield gap up to 4x; millets. UNEVEN.
- FACTORY INDUSTRY: employment 100,000 (1860) → 2 million (1940), 4%/yr; fastest 1870–1921 (5.3%); WWI boost; net factory income +4.3%/yr 1900–47; concentrated in BOMBAY & CALCUTTA (>half employment even at end). Obstacles (high interest, no labour markets, transport costs, merchants didn't understand machines) overcome by colonial connection: railways, British capital, hire foremen & buy machinery; Suez 1869. First industrialists made money in IO trade (Parsis originally artisans/carpenters/weavers turned brokers, merchants, shipbuilders); Ahmedabad banking houses switched to cotton/opium. First steam cotton-yarn factory near Calcutta 1817/18 (European); sustainable mill by Parsi merchant of Bombay 1854 (Cowasji Davar); railways cut cotton transport 3 months → 1 day; 1860s cotton price spike (American Civil War) → profits into mills; 10 mills 1865 → 271 by 1914, 260,000 workers/day. 1870–1914 main buyers of Bombay yarn = Chinese handloom weavers; Lancashire finer counts vs Bombay coarse; lost China to Japan c.1900 → Indianisation, weaving. JUTE: Calcutta, sacking; Dundee & Germany until 1870s; George Acland's mill 1855; 5 mills (1869) → 64 (1913), 215,000 workers; world near-monopoly; EUROPEAN enterprise for world packaging market (cotton = Indian enterprise for Indian/China markets) — Europeans raised capital in London, Indians from personal resources; Marwari jute merchants & Bengali peasants. Tea plantations (Assam) needed institutions.
- ARTISANS: nationalists said destroyed by English cloth — overstatement: >10 million artisans in 1900; differentiated markets; HANDLOOM revival with fly-shuttle slay, frame loom, jacquard, dobby, drop-box, synthetic dyes; handloom cloth volume ~doubled 1900–1930 with same workers & looms. IRON: smelters died but blacksmiths gained — per capita iron 0.9 kg (1788) → 3.2 kg (1914); import share 30% → 78%; urban blacksmiths "substantial and prosperous class", English anvils & steel. LEATHER/tanning: hides export from 1860s; quality problems; factories in Bombay, Madras, Calcutta, Agra, Kanpur; Muslim merchants of north India; Hakka Chinese of Calcutta dominant during WWI, integrated tanning & footwear; after 1962 war pressure → restaurateurs.
- SCIENCE & TECH: three modes — European scholarship on Indian culture (Warren Hastings codification; Said's Orientalism critique — but much research autonomous; William Jones, James Mill; Indian translators shaped it), engineering & medicine via public works/education, private business choices. Fort William College (1800), Calcutta Botanic Garden (1787 Robert Kyd), Asiatic Society, Serampore mission; Francis Buchanan surveys; James Prinsep (mint, metrology, Asiatic Society); William Roxburgh (Flora Indica, Hortus Bengalensis; coffee, teak, tobacco, hemp); James Dinwiddie brought tea plant from China; Henry Piddington "law of storms" cyclonology for merchant marine; indigo, cinchona, rubber, tea; Robert Bruce found wild tea in Assam (120 sites), 90 chests to London 1839; first company tea estate 1839 (John Masters) failed commercially.
- Second half 19th c transfers: stationary steam engine, telegraph, railways, engineering education, medicine. Buy-British bias → costs (mule vs ring spindles; no import substitution in machinery like Japan) but Bombay–Manchester contact enabled diversification into weaving; Europeans among mill supervisors 100% → <30% by 1925; learning in railway workshops, arsenals, mills, surveys, engineering colleges; Indians majority of technical cadres by 1947.
- LAW: common factor of empires = LEGISLATION. Three strategies: APPROPRIATION (settler land via terra nullius, slavery/indenture — irrelevant in India), INCORPORATION (juridical autonomy to communities — India: religious codes as civil law; failed — too many divisions, costly litigation), STANDARDISATION (uniform commercial law — sales contracts, credit instruments, company law) — shift early in Crown rule. Contract breach as CRIMINAL offence: Act VIII 1819 (presidency towns), Act XIII 1859 (plantations), Act IX 1860 (public works); indigo regulations 1823, 1824, 1830; Act XI 1860; Blue Mutiny (indigo, Bengal 1860); Assam indenture. Contract Act 1872 (English/American, no Indian custom); 1870–1890 codification of trusts, transfer of property, promissory notes, evidence, wills, specific relief. Assam tea land & labour laws.
- CONCLUSION: Marxists right that empire sought market integration but wrong to jump to state-directed surplus transfer making periphery poor; difference lies in HOW integration happened: LAW, LANGUAGE (English lingua franca, eagerly embraced), KNOWLEDGE/SKILLS. Mauritius planters accessed Indian workers; Bombay millowners accessed Lancashire foremen; "India's precocious industrialization cannot be explained otherwise"; cost = explosion of contractual disputes → standardisation. Contradiction: liberalism/freedom of markets vs subjection (unfreedom) — Great Depression exposed it.
