SESSION12

Mariners, Merchants and Monks in the Indian Ocean

Ranabir Chakravarti, "Indic Mercantile Networks… c. 500–1500" (2019) · Mansur al-Sharida, "Merchants without Borders: Qusman Traders… c. 1850–1950" (2023) · Lecture 12
In one lineThe Indian Ocean was connected by people (mariners, merchants, monks), not by geography alone. From the Geniza traders of 1100 to the Najdi traders of 1900, the same ocean kept rewarding mobility and trust; only the scale, technology and imperial setting changed.

1. The big picture Must know

Who made the "connected space" work?

Mariners knew winds and routes. Merchants organised goods, credit and risk. Monks and religious groups carried texts, rituals and gifts. Trade also created diasporas, many languages in one port, and trust.

Buddhist monks and merchants used the same ships and ports; a Hindu Chola king paid for a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam.

"Connected space" (Subrahmanyam)

Places are linked by movement, shaped by encounters, part of larger processes, and the links are repeated, not accidental.

a railway network: no station makes sense alone; it matters because trains keep coming back.

Why read the two articles together?

Chakravarti = medieval foundations (500–1500). al-Sharida = modern transformation (1850–1950). Together they show continuity and change in one ocean.

"Segmented mobility"

Goods moved in stages: ship → port → caravan → inland. No ship could cross the whole ocean in one monsoon, so goods were transshipped at stopover ports.

Gulf goods reached al-Qasim by ship to Bahrain, then by camel caravan inland.

2. Chakravarti: South Asia was "a hinge, not an edge" Must know

Why India is central

The Indian Ocean is the only enclosed ocean. India and Sri Lanka sit in the middle, with two long coasts, so ships stopped there to wait for the next monsoon and swap cargo. This is geography, not Indian pride (he rejects replacing Eurocentrism with Indocentrism).

The "yawning gap"

Most historians study 1500–1800 because the Portuguese and the East India Companies left big archives. Chakravarti fills the millennium before 1500.

Two lessons from the sources

(1) Indians did go to sea; the "taboo on sea travel" idea is wrong. (2) Ships carried everyday bulk goods, not only luxuries.

Bengal sent rice to the Maldives and got cowrie shells (used as money) back.

Ports and power shifts

Abbasids + Siraf → Persian Gulf strong; Fatimid Egypt (969) → Red Sea strong; Hormuz from the 13th c. Indian ports: Cambay, Somnath, Thana, Quilon, Calicut (west); Nagapattinam, Motupalli, Chittagong (east).

The Cairo Geniza: letters of merchants

Old letters kept in a Cairo synagogue. Jewish traders went Tunisia → Cairo → Red Sea → Aden → Malabar.

Abraham Ben Yiju lived in Mangalore 1132–49, ran a bronze factory, married a local Tulu woman, Ashu. His friend in Aden was Madmun.

A ship carrying his pepper sank at Bab-el-Mandeb (c. 1138): "The pepper was a total loss." Traders lived with real risk.

Trust across religions

A Jewish trader robbed near Thana was told to ask the Hindu shipowner Tinbu for help, because they had "bonds of friendship and brotherhood". Information mattered: "one who is present can see what is not seen by one who is absent".

Chola power and merchant guilds

Merchant bodies like the ainnurruvar ("the 500 of Ayyavole", an umbrella guild), manigramam and anjuvannam traded and gave money to temples. The Cholas sent embassies to China and in 1025 raided Srivijaya (Kadaram) to cut out this middleman.

Kings as protectors

The Kakatiya king Ganapati's Motupalli charter (1245) promised merchants safety from pirates and only fixed taxes, because "wealth is dearer than life" to merchants.

Visakhapatnam was even renamed after the Chola king Kulottunga, who cut tolls to attract trade.

Horses: the biggest import

India lacked good warhorses. Arabian and Persian horses came by sea: Marco Polo says 10,000 a year to the Pandya kingdom, at about 220 gold coins each.

Plural ports; an open sea

At Somnath (1264) a Muslim shipowner from Hormuz built a mosque with land arranged by his Hindu friends, approved by the Saiva priest. Rulers rarely kept navies at sea: before 1500 the ocean was open (mare liberum), unlike the Mediterranean.

Skip: Socotra inscriptions, Cosmas Indicopleustes, Harikela coins, William of Adam's crusade plan (good extras, rarely asked).

3. al-Sharida: desert traders in an ocean world (1850–1950) Must know

Who were the Qusman?

Traders from al-Qasim in Najd (central Arabia; towns Buraydah and Unayzah). Najd was poor, so its people moved to Kuwait, Bahrain, Basra and on to Bombay, Calcutta, Karachi.

Salih al-Bassam reached Bombay in 1887; his family brand "Shimagh al-Bassam" (red headdress) still exists.

His main point

The desert interior and the ocean were one commercial world. Goods came by sea to the Gulf, then by camel (about 20 days) to al-Qasim, which became an entrepôt. So Najd was in the global economy by the 19th c.

Trade in anything profitable

Horses, pearls, gold, currency, weapons, textiles, carpets, dates, rice, sugar, tea, coffee, perfume. Diversification helped them survive shocks.

When pearls crashed (WWI, cultured pearls 1925, Depression 1929), they switched to gold smuggling to India for 300–400% profit.

How they worked

Family firms and agents in many cities, letters instead of contracts, constant travel home and back. Families: al-Bassam, al-Assafi, al-Dhukayr, al-Mushayqih, al-Khunayni.

Arab horses were sold at Bombay's Byculla market for Rs 600–900.

What continued (500 → 1950)

Monsoon routes · ports linking sea and inland · trust, letters, diasporas · luxuries + staples · many cultures together.
vs

What changed

Steamships and Suez (1869) · British rules and treaties (1820) · price-sensitive global markets · gold and currency trades · colonial control of movement.

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Who connected the Indian Ocean more effectively before modern nation-states: rulers, merchants, mariners, or religious communities?

The Indian Ocean was connected less by geography than by the people who moved across it. Reading Chakravarti and al-Sharida together shows that merchants and mariners made the daily connections, religious communities gave them trust, and rulers mostly protected them.

Merchants and mariners built the routine links. The Cairo Geniza letters show Abraham Ben Yiju living in Mangalore (1132–49), trading pepper and iron with partners in Aden, while Indian shipowners such as Fatanswami carried the cargo. Centuries later, Qusman families from Najd linked the Arabian desert to Bombay and Calcutta through agents and letters (al-Sharida).

Religious communities supplied moral ties: the Chola kings funded a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam, and at Somnath in 1264 Hindu friends helped a Muslim shipowner build a mosque. Rulers acted mainly as patrons; the Motupalli charter (1245) protected merchants from pirates. But rulers seldom kept navies, so the sea stayed open (mare liberum), with the 1025 Chola raid as an exception.

Thus no single group connected the ocean. Merchants and mariners did most of the work, but it was the combination of trade, faith and protection that made the Indian Ocean a connected world.

SESSION13

Textiles, Spices and Perfumes across the Indian Ocean

H.P. Ray, "Warp and Weft: Producing, Trading and Consuming Indian Textiles Across the Seas" (2018) · Toslima Khatun, Global Trade in the Early Medieval World (2025), ch. 3 (via slides) · Lecture 13
In one lineThings connected Europe and Asia as much as ships did: Indian cloth worn on bodies, spices used in food and medicine, and perfumes burned in courts. Europe entered an older system; it did not create it.

1. Why study commodities? Must know

Commodities as "historical actors"

Goods show who wanted what, and where; how local ecology fed long-distance trade; how trade linked to ritual, medicine and diplomacy; and they make consumers visible, not only merchants.

reading history through your wardrobe: each item tells you where it came from and what it says about you.

Two kinds of goods

Bulk/practical: everyday cotton, indigo dye, wood, food. Prestige: fine muslin and silk, pepper, cloves, camphor, musk, ambergris, incense.

2. Textiles (Ray): "not a mere luxury" Must know

Ray's argument

Indian cloth was a means of communication and a marker of identity across the ocean, used to build social relationships. Only some kinds travelled (fine, patterned, valuable). It was not just something foreigners bought to pay for spices.

A very old tradition

Cotton was grown at Mehrgarh (5th millennium BCE); Mohenjo-daro had red-dyed cotton around silver vases.

Cloth at home showed rank and rules

Dharma texts gave colours to varnas (brahmin white, kshatriya red, vaishya yellow) and told brahmins not to trade indigo. Buddhist monks first could wear only cotton (to look different); silk came later, the "middle way".

Tamil poems: white cloth covers a dead hero; the king gives silk to a poet.

The technology

Cotton doesn't hold dye easily, so a mordant (fixative) was painted or block-printed; resist dyeing used wax or mud to keep areas white. Indigo gave durable blue (Greek indikon melan = "Indian black"; Sanskrit nīla).

Archaeology proves the trade

Berenike (Red Sea port, Egypt): about 800 textile pieces; half the cotton is Z-spun, the Indian twist (Romans used S-spin). Fustat, Quseir al-Qadim (Egypt): Indian blue cloth with elephants and "tree of life". Newberry collection: designs like Jain manuscripts, so from Gujarat. Thailand and Sulawesi also have Indian cloth.

"Archaeology confirms that written accounts were not exaggerating."

How the trade was organised

Guilds produced and financed: at Nasik a donor invested money with a weavers' guild and used the interest to clothe monks. Merchant bodies (Anjuvannam, Manigramam) linked ports and inland areas. States taxed but did not control trade (Periplus: no state control).

Gifts, not just goods

Geniza traders sent fine muslins mostly as presents (to wives, partners, rabbis); plain cotton was traded but "secondary". In Java, land-grant ceremonies gave out 200 pieces of cloth.

Cloth as paper

Cotton was also writing material: when Ben Yiju ran out of paper in India, he wrote on cloth.

Ray's conclusionValue differed by time and place, so avoid generalising. Study cloth both locally (micro) and across the ocean (macro).

3. Spices: more than flavour Must know

Why spices travelled so well

High value for their weight, long shelf life, elite demand, and medicinal use. Rarity came from ecology and distance.

pepper from Malabar, cinnamon from Sri Lanka, cloves from the Moluccas.

Khatun: no single boss

Arab traders joined existing Asian markets, using cabotage (short coastal hops). There was no hegemonic group and no sudden "explosion". When spices' medical uses were studied, their value rose.

Abu-Lughod

Before European Hegemony (1989): a world system existed before Europe; the Red Sea was the preferred route; trade adapted when empires fell, it never stopped.

Fragile ships, important ports

al-Sirafi: if winds were too strong, merchants sold wherever they landed, so ports mattered.

4. Perfumes: political goods Must know

Luxury + ritual

Musk, ambergris, camphor, sandalwood, aloe wood were used in tribute, diplomacy and court ceremony; they mixed luxury, medicine and ritual, turning rarity into political capital.

At Caliph Harun al-Rashid's wedding, guests received perfume in glass bottles; the bride got huge pearls.

The Caliphate as a hinge

Baghdad absorbed Indian goods and passed them on to Egypt and Europe (even Charlemagne's court). Controlling Sind let the Caliphate cut out middlemen. Expansion intensified but did not invent these networks.

The Belitung wreck carried 60,000+ Chinese ceramics: the market was huge.

Shared

Needed special knowledge and local ecology; moved through ports and middlemen; carried meanings beyond price.
vs

Different

Textiles = identity on bodies and in homes. Spices = concentrated, portable value. Perfumes = court ritual and diplomacy.

What they changed: tastes and consumer culture; merchant diasporas; spread of techniques and words; made the ocean "a lived material world, not just a map".

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Which commodity best explains Indian Ocean connectivity: textiles, spices or perfumes?

All three linked Europe and Asia, but each did so differently. Comparing their portability, meaning and market reach shows that textiles explain connectivity most fully, while spices and perfumes add value and political meaning.

Spices were the most portable: high value for their weight and a long shelf life, so they suited long voyages. Khatun shows Arab merchants joining existing Asian markets through cabotage to obtain them. Perfumes carried the most political meaning: musk, camphor and ambergris appeared in tribute and court ritual, as at Harun al-Rashid's wedding, where perfumes in glass vessels displayed the Caliphate's wealth.

Textiles had the widest reach. H.P. Ray shows that Indian cotton reached Berenike on the Red Sea (Z-spun cotton), Fustat, Java and Sulawesi, served both everyday and elite buyers, and was organised by weavers' guilds and merchant bodies. Cloth also carried identity and ritual meaning. But Ray warns that value varied by region, so we should not generalise.

Textiles therefore best explain the depth of connection, though the three goods worked together. Their story also shows that Europe entered an older Asian system of exchange rather than creating it.

Islam and the Indian Ocean World

Geoff Wade, "Islam Across the Indian Ocean to 1500 CE" (2019) · Lecture 14 (with Mahmood Kooria on Ponnāni)
In one lineIslam did not simply "spread" across the ocean. Merchants settled, communities formed, networks grew, and local societies remade Islam in their own way, often under non-Muslim rulers, until the Portuguese turned trade rivalry into religious conflict.

1. How the network formed Must know

"Islam does not travel alone"

People, trade, language, law and politics move as one bundle. So ask "who brought Islam, and through which relationships?" Carriers: Arab and Persian merchants, Gujarati and Tamil Muslims, Sufis, scholars, refugees.

Pearson's "Indian Ocean world"

The ocean is an arena of connection, not an obstacle. For Malabar the real "frontier" was the sea, not inland India.

Why it's hard to study

Islam has no images, so historians use tombstones, a few texts, shipwreck finds and Chinese records (Wade's new source).

Background

Baghdad (762) pushed Gulf–China trade (~6 months to Canton). The 878 Canton massacre ended direct voyages, so trade became segmented (Arabian Sea / Bay of Bengal / South China Sea).

2. South India: Muslims without a Muslim state Must know

Muslim presence ≠ Muslim rule

A town could have a Hindu king, Muslim merchants, a mosque and Muslim law all at once. So Islam does not need a Muslim state.

Al-Masudi: 10,000 Muslims at Saymur (Chaul) under a Hindu king, who appointed a Muslim leader to judge them.

Calicut and the Zamorin

The Hindu Zamorin protected Muslim merchants because they were economically essential; merchants even gave him ships and soldiers: mutual dependence.

Coast vs inland

Coastal Muslims followed the Shafi'i school (comfortable under non-Muslim kings) and linked to other Shafi'i ports; inland Muslims were Hanafi. Malabar Muslims: Pardesi (foreign) and Mappila (local converts).

Why convert? (Eaton)

No single cause: trade, marriage, patronage, social mobility, Sufis, local adaptation. A slow, negotiated process, elites first.

Low-caste Malabaris who became Muslim could move freely, outside caste rules (Correia).

3. Networks beyond trade Must know

Khutba network (Lambourn)

In the Friday sermon (khutba), Indian coastal mosques named the Rasulid sultan of Yemen, who paid their preachers. This meant loyalty without territorial rule and "preferred trading partners".

Sufi networks

The Kazaruni order had lodges at Cambay, Calicut, Quilon and Quanzhou; ships paid money to its saint, so it was called a "Sufi insurance company". Caution (Wade): don't assume Sufis converted Asia before 1500.

Family, contracts, gravestones

Hadhrami scholars in Malabar (Engseng Ho); partnership contracts (mufawada, commenda); gravestones carved in Cambay shipped to Pasai and Gresik (Lambourn).

Layered identity

A Malabar Muslim could be Malabari + Muslim + merchant + the Zamorin's subject + part of a global network at once. The umma never erased local identity. Engseng Ho: a diaspora stops being "foreign" when later generations stay local but keep their wider ties.

4. Southeast Asia and China Must know

Locals remade Islam

Southeast Asians were not passive. Muslim communities by the 10th c.; first Muslim rulers in the 13th c.: Malik al-Salih of Samudera-Pasai (d. 1297). Feener: real conversion first among elites.

Malacca: why would a king convert?

Trade, diplomacy, legitimacy, marriage. Its ruler Iskandar Shah converted after marrying into the Pasai royal family. Malacca became a hub of trade and learning (Reid's "Age of Commerce").

Quanzhou: read a tombstone as a map

A nisba (name of origin) on Quanzhou graves (Isfahan, Shiraz, Bukhara, Yemen) shows the wide world the community imagined. Chinese records call Muslim envoys Li (= 'Ali) and Pu (= Abu).

Refugees spread Islam too

The 1360s violence in Quanzhou made Muslims flee by sea; soon Muslim graves appear in Java (Trowulan, from the 1370s). Lesson: forced migration creates new religious maps.

Zheng He, a Muslim admiral, led Ming fleets (1405–35) that linked these communities.

5. Ponnāni and the ribāṭ (Kooria) Must know

Microhistory

Study one place to see the whole ocean. Ponnāni was the Zamorin's second (military) capital: port, navy base, school, trade hub, frontier.

What is a ribāṭ?

First a frontier fort; for Kooria, a whole coastal community organised around defence, religion and sea life. An idea, not just a building.

1498: what changed

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut. The Portuguese wanted to control trade by force. Trade rivalry → political conflict → expressed in religious terms (jihad).

Who made the ribāṭ?

A coalition: the Zamorin (revenue, sovereignty), the 'ulamā' (jihad language), the Kuññāli Marakkārs (navy), the Māppiḷas (trade, fishing, money). Not simply "Muslims vs Christians".

ConclusionFrom "how did Islam spread?" to "how did Muslim communities become embedded and reshape their identities?" Islam was made, remade and localised through maritime networks.

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "Islam became an Indian Ocean religion not simply because Muslims travelled, but because mobility created communities that local societies reshaped." Discuss.

Intro: Islam "does not travel alone"; the better question is who carried it and how communities became embedded (Wade; Kooria).

Settlement without states: monsoon stays turned visitors into residents. At Saymur (Chaul) 10,000 Muslims lived under a Hindu king with their own judge; at Calicut the Zamorin and Muslim merchants depended on each other.

Networks: Lambourn's khutba network tied Indian mosques to the Rasulids of Yemen without territorial rule; the Kazaruni Sufis had lodges from Cambay to Quanzhou; Cambay gravestones reached Pasai and Gresik.

Local remaking: Malacca's ruler converted through marriage to Pasai; Quanzhou's refugees of the 1360s carried Islam to Java; Kooria's ribāṭ at Ponnāni joined the Zamorin, 'ulamā', Marakkār navy and Mappila traders against the Portuguese.

But: Sufi roles before 1500 should not be assumed (Wade), and conversion was slow and elite-led (Eaton, Feener).

Conclusion: Islam was made and remade locally; mobility created the communities, and local societies gave them their shape.

SESSION15

Shipping, Navigation and Technology: Power in the Indian Ocean

Nick Burningham, "Shipping of the Indian Ocean World" (2019) · S. Bhattacharya, "The Indian Ocean in the 19th and Early 20th Centuries" (1987, via slides) · Lecture 15
In one lineIndian Ocean ships were advanced, not primitive. Europe first won by force (16th c.), and only later by better technology: steam, iron and the telegraph (19th c.). Even then the old dhows survived.

1. Two authors, before and after Must know

Burningham (before)

Maritime archaeologist. Uses shipwrecks, pictures and fieldwork over 5,000 years. Says regional shipbuilding traditions were sophisticated engineering, not crude boats waiting to be replaced.
→

S. Bhattacharya (after)

Economic historian (world-systems, Wallerstein). 1800–WWII. Says steam, navigation and telegraph pulled the Indian Ocean into world capitalism as a subordinate supplier of raw materials (periphery).

2. Five shipbuilding traditions Must know

Arabian Sea: sewn-plank

Planks stitched with coconut rope, no nails. First described by al-Sirafi (916). The Belitung wreck (9th c.) had zero nails.

The replica Jewel of Muscat (2010) was lifted by crane without bending: the hull worked like an egg shell (a "monocoque").

Other Indian (east coast)

Planks joined with dovetail wooden straps, flat bottoms; later mixed with British designs.

Swahili (Kenya)

The mtepe: planks doweled and sewn.

Southeast Asian: lashed-lug

Wooden lugs carved on planks are tied to the frames. Already mature in the Pontian boat (3rd–5th c.).

Maldivian

More like Southeast Asian ships than its neighbours': "geography does not predict technology".

Weak points

Stitching must be replaced every few years, and there is a size limit unless the hull is strengthened.

Rudder ahead of Europe

Arabian ships had sternpost rudders by the 12th c., maybe before Europe's first (Winchester, c. 1180). Without iron hinges they used rope and an extended sternpost (fashin), which also let them lift the rudder for beaching.

Gujarati and Parsi shipwrights

The Portuguese (c. 1500) called Gujarati shipwrights the best; ships of 300–800 tons with rabbeted seams. In 1736 the EIC brought Parsi shipwrights from Surat under Lovji Nusserwanjee Wadia to Bombay.

HMS Trincomalee (1817) is still afloat; HMS Cornwallis lasted 144 years.

The vanishing jong

In 1512 the Portuguese met the Javanese jong: bigger than their ships, with planks too thick for their cannon. By the late 16th c. the jong disappeared, because Portuguese attacks on trade routes killed it, not better design.

So what?

European dominance did not start with superior technology. It started with military coercion of sea routes.

3. Two ways Europe dominated Must know

16th c.: COERCION

Portuguese navies attack ships and routes. Asian ships were as good or better. Power = force.
vs

19th c.: COMPETITION

Steam, iron, telegraph give a real edge: cheaper, faster, centralised. Power = economic advantage.

4. Bhattacharya: the transport revolution Must know

1770sFrench build marine steam engines1830first regular steamer: Bombay–Suez mail (state-paid)1840sP&O and others; but wooden ships still 90% of tonnage1869Suez Canal: the decisive blow to sailing ships1870submarine telegraph Britain–India

Slow, uneven change

Early steamers were slower than good sailing ships (about 25% longer in 1825) and needed coal. Not a "quantum jump".

Charts and compasses

Iron hulls confused compasses; Flinders' fixes and new charts cut England–Australia trips by ~20%. Tech solved a problem tech created, and made empire cheaper to run.

Telegraph = political control

It made trade faster and prices the same worldwide, but also let London run India more directly than the post-1857 reforms did.

What it changed

British fleet 2.5 m → 9.3 m tons by 1900; shipping income paid >half of Britain's trade deficit (1825–50); raw materials got cheaper; Asian shipbuilding skills became useless (dependency); cheap ships made mass indentured labour migration possible.

Survivals: the dhow beneath the steamshipArab dhows and Indian katias survived in the "unorganised sector" of a dual economy (Boeke). Steam pushed old technology to the margins, but did not destroy it.

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Compare the disappearance of the Southeast Asian jong in the 16th century with the decline of Indian Ocean sail after 1869. What differs about the mechanism?

Both cases ended with European shipping on top, but they happened in very different ways: the first through military coercion, the second through technological and economic competition.

When the Portuguese met the Javanese jong in 1512, it was bigger than their own ships and its four layers of planking resisted their cannon (Burningham). The jong was not beaten by better design. Sustained Portuguese attacks on trade routes made large jongs too risky, and by the late 16th century smaller vessels replaced them. Here dominance came from force.

After 1869 the mechanism was competition. Steam, iron hulls, new charts and the telegraph (from 1870) made transport cheaper, faster and centrally controlled, and the Suez Canal finished off timber sailing ships on the main routes (S. Bhattacharya). Asian shipyards lost their role, creating dependency.

But neither change was total: dhows and katias survived in the "unorganised sector" well into the 20th century. The comparison shows that technology is inseparable from power, and that European dominance began with coercion long before it had a real technical edge.

SESSION17

European Expansion in South Asia: Trade, Merchants, Empire

Ghulam Nadri, "The English and Dutch East India Companies and Indian Merchants in Surat" (2018) · Tirthankar Roy, India in the World Economy, ch. 7, "Colonialism and Development, 1860–1920" (2012) · Lecture 17
In one lineEuropeans did not arrive as rulers. In Surat they depended on Indian merchants (Nadri); only slowly did dependence turn into leverage. Later, colonial rule changed India's economy mainly through law, railways and knowledge, with real but uneven development (Roy).

1. Surat: Europeans enter an existing world Must know

Surat before empire

A major Mughal port linking inland goods (indigo, textiles) to the Red Sea, Gulf, Southeast Asia and Europe. The English (1600s) and Dutch (1610s) joined a mature system where Indians controlled credit, supply and market knowledge.

Not like the Portuguese

The Portuguese demanded passes (cartaz) and used violence. The EIC and VOC were pragmatic merchants who asked Mughal permission (Jahangir's farman via Thomas Roe).

What the Companies needed

Credit · textile supply · shipping · market information · political go-betweens.
⇄

What merchants gained

New buyers · price leverage · access to silver · alliances · room to bargain.

2. Nadri: interdependence, competition, contestation Must know

His argument

The companies needed Indian bankers, brokers and suppliers; merchants gained profit and status. The relationship was interdependent, but also competitive and contested. Indian merchants were strategic actors, not passive middlemen.

Virji Vora: the merchant who set the terms

Surat's richest merchant. He lent money to both companies and bought almost all their imports (a monopsony, a single buyer).

An EIC official (1643): Vora sold them pepper at double the price, but "must not be displeased in any case", because the Company owed him money.

Broker families for generations

EIC: the Parekh family (Tapidas, Bhimji Parekh) vs the Parsi Rustamji Manikji family, fighting over the office. VOC: the Kishandas family until 1795; later the Parsi Mancherji Khurshedji.

Conflict too

Mughal officials who traded saw companies as rivals; companies sometimes used violence (Middleton plundered ~15 Surat ships in 1612) but usually negotiated first.

18th-century change

Companies split the broker's job (one sells imports, another supplies exports) and renamed the broker a marfatia (agent). Brokers became more dependent, though still needed.

Protection

Merchants worked with companies partly for protection. In 1771 the Portuguese seized broker Dhanjishah's ship; the EIC said he was its subject and seized Portuguese goods in return.

3. Roy: how empire changed India's economy (1860–1920) Must know

Roy's argument

He challenges the simple "drain and deindustrialisation" story. Empire changed India by integrating markets: railways, telegraph, ports and uniform law. The state was poor (tiny taxes) but made many laws. Results were uneven.

"Far from deindustrializing, India industrialized": Bombay cotton mills, Calcutta jute mills.

Law: three strategies

Appropriation (taking land and labour, as in settler colonies): not important in India. Incorporation (ruling through religious/community law): early, but failed. Standardisation (one law for all, e.g. Contract Act 1872): grew later. These often helped capital more than labour.

Industry

Agriculture: uneven

Only 10–15% of land grew export crops; gains came only where there was land, clear rights, cheap water and railways. The main problem was scarce irrigation.

Empire brought machines, engineers, medicine; Indians learned in mills and workshops (Europeans among mill supervisors fell from 100% to under 30% by 1925). But "buy British" also imposed costs.

Q. How did dependence become a route to later domination in European expansion in South Asia?

European expansion began from weakness, not strength. In Surat the English and Dutch companies depended on Indian merchants, but repeated bargaining slowly turned this dependence into leverage and then political power.

Nadri shows that in the 17th century the EIC and VOC needed Indian credit, supplies and brokers. Virji Vora lent them money and bought nearly all their imports, so he could dictate prices; an EIC officer wrote in 1643 that he "must not be displeased in any case". Broker families such as the Parekhs managed Company trade for generations.

In the 18th century the balance shifted. The companies split the broker's role and renamed him a marfatia, and merchants increasingly relied on Company protection: in 1771 the EIC treated its broker Dhanjishah as its own subject against the Portuguese. A Parekh agent even helped the English take Surat castle in 1759. By 1860–1920, Roy shows, empire was reshaping markets through law and infrastructure.

But this was not a smooth plan: Mughal power and Indian merchants limited the companies for a long time. The lesson is that European power was negotiated before it was institutionalised; empire was a process, not a single event.

The Dutch bought tea through Batavia (it lost flavour in transit) and started direct trade only in 1729; the English took "the lion's share". From 1716 the EIC kept supercargoes at Canton.

One commodity shows the structure of an empire. It linked production, shipping, taxes, finance and consumption into one system across India, Southeast Asia and China.

Trocki's thesis

Opium gave revenue, profit and leverage. Empire depended on controlling commodity circuits, not only on conquering land.

2. The chain: India → Southeast Asia → China Must know

The EIC turned opium into a regulated tax commodity in Bengal and Bihar: peasants grew poppy on contracts with cash advances; Company factories (Patna) processed and packed it; it was auctioned at Calcutta.

Shipping

Ports like Singapore were key. Colonial governments sold opium farms: licences giving a monopoly to sell opium. An opium farm is a tax institution, not a farm. The state got steady revenue; the farmer took the risk.

Chinese kongsi

Farms were run by Chinese kongsi (shareholding partnerships) who organised labour, money and sales. Trocki: opium was "the trap" in which kongsi economies were captured by colonial capitalism.

China: not just "addiction"

Demand grew in urban, commercial, social settings and varied by class and place. Chinese consumers had agency too; they were not only passive victims.

Value along unequal links

Follow the commodity, not the nation: peasant → factory → Calcutta merchants → ships → Chinese markets. Value was created "across a chain of unequal relationships". The same chain was a money circuit (revenue, liquidity, empire).

Interconnected Port Cities in Colonial Asia

René Barendse, "Port Cities in the Gulf and the Red Sea During the Long Eighteenth Century (c. 1720–c. 1840)" (2019) · Meera Kosambi & John Brush, "Three Colonial Port Cities in India" (1988) · Lecture 20

1. Why port cities matter Must know

Nodes, not just cities

Ports linked sea routes, inland markets and states; gathered merchants, migrants, soldiers and workers; handled information, credit, storage and prices. Their importance came from connections, not population.

Kuwait had only ~5,000 people but became a major hub in the 1750s when a caravan route to Aleppo opened.

Two lenses

Barendse: trade networks, Asian autonomy, continuity (Gulf and Red Sea). Kosambi & Brush: urban morphology (shape and layout) of Madras, Bombay, Calcutta.

2. Barendse: "the last stand of Asian autonomies" Must know

His claim

In the long 18th century (c. 1720–1840), Asian trade in the Arabian seas stayed strong or grew (Anthony Reid's phrase "last stand of Asian autonomies"). Europeans mattered but did not erase Asian agency. The big break came later.

When did the break come?

1860s–70s: the cotton boom (American Civil War), steamships (P&O) and the Suez Canal (1869). After that British firms gained the upper hand and the Gulf became a quiet "British lake".

Who made trade work?

Indian merchants (Banias, mostly from Sind; Bohras, Khatris, Marwaris, Parsis), Arab shipowners and sheikhs, Iranian, Armenian, Jewish, Greek go-betweens, plus sailors, brokers and labourers.

Indians were the richest group in Mocha (Yemen).

Finance made trade work

Bills of exchange (hundis), banking links, insurance, warehouses and legal pluralism (each community its own law) cut costs.

By the 1780s a Pune firm could write a bill directly on Istanbul; in the 1500s it needed many stages.

Why trade boomed

More farming and middle-class demand; cheaper transport; China's growing demand; and new American demand for slaves from East Africa (3,000 → 20–30,000 a year).

Small, fragile ports

Most ports were small (Aden ~3,000 in 1839), short of food (Muscat's rice came from British Bengal), and hit by epidemics. Some were moving "fairs".

The "Anglo-Bania alliance" and a warning By 1780 most ships were British-run but often financed by Indian, Armenian and Arab partners. British power and Indian merchants worked together until the 1880s. "Interconnection was not simply cosmopolitan exchange": it also depended on coercion, unequal labour and slavery. Barendse agrees with Sugata Bose that under empire the ocean became more "Indian" (migration, Indian soldiers, rule from India).

The shared layout of Madras, Bombay and Calcutta.

What they share

A fort on the waterfront, an open esplanade (so guns could fire), separate European and Indian towns, a central business district (CBD), and mills and army zones on the edges. Built at empty village sites fully controlled by the EIC.

Three phases

(1) Fortified factory + white and black towns. (2) Expansion: stronger forts, esplanades, Europeans move to suburbs. (3) CBD + industry: European-style business district, crowded Indian towns, a middle zone, mills and slums.

Space encoded hierarchy

Forts held power; esplanades created "defensive emptiness"; Europeans lived spread out; Indian areas were dense and mixed. The city's map showed who ruled.

Q. Were colonial port cities engines of empire, or sites where empire had to negotiate with older Asian worlds?

Q. Why does Aspengren argue that focusing only on connectivity "risks romanticising exile"?

Recent global histories celebrate Indian revolutionaries abroad as builders of worldwide networks. Aspengren argues that this picture is partly the historian's own construction, made with hindsight, and that it hides the long silences, fears and failures of exile.

His case is two exiles in Sweden. Virendranath Chattopadhyaya arrived in Stockholm in May 1917 for a peace conference that never met; with German money gone and police urging him to stay out of politics, he fell silent and turned to translation and trade. Har Dayal, a founder of the Ghadar Party, wrote desperate letters in 1918, then called revolutionary propaganda "foolish and useless" and turned to adult education. Following Edward Said, exile was restricting and painful, not only creative.

But the revolutionaries still mattered: their plots provoked repression such as the Rowlatt Acts, which sparked Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha. Most of what we know also comes from surveillance files, so even silence reaches us through the empire's eyes.

Aspengren therefore asks for a humane history that includes despair as well as connection, a reminder that concepts shape what historians see and that writing history is an ethical act.
