European Expansion in South Asia: Trade, Merchants, Empire

Ghulam Nadri, "The English and Dutch East India Companies and Indian Merchants in Surat" (2018) · Tirthankar Roy, India in the World Economy, ch. 7, "Colonialism and Development, 1860–1920" (2012) · Lecture 17
In one lineEuropeans did not arrive as rulers. In Surat they depended on Indian merchants (Nadri); only slowly did dependence turn into leverage. Later, colonial rule changed India's economy mainly through law, railways and knowledge, with real but uneven development (Roy).

1. Surat: Europeans enter an existing world Must know

Surat before empire

A major Mughal port linking inland goods (indigo, textiles) to the Red Sea, Gulf, Southeast Asia and Europe. The English (1600s) and Dutch (1610s) joined a mature system where Indians controlled credit, supply and market knowledge.

Not like the Portuguese

The Portuguese demanded passes (cartaz) and used violence. The EIC and VOC were pragmatic merchants who asked Mughal permission (Jahangir's farman via Thomas Roe).

What the Companies needed

Credit · textile supply · shipping · market information · political go-betweens.
⇄

What merchants gained

New buyers · price leverage · access to silver · alliances · room to bargain.

2. Nadri: interdependence, competition, contestation Must know

His argument

The companies needed Indian bankers, brokers and suppliers; merchants gained profit and status. The relationship was interdependent, but also competitive and contested. Indian merchants were strategic actors, not passive middlemen.

Virji Vora: the merchant who set the terms

Surat's richest merchant. He lent money to both companies and bought almost all their imports (a monopsony, a single buyer).

An EIC official (1643): Vora sold them pepper at double the price, but "must not be displeased in any case", because the Company owed him money.

Broker families for generations

EIC: the Parekh family (Tapidas, Bhimji Parekh) vs the Parsi Rustamji Manikji family, fighting over the office. VOC: the Kishandas family until 1795; later the Parsi Mancherji Khurshedji.

Conflict too

Mughal officials who traded saw companies as rivals; companies sometimes used violence (Middleton plundered ~15 Surat ships in 1612) but usually negotiated first.

18th-century change

Companies split the broker's job (one sells imports, another supplies exports) and renamed the broker a marfatia (agent). Brokers became more dependent, though still needed.

Protection

Merchants worked with companies partly for protection. In 1771 the Portuguese seized broker Dhanjishah's ship; the EIC said he was its subject and seized Portuguese goods in return.

3. Roy: how empire changed India's economy (1860–1920) Must know

Roy's argument

He challenges the simple "drain and deindustrialisation" story. Empire changed India by integrating markets: railways, telegraph, ports and uniform law. The state was poor (tiny taxes) but made many laws. Results were uneven.

"Far from deindustrializing, India industrialized": Bombay cotton mills, Calcutta jute mills.

Law: three strategies

Appropriation (taking land and labour, as in settler colonies): not important in India. Incorporation (ruling through religious/community law): early, but failed. Standardisation (one law for all, e.g. Contract Act 1872): grew later. These often helped capital more than labour.

Industry

Factory jobs 100,000 (1860) → 2 million (1940), mostly in Bombay and Calcutta. Cotton mills: Indian capital (first lasting mill 1854; 271 mills by 1914). Jute: European capital. Limits: few regions, imported machines.

Artisans: loss and reinvention

Some crafts declined, but handloom weavers used new tools (fly-shuttle, synthetic dyes) and doubled output 1900–30; blacksmiths used imported iron; leather grew.

Agriculture: uneven

Only 10–15% of land grew export crops; gains came only where there was land, clear rights, cheap water and railways. The main problem was scarce irrigation.

Knowledge and dependence

Empire brought machines, engineers, medicine; Indians learned in mills and workshops (Europeans among mill supervisors fell from 100% to under 30% by 1925). But "buy British" also imposed costs.

Q. How did dependence become a route to later domination in European expansion in South Asia?

European expansion began from weakness, not strength. In Surat the English and Dutch companies depended on Indian merchants, but repeated bargaining slowly turned this dependence into leverage and then political power.

Nadri shows that in the 17th century the EIC and VOC needed Indian credit, supplies and brokers. Virji Vora lent them money and bought nearly all their imports, so he could dictate prices; an EIC officer wrote in 1643 that he "must not be displeased in any case". Broker families such as the Parekhs managed Company trade for generations.

In the 18th century the balance shifted. The companies split the broker's role and renamed him a marfatia, and merchants increasingly relied on Company protection: in 1771 the EIC treated its broker Dhanjishah as its own subject against the Portuguese. A Parekh agent even helped the English take Surat castle in 1759. By 1860–1920, Roy shows, empire was reshaping markets through law and infrastructure.

But this was not a smooth plan: Mughal power and Indian merchants limited the companies for a long time. The lesson is that European power was negotiated before it was institutionalised; empire was a process, not a single event.
