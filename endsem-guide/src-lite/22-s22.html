Indian Revolutionaries Abroad: Connectivity, Silence and Memory

Henrik Chetan Aspengren, "Indian Revolutionaries Abroad: Revisiting their Silent Moments" (2014) (via Lecture 22 slides; article not in folder)
In one lineIndian revolutionaries abroad (1905–1920) built global networks from London to San Francisco to Kabul, but Aspengren warns that historians who only see connections risk romanticising exile: much of exile was silence, fear and disconnection.

1. Why go abroad? Must know

Four forces

Push: repression after Swadeshi and the 1905 partition of Bengal. Pull: London's student radicalism and free press. Exclusion: racist immigration laws in North America. Opportunity: great-power rivalry, especially Germany in WWI.

Europe

India House, London (Krishnavarma, later Savarkar). Dhingra shot Curzon Wyllie (1909) → crackdown. Bhikaiji Cama raised an Indian flag at Stuttgart (1907). Berlin Committee (1914) worked with Germany.

North America

Ghadar Party (1913) of Punjabi migrants and students (Oregon/San Francisco). Komagata Maru (1914) turned away at Vancouver. Failed risings in Punjab (1914–15); Hindu–German Conspiracy trial (1917–18).

Asia

Kabul 1915: a Provisional Government of India. Singapore 1915: an Indian regiment mutinied, crushed in days. Later echoes: Rash Behari Bose, Subhas Bose's INA; M.N. Roy in Tashkent and Moscow.

2. Aspengren's warning Must know

His question and answer

What does recent "global" history of exiles leave out? The long periods of silence and disconnection. "By omitting them, one risks romanticising exile."

Why historians over-connect

We look back with hindsight and "connect the dots". But exile was often painful and insecure (Edward Said). Better words: silence, disconnection.

looking at someone's highlight reel on social media and forgetting the boring and lonely days in between.

Both were radicalised in England, not India, and both were funded by Germany. Their big plans (arms, Afghanistan, Constantinople) failed: British spies, poor German–Indian links, infighting, an India not ready.

Why Sweden?

Germany lost interest as it lost the war; both were barred from British lands; Sweden was neutral; the Stockholm Peace Conference (1917) was planned there.

Chattopadhyaya: silence imposed

Arrived 12 May 1917, opened a bureau. The conference never happened: "no conference, no audience, no allies". German money dried up, the police told him to avoid politics; he turned to translation and trade; the bureau closed.

Har Dayal: a changed mind

In Dec 1918 he wrote that he would take his life if made to leave Sweden; the next day he called anti-British propaganda "foolish and useless" and dropped his comrades. Later he backed autonomy within the British Empire, taught in Swedish adult education, and never returned to the movement.

Even in 1910 in Algiers he had found "relief in crying" and took up the mandolin on a doctor's advice: these moments "existed, so they matter".

Aspengren's conclusion

Connectivity is the historian's later construction. At the time things looked bleak and chaotic. Silence could also mean reflection that changed later thinking. We need a humane history of displaced people.

3. Impact and lessons Must know

Impact in India

Led to repression: Defence of India Act (1915), Rowlatt Report (1918), Rowlatt Acts (1919), which triggered Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha. Gave symbols (Dhingra, Kartar Singh Sarabha, later Bhagat Singh). But the plots failed. They shaped the climate more than the outcome.

Separate or connected?

Separate: different causes and rival ideas. Connected: same people, journals, routes; the British saw one conspiracy. Third view: connected but fragile, perhaps linked most by the empire's surveillance.

Three things to remember(1) Concepts shape what historians see. (2) Sources are never neutral: even silence reaches us through police and spy files. (3) Writing history is an ethical act.

Q. Why does Aspengren argue that focusing only on connectivity "risks romanticising exile"?

Recent global histories celebrate Indian revolutionaries abroad as builders of worldwide networks. Aspengren argues that this picture is partly the historian's own construction, made with hindsight, and that it hides the long silences, fears and failures of exile.

His case is two exiles in Sweden. Virendranath Chattopadhyaya arrived in Stockholm in May 1917 for a peace conference that never met; with German money gone and police urging him to stay out of politics, he fell silent and turned to translation and trade. Har Dayal, a founder of the Ghadar Party, wrote desperate letters in 1918, then called revolutionary propaganda "foolish and useless" and turned to adult education. Following Edward Said, exile was restricting and painful, not only creative.

But the revolutionaries still mattered: their plots provoked repression such as the Rowlatt Acts, which sparked Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha. Most of what we know also comes from surveillance files, so even silence reaches us through the empire's eyes.

Aspengren therefore asks for a humane history that includes despair as well as connection, a reminder that concepts shape what historians see and that writing history is an ethical act.
