Islam and the Indian Ocean World

Geoff Wade, "Islam Across the Indian Ocean to 1500 CE" (2019) · Lecture 14 (with Mahmood Kooria on Ponnāni)
In one lineIslam did not simply "spread" across the ocean. Merchants settled, communities formed, networks grew, and local societies remade Islam in their own way, often under non-Muslim rulers, until the Portuguese turned trade rivalry into religious conflict.

"Islam does not travel alone"

People, trade, language, law and politics move as one bundle. So ask "who brought Islam, and through which relationships?" Carriers: Arab and Persian merchants, Gujarati and Tamil Muslims, Sufis, scholars, refugees.

Pearson's "Indian Ocean world"

The ocean is an arena of connection, not an obstacle. For Malabar the real "frontier" was the sea, not inland India.

Why it's hard to study

Islam has no images, so historians use tombstones, a few texts, shipwreck finds and Chinese records (Wade's new source).

Background

Baghdad (762) pushed Gulf–China trade (~6 months to Canton). The 878 Canton massacre ended direct voyages, so trade became segmented (Arabian Sea / Bay of Bengal / South China Sea).

2. South India: Muslims without a Muslim state Must know

Muslim presence ≠ Muslim rule

A town could have a Hindu king, Muslim merchants, a mosque and Muslim law all at once. So Islam does not need a Muslim state.

Al-Masudi: 10,000 Muslims at Saymur (Chaul) under a Hindu king, who appointed a Muslim leader to judge them.

Calicut and the Zamorin

The Hindu Zamorin protected Muslim merchants because they were economically essential; merchants even gave him ships and soldiers: mutual dependence.

Coast vs inland

Coastal Muslims followed the Shafi'i school (comfortable under non-Muslim kings) and linked to other Shafi'i ports; inland Muslims were Hanafi. Malabar Muslims: Pardesi (foreign) and Mappila (local converts).

Why convert? (Eaton)

No single cause: trade, marriage, patronage, social mobility, Sufis, local adaptation. A slow, negotiated process, elites first.

Low-caste Malabaris who became Muslim could move freely, outside caste rules (Correia).

3. Networks beyond trade Must know

Khutba network (Lambourn)

In the Friday sermon (khutba), Indian coastal mosques named the Rasulid sultan of Yemen, who paid their preachers. This meant loyalty without territorial rule and "preferred trading partners".

Sufi networks

The Kazaruni order had lodges at Cambay, Calicut, Quilon and Quanzhou; ships paid money to its saint, so it was called a "Sufi insurance company". Caution (Wade): don't assume Sufis converted Asia before 1500.

Family, contracts, gravestones

Hadhrami scholars in Malabar (Engseng Ho); partnership contracts (mufawada, commenda); gravestones carved in Cambay shipped to Pasai and Gresik (Lambourn).

Layered identity

A Malabar Muslim could be Malabari + Muslim + merchant + the Zamorin's subject + part of a global network at once. The umma never erased local identity. Engseng Ho: a diaspora stops being "foreign" when later generations stay local but keep their wider ties.

4. Southeast Asia and China Must know

Locals remade Islam

Southeast Asians were not passive. Muslim communities by the 10th c.; first Muslim rulers in the 13th c.: Malik al-Salih of Samudera-Pasai (d. 1297). Feener: real conversion first among elites.

Malacca: why would a king convert?

Trade, diplomacy, legitimacy, marriage. Its ruler Iskandar Shah converted after marrying into the Pasai royal family. Malacca became a hub of trade and learning (Reid's "Age of Commerce").

Quanzhou: read a tombstone as a map

A nisba (name of origin) on Quanzhou graves (Isfahan, Shiraz, Bukhara, Yemen) shows the wide world the community imagined. Chinese records call Muslim envoys Li (= 'Ali) and Pu (= Abu).

Refugees spread Islam too

The 1360s violence in Quanzhou made Muslims flee by sea; soon Muslim graves appear in Java (Trowulan, from the 1370s). Lesson: forced migration creates new religious maps.

Zheng He, a Muslim admiral, led Ming fleets (1405–35) that linked these communities.

5. Ponnāni and the ribāṭ (Kooria) Must know

Microhistory

Study one place to see the whole ocean. Ponnāni was the Zamorin's second (military) capital: port, navy base, school, trade hub, frontier.

What is a ribāṭ?

First a frontier fort; for Kooria, a whole coastal community organised around defence, religion and sea life. An idea, not just a building.

1498: what changed

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut. The Portuguese wanted to control trade by force. Trade rivalry → political conflict → expressed in religious terms (jihad).

Who made the ribāṭ?

A coalition: the Zamorin (revenue, sovereignty), the 'ulamā' (jihad language), the Kuññāli Marakkārs (navy), the Māppiḷas (trade, fishing, money). Not simply "Muslims vs Christians".

ConclusionFrom "how did Islam spread?" to "how did Muslim communities become embedded and reshape their identities?" Islam was made, remade and localised through maritime networks.

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "Islam became an Indian Ocean religion not simply because Muslims travelled, but because mobility created communities that local societies reshaped." Discuss.

Intro: Islam "does not travel alone"; the better question is who carried it and how communities became embedded (Wade; Kooria).

Settlement without states: monsoon stays turned visitors into residents. At Saymur (Chaul) 10,000 Muslims lived under a Hindu king with their own judge; at Calicut the Zamorin and Muslim merchants depended on each other.

Networks: Lambourn's khutba network tied Indian mosques to the Rasulids of Yemen without territorial rule; the Kazaruni Sufis had lodges from Cambay to Quanzhou; Cambay gravestones reached Pasai and Gresik.

Local remaking: Malacca's ruler converted through marriage to Pasai; Quanzhou's refugees of the 1360s carried Islam to Java; Kooria's ribāṭ at Ponnāni joined the Zamorin, 'ulamā', Marakkār navy and Mappila traders against the Portuguese.

But: Sufi roles before 1500 should not be assumed (Wade), and conversion was slow and elite-led (Eaton, Feener).

Conclusion: Islam was made and remade locally; mobility created the communities, and local societies gave them their shape.
