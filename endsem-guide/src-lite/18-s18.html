Silver, Tea and Opium I: The Bullion and the Tea

Holden Furber, Rival Empires of Trade in the Orient, 1600–1800, ch. 3, "The Triumph of Tea" (1976) · H.V. Bowen, "Bullion for Trade, War, and Debt-Relief… 1760–1833" (2010) · Lecture 18
In one lineBritish power in Asia grew from commerce + private enterprise + war + land revenue + finance together. Tea made China central, country trade tied India to China, and silver moved in all directions, paying for trade, war and debt, not just goods.

1. The old model, and why it is too simple Must know

"Bullion for goods"

Old picture: Europe sends silver, Asia sends goods, in a straight line. Furber and Bowen add private traders, trade inside Asia, war, land revenue and reverse flows.

thinking a company's only cash flow is "sales": you miss loans, investments, and money moving between branches.

Country trade

Europe trade = Europe ↔ Asia through the companies. Country trade = trade within Asia (India ↔ Southeast Asia ↔ China) by private Europeans and Company servants. Britain grew by entering existing Asian circuits.

The paradox of monopoly

The Company had a monopoly on paper, but its own servants, ship captains and private merchants traded for themselves. Private enterprise ate away the monopoly.

Why the Dutch lost tea

The Dutch bought tea through Batavia (it lost flavour in transit) and started direct trade only in 1729; the English took "the lion's share". From 1716 the EIC kept supercargoes at Canton.

Plassey 1757: more than a battle

A conspiracy as much as a fight: Clive's side bought over Mir Jafar, backed by Bengal's Seth bankers; a fake treaty fooled Amin Chand. Little real fighting. Officers expected rewards (Clive got about £234,000).

After Plassey only one European power could be a "country power" in India; the Dutch were crushed in 1759, the French at Wandiwash (1760).

1765: revenue changes everything

After Baksar (1764), Clive got the diwani of Bengal (the right to collect revenue). The Company became a government. But land brought big military and administrative costs.

The Company paradox

As a government it grew stronger (land, revenue, army); as a trading monopoly it grew weaker (private traders, agency houses). Parliament stepped in: Regulating Act 1773, Pitt's India Act 1784.

Tea makes one Asian system

British tea demand grew; the Commutation Act (1784) cut the tea tax and beat smuggling: London tea imports rose to 23 million lb by 1800. India sent opium and cotton east; China sent tea and silk west.

Canton officials sold bills on London to country traders for their silver, so the Company needed less silver from Britain.

3. Bowen: follow the silver (1760–1833) Must know

Silver moved to, around and from Asia.

The debate

Irfan Habib: British silver to India stopped after 1760. Rajat Datta: it went on and rose after 1785. Bowen rebuilds the records and shows Datta was right (with corrections).

1760–84: almost none to India

After conquering Bengal, the Company used Indian revenue and private fortunes (paid in India for bills on London) instead. Silver even went from India to Canton for tea.

1785–1820: silver flows again

Out of £16.12 m: India £8.50 m, China £7.62 m. Why? Tea (after 1784), raw cotton for Canton, and above all war (Wellesley's wars, Tipu) and debt relief (after the 1817–18 Maratha war).

In 1790 China-bound silver was taken off ships at Madras to pay for the war against Tipu Sultan.

Silver moves around Asia

Private traders and officers also carried silver. About £2 m moved from Canton to India in 1792–1809; opium and cotton sales created a "golden current" back to India.

After 1800–1810: reverse flow

The Company, losing money (India trade monopoly ended 1813), began shipping bullion back to Britain (about £8 m net in 1811–33). Britain probably became a net importer of Indian silver.

So silver was…

…not just money for goods, but a flexible resource for commerce, war, treasury needs and debt.

Q. How did commerce and finance contribute to the making of British power in Asia?

Intro: British power was not simply "trade then conquest"; it grew from commerce, private enterprise, war, revenue and finance working together (Furber; Bowen).

Commerce: country trade inserted Britain into intra-Asian circuits; private traders and agency houses grew inside and around the Company's monopoly. Tea demand, boosted by the 1784 Commutation Act, made China central, while India supplied opium and cotton.

From trade to territory: Plassey (1757) was a conspiracy with Bengal's bankers as much as a battle; the diwani (1765) gave land revenue, turning the Company into a government (the "Company paradox").

Finance: Bowen shows silver did not stop after 1760: in 1785–1820 more went to India (£8.5 m) than to China, largely to pay for war and debt; silver also moved from Canton to India and, after 1810, back to Britain.

But: the Company as a business declined, and private interests rather than a master plan drove much of the change.

Conclusion: commerce and finance were the engine of British power; empire was a process, and silver its flexible fuel.
