Silver, Tea and Opium II: Opium, Empire and the Global Economy

Carl A. Trocki, Opium, Empire and the Global Political Economy (1999), ch. 5 (via Lecture 19 slides; reading not in folder)
In one lineOpium was not a side business: grown in British India, sold through Southeast Asian ports and Chinese networks, and consumed in China, it paid for tea and funded colonial states. Trocki: "Without opium there would have been no empire" (a deliberately provocative claim).

Why study opium?

One commodity shows the structure of an empire. It linked production, shipping, taxes, finance and consumption into one system across India, Southeast Asia and China.

Trocki's thesis

Opium gave revenue, profit and leverage. Empire depended on controlling commodity circuits, not only on conquering land.

2. The chain: India → Southeast Asia → China Must know

India: the production zone

The EIC turned opium into a regulated tax commodity in Bengal and Bihar: peasants grew poppy on contracts with cash advances; Company factories (Patna) processed and packed it; it was auctioned at Calcutta.

The Patna opium factory: a crop turned into a standardised product the state could tax. "Free trade" outside, monopoly inside.

Shipping

Fast opium clippers (e.g. Red Rover, Water Witch, built at Calcutta) ran Calcutta → Malacca Strait → Singapore → Lintin (a receiving depot) → Canton.

Southeast Asia: the crucial middle

Ports like Singapore were key. Colonial governments sold opium farms: licences giving a monopoly to sell opium. An opium farm is a tax institution, not a farm. The state got steady revenue; the farmer took the risk.

Chinese kongsi

Farms were run by Chinese kongsi (shareholding partnerships) who organised labour, money and sales. Trocki: opium was "the trap" in which kongsi economies were captured by colonial capitalism.

China: not just "addiction"

Demand grew in urban, commercial, social settings and varied by class and place. Chinese consumers had agency too; they were not only passive victims.

Value along unequal links

Follow the commodity, not the nation: peasant → factory → Calcutta merchants → ships → Chinese markets. Value was created "across a chain of unequal relationships". The same chain was a money circuit (revenue, liquidity, empire).

3. The Opium Wars Must know

Qing China

Commissioner Lin Zexu destroyed opium (1839): a statement of sovereignty.
1839–42

Britain

Protected merchants by force to win access and good terms.

Treaty of Nanking, 1842

Five treaty ports opened; Hong Kong ceded; China paid an indemnity; China's tariffs limited; extraterritoriality (foreigners judged by their own consuls).

After

Britain said opium was "irrelevant" to the war. The Second Opium War (1857–60) and Treaty of Tianjin legalised opium. Asian traders grew stronger.

Key questions and paradox"Was this a war about drugs, or about who had the right to regulate trade?" · Free trade through monopoly: empire preached open markets abroad but ran monopolies at home. "The question is not whether capitalism was free or unfree, but who had the power to define the rules."

→

Trocki's view

Commodity circuits · fiscal extraction · maritime networks · Asian intermediaries · global political economy.

Strengths

Joins economic and imperial history; makes Southeast Asia central; explains why ports and tax institutions mattered. A connected history: follow people, goods and capital across borders.

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "Without opium there would have been no empire." Discuss with reference to Trocki.

Intro: Trocki's claim is deliberately provocative; it shifts the history of opium from drugs to political economy.

Why opium mattered: Britain's tea problem (little Chinese demand for British goods) was solved by Indian opium, which redirected silver. In Bengal and Bihar the Company ran contracts, factories (Patna) and Calcutta auctions, turning a crop into a fiscal commodity.

Southeast Asia: opium farms (licensed monopolies) in ports like Singapore, run by Chinese kongsi, gave colonial states steady revenue: "the trap" that captured kongsi economies.

Force and rules: the Opium War and Treaty of Nanking (1842) turned commercial power into imperial privilege (Hong Kong, treaty ports, extraterritoriality): free trade outside, monopoly inside.

But: Klimburg questions Trocki's evidence; empire also rested on land revenue (the diwani, S18), law (Roy, S17) and the Indian army (S21); Chinese consumers had agency.

Conclusion: opium was a keystone of empire's finances and networks, though not its only foundation.
