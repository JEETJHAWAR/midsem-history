1. The colonial state (ch. 10) Must know

1858: what changed and what didn't

Changed: Governor-General → Viceroy; a Secretary of State in London; army reorganised; stronger bureaucracy; new Indian collaborators. Same: India as an economic resource and a military resource.

"1858 is institutional reorganisation, not an end to colonial exploitation."

The army and "martial races"

The Indian Army fought for the empire abroad (Egypt, China, Burma…) and kept order at home. Recruitment favoured Sikhs, Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims, Pathans; Bengalis and Tamils were called "less martial".

Recruiting in Punjab brought irrigation investment there: a military label became economic policy and uneven development.

The "drain of wealth"

Naoroji and R.C. Dutt: home charges, pensions, interest, profits and remittances took India's investible surplus away. British view: some were fair payments. Better question: through what institutions was surplus moved?

Export farming: boom and risk

Cash crops → export markets → merchant credit → debt → exposed to world prices. The cotton boom of the American Civil War was followed by a crash. Integration ≠ security.

Railways: modern but unequal

They moved troops, British goods and raw materials, and paid guaranteed returns to British investors even when losing money. Ask: who designed, financed and benefited?

Ruling through collaboration

Princes, landlords, nominated members; small reforms (1861, 1882, 1892, 1909). Indians were subjects, not citizens. The census classified caste and religion → separate electorates (Morley–Minto, 1909). Did the state record identities or create them?

2. From elite to mass politics (ch. 13) Must know

Before Gandhi

Petitions, moderate politics, revolutionary violence (S22): no big concessions.
→

Gandhi

Ideas from South Africa and Hind Swaraj; mass mobilisation and disciplined non-cooperation.

Local struggles → national movement

Champaran 1917 (forced indigo), Kheda 1918 (revenue), Ahmedabad 1918 (mill workers). Pattern: local grievance → moral claim → mass action → national meaning.

1919: the turning point

Rowlatt Act → protest → Khilafat alliance → Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919), 379 dead officially. Repression deepened nationalism, and showed that mass movements are hard to control.

Religion and nationalism

Gandhi joined Indian nationalism with the Muslim Khilafat cause. Religion could be a language of unity or of majority vs minority politics; Bose & Jalal: it depended on how it was used politically.

Non-Cooperation (1920–22)

Boycotts, khadi and charkha, satyagraha; Congress became a mass party at Nagpur (1920). But peasants, workers and industrialists wanted different things; Congress avoided anti-landlord struggles.

Chauri Chaura (Feb 1922): 22 policemen killed → Gandhi stopped the movement. Who really controls a mass movement?

Then: Swaraj Party enters councils (1923) → labour and student radicalism → Simon Commission (1928) → Purna Swaraj (1929).

3. War and the crisis of empire (ch. 15) Must know

Bengal famine 1943–44

War spending (money supply Rs 3 bn → Rs 22 bn) caused inflation. 3.5–3.8 million died. Not simply "not enough food": poor groups lost the ability to buy food (loss of entitlements).

1942–46: authority collapses

Quit India (8 Aug 1942, "Do or die"); Subhas Bose's INA (beaten at Imphal and Kohima; Rani of Jhansi Regiment); INA trials (a Hindu, a Muslim, a Sikh) caused mass protest; the Navy mutiny (1946) showed the armed forces wavering.

Q. Was Indian independence produced by one nationalist movement, or by the cumulative crisis of colonial authority?

Intro: Bose and Jalal treat state formation, mass nationalism and wartime crisis as one connected process; independence came from their combination.

A state built on extraction: after 1858 the Crown reorganised rule but kept India as an economic and military resource (the drain debate, railways with guaranteed returns, "martial races"); collaboration and separate electorates (1909) governed subjects, not citizens.

Mass nationalism: Gandhi turned local struggles (Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad) into national movements; 1919 (Rowlatt, Jallianwala) was a turning point; Non-Cooperation built a mass Congress; Purna Swaraj (1929) set the goal.

But nationalism had limits: Chauri Chaura showed leaders could not fully control the masses, and religion and class divided the coalition.

War crisis: inflation and the Bengal famine (an entitlement failure), Quit India, the INA trials and the 1946 naval mutiny eroded authority from within.

Conclusion: nationalism was the main force, but independence came from a cumulative crisis of political, economic and military authority.
