SESSION18

Silver, Tea and Opium in the Imperial Expansion I: The Bullion and the Tea

Readings: Holden Furber, Rival Empires of Trade in the Orient, 1600–1800 (1976), ch. 3, "The Triumph of Tea: The Making of British Power", pp. 125–184 (R14) · H. V. Bowen, "Bullion for Trade, War, and Debt-Relief: British Movements of Silver to, around, and from Asia, 1760–1833", Modern Asian Studies 44:3 (2010), pp. 445–475 (OT9) · Lecture 18 PPT
Where this fits / why it matters Session 17 showed the companies dependent on Indian merchants. This session asks how commercial power became imperial power (Furber) and what silver flows tell us about imperial finance (Bowen). Both readings break the old, simple model of "bullion for goods" (Europe sends silver, Asia sends goods) by adding private actors, intra-Asian trade, war, territorial revenue and reverse flows. Keywords from the title slide: tea · country trade · private enterprise · territorial revenue · bullion flows.

18.1 Furber: the age of calm and the rise of tea (1713–44) Supporting

  • After the Peace of Utrecht (1713), three decades of calm in the eastern seas; Anglo-Dutch wars became "unthinkable". A long background trend: ever-rising China trade (tea, porcelain, chinoiserie).
  • The Dutch missed tea. They traded with China indirectly through Batavia (transshipment spoiled the flavour), opened direct trade only in 1729, and gave up by 1734. The English took "the lion's share".
  • First tea in England: 1664 (2 lb 2 oz for Charles II). Early trade through Amoy in Fukien, which is why Europe says "tea" (Fukienese) rather than "cha" (Cantonese).
  • 1716: the EIC's regular council of supercargoes at Canton, the system that lasted until 1833. Tea imports passed 100,000 lb by 1706; within 75 years, goods from Canton alone nearly equalled the value of all "India" goods.
  • The united EIC (merger completed 1709) was stable: Bank of England credit, parliamentary support, India stock second only to Bank stock. Ships 100 → 150 and tonnage 41,000 → 62,000 (1717–27); sales exceeded costs by about £30 m in 1713–43.
  • Calicoes: despite British laws against wearing Indian painted cloth, re-exports went to Poland, Russia, the Balkans and the Levant, and coarse cloth went to the slave trade. Calico imports rose from 500,000 to 980,000 pieces a year; tea sales from £116,000 (1712) to £348,000 (1744).
  • The centre of British activity shifted from the west coast to Bengal: Calcutta became a separate presidency; 1717 farman of Farrukhsiyar (John Surman's embassy) gave duty-free trade (dastaks) and zamindari rights over Calcutta villages, unknowingly "the basis on which its political power was later to be built".
  • Able Bengal nawabs (Murshid Quli Khan) and the Jagat Seth banking family (who controlled Bengal's coinage and blocked a Company mint) held the British in check.
  • Country captains in Calcutta's punch houses doubled (15–20 → 40–50, 1713–43); British country ships outnumbered French 2:1 and carried Malayan tin and Japanese copper once controlled by the Dutch.

18.2 Furber: country trade, monopoly and "country power" Must know

Country trade: Britain inside Asian networks

Europe trade = Europe–Asia commerce through the European companies. Country trade = intra-Asian commerce by European merchants, Company servants and private traders, linking India, Southeast Asia and China. British power grew partly by inserting itself into existing Asian circuits.

The paradox of monopoly: the Company's monopoly existed on paper, but its own servants traded privately, and country captains, supercargoes and merchants expanded networks. Private enterprise increasingly challenged the mercantilist monopoly system.

Commercial and political interests became mutually reinforcing.

Plassey (1757): more than a battlefield

  • The nawab Siraj-ud-daula; news of Anglo-French war (1757); Admiral Watson took French Chandernagore; the Afghan Ahmad Shah Abdali threatened from the west.
  • The Company's Select Committee resolved to depose the nawab (April 1757). Bengal's non-Muslim elites (Hindu, Parsi, Sikh), especially the Seth bankers, saw the British as "a means whereby they could gain more power".
  • Conspirators chose Mir Jafar. Amin Chand (Omichund) demanded 5% of the treasure, so a fake treaty was drawn up with Watson's signature forged, with Clive's connivance.
  • Battle, 23 June 1757: Clive's ~3,000 men vs the nawab's ~50,000. Rain silenced the nawab's guns; Major Kilpatrick's initiative; Mir Jafar's troops held back; the nawab fled on a camel. "Less fighting... than at any other 'decisive battle'."
  • Rewards: Clive about £234,000; councillors £12,000 each; a subaltern £3,000.
Why Plassey mattered (lecture + Furber)
  • It involved military force and political factionalism and conspiracy.
  • Indian bankers and political elites mattered.
  • Company officials and private interests expected rewards.
  • It sped the move from commercial presence to territorial power.

Furber: after Plassey the Europeans "could not actively intervene in Indian politics and yet remain mere traders". Several European powers could not all be "country powers"; one had to stop the others. The Dutch learned this in 1759 ("Dear Forde, fight 'em immediately"). The French were defeated at Wandiwash (1760) and Pondicherry (1761); Treaty of Paris 1763.

1765: revenue transforms the Company

  • Mir Jafar was replaced by Mir Kasim, who resisted Company servants' abuse of trade passes. At Baksar (Buxar), 1764, Hector Munro defeated Mir Kasim and the nawab of Awadh: Bengal's independence ended.
  • 1765: Clive obtained from the Mughal emperor Shah Alam the diwani of Bengal, the right to collect its revenue: "the beginning of British rule in India".
  • Territorial revenue could fund both commerce and politics; the Company increasingly acted as a governing institution. But territory brought huge military and administrative costs.
  • Westminster stepped in: Lord North's Regulating Act (1773) (governor-general, Supreme Court at Calcutta); Warren Hastings (governor from 1772) acted like the ruler of a "country power"; Pitt's India Act (1784) placed the Company under state control.
The Company paradox (lecture) Political power (territory, revenue, army, administration) grew while commercial monopoly (eroded by private traders, country trade, agency houses) weakened. "An imperial government could become stronger as the old trading monopoly weakened." Furber: by the 1790s the EIC "as a government" went "from strength to strength", but "as a company" it was "in eclipse". The era ended symbolically at Seringapatam (1799), where Tipu Sultan fell to Wellesley's forces.

18.3 Furber: tea, India and China as one Asian system Must know

  • Tea imports to London: 2.5 m lb (1760) → 9 m (1769–70) → down during the American War → the Commutation Act (1784), Pitt's cut in tea duty to beat smuggling → 9 m (1784–85), 14 m (1785–86), 23 m lb by 1800.
  • The more bills of exchange on London that Canton supercargoes sold to country traders (who had earned silver selling Indian goods in China), the less Company silver they needed: negligible in the 1750s → 1 million taels (£333,000) by 1781–82 → £500,000 a year by 1800.
  • Bengal opium exports in country trade: Rs 125,000 (1774–75) → over Rs 1 million (1786–87).
  • Net China-trade profits: £234,000 (mid-1770s) → £418,000 (1783–84) → £800,000–900,000 by 1800.
  • So: opium and cotton moved east; tea and silk moved west; India supplied goods and became the base for country trade.
Agency houses and private fortunes
  • Agency houses (growing after 1763) managed Company servants' savings at 9%+ interest, linking British power on land and at sea.
  • Servants sent fortunes home through French, Dutch and Danish companies and "clandestine" voyages under foreign flags; 100–150 such voyages in 1783–93 were "British in all but name". Hastings himself bought Dutch bills.
  • Wars were profitable: in the war with Tipu (1789) Madras borrowed at 8–12% from Indians, agency magnates and even church wardens.
  • 70 British country ships called at Cochin in one season (1791–92); Penang was founded in 1786 as a free "emporium".
  • Furber: the gains of Company servants were "a catalyst", and individual enterprise was "destroying the world of mercantilism and monopoly, making way for the world of free trade".

18.4 Bowen: follow the silver (1760–1833) Must know

Bowen's question and method He reconstructs British silver flows to, around and from Asia to test the belief that bullion exports simply stopped after the conquest of Bengal. Method: rebuilding fragmented evidence from Company invoice data (customs ledgers were partly destroyed by fire). Finding: exports were at times very large, especially after 1785, and were used for war and debt relief as well as trade; private flows mattered; and after 1810 silver began to flow back to Britain.

The debate he resolves

  • Before 1760: K.N. Chaudhuri showed treasure exports rising to about £750,000 a year in the 1750s (65–85% of Company exports).
  • Irfan Habib: silver to India stopped after 1760 (removing an inflationary force). Rajat Datta: it continued and rose after 1785. Bowen: Datta is right, but his figures need correction (the Company counted Mocha, Benkulen and Balambangan as "India").

Phase 1 (1760–84): almost nothing to India

  • Under £2 m in total, mostly to Canton; only two shipments to India (Madras 1773, 1776).
  • Why? Directors: more British goods, treaty payments by Indian rulers, and above all private fortunes paid into Indian treasuries in return for bills of exchange on London.
  • After 1765, silver was sent from India to Canton to buy tea, until Bengal was "drained of silver"; then the "country supply" of the Canton treasury (Indian goods sold in China plus private deposits) took over.

Phase 2 (1785–1820): exports resume

Lecture figures: more silver went to India than to China.
  • Trade: the 1784 Commutation Act raised tea demand → silver to China; silver to Bombay to buy raw cotton for Canton; lumps of fine silver (sycee) for Chinese duties.
  • War: by 1793 Calcutta's treasury had no silver; Wellesley's wars (1798–1805) forced big shipments (over £1 m to Bengal in 1802 and 1804). China-bound silver was seized en route: £70,000 at Madras in 1790 for the war against Tipu; £202,281 at Madras in 1805; 505,298 dollars at Penang in 1806.
  • Debt: after the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–18), bulk shipments in 1816–20 to reduce Indian debt (sinking fund).
  • Silver was only 18.6% of Company export value in 1760–1829 (peak 47.9% in 1785–89), as directors pushed British manufactures.
Private flows and circulation inside Asia
  • Private silver: ship commanders, crews, passengers, interlopers; diamond merchants; General Claude Martin of Lucknow paid for indigo in silver (70,000 dollars in 1795–96); Parsi shipbuilder Pestonji Bomanji. Private exports to India 1791–1806: about £2.67 m.
  • 1802–11: of India's silver imports, Britain supplied only 43.8%, other European countries 16% and America 40.2%.
  • Silver circulates within Asia: almost £2 m moved from Canton to India in 1792–1809; later the opium and cotton trades created a "golden current" from Canton to India (about £681,577 a year in 1810/11–1827/28).
After 1800: the direction reverses
  • After the Company lost its India trade monopoly (1813) and its textile imports fell, it paid its "home charges" partly with bullion shipped from Asia to London.
  • About 1.5 million dollars from China in 1811; an "extraordinary remittance" of £2 m ordered in 1823; banker Nathan Rothschild bought the bullion.
  • Net bullion received in London 1811–33: about £8.1 m. Britain probably became a net importer of silver from India: "a far cry from the one-way traffic in bullion for goods".
  • Private flows remain hard to measure. The shift reflected mounting Company financial pressure.
Bowen: silver moved to, around and from Asia; the "bullion for goods" model breaks down.

Exam corner

Professor's exit question: "How did commerce and finance contribute to the making of British power in Asia?" Plan: (1) country trade inserted Britain into Asian circuits (Furber); (2) Plassey and the diwani turned commercial presence into revenue (1757, 1765); (3) tea demand after the 1784 Commutation Act and Indian opium/cotton created one India–China–Britain system; (4) silver financed trade, war and debt, and flowed in many directions (Bowen); (5) but: the Company as a company declined, and private interests were the catalyst. Conclude: process, not event.

Other likely questions: What was "country trade" and why did it matter? Explain the "Company paradox". Does Bowen's evidence support Habib or Datta? Why did the Dutch lose the tea trade?

MCQ traps: Commutation Act = 1784 (tea duty cut); Plassey 1757, Baksar 1764, diwani 1765; Canton supercargo council 1716; Bowen's period is 1760–1833; 1785–1820 India received more silver than China.
