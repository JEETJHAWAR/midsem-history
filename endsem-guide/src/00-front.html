What the examiner wants (Lecture 16 guidance, still valid)

The professor's rules for answers
  • Read the question carefully; it tells you what answer it wants.
  • Match length to marks; sketch your points first.
  • Write a proper introduction and conclusion.
  • Short, clear paragraphs, not bullet points.
  • Examples of people, events, dates, scholars, methods and publications "will enhance your writing".
  • Marks depend on content, language and presentation. No fixed word count.
A reliable answer template (from the professor's two models)
  1. Thesis (1–2 sentences that answer the question directly).
  2. Point 1 with scholar + example + date.
  3. Point 2 with scholar + example + date.
  4. Point 3 (for essays) from a different region or period.
  5. "But": a critique or limit (the professor always includes one).
  6. Conclusion linked to a big course idea: connection, agency, continuity and change, "process not event".

The professor's model essay ended: history "advances not by total replacement, but by layered revision". Endsem answers score well when they show this kind of balance, for example "European power was real, but it was built on Asian networks".

The story of the endsem half: how the ten sessions connect

One paragraph to remember Session 11 (midsem) showed the Indian Ocean as a connected space. The endsem asks who and what made it connected: people (S12), things (S13), a religion (S14) and ships (S15). It then asks how Europeans entered and transformed this world: first as dependent traders in Surat (S17), then as a territorial power funded by revenue, silver and tea (S18), and through the opium circuit that tied India, Southeast Asia and China together (S19). Port cities (S20) show both continuity with Asian networks and the new colonial order in stone and street. Finally, the course turns to how that empire was governed, resisted and ended (S21), and how anti-colonial activists also moved across the world (S22).
