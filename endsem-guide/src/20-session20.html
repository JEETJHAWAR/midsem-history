Interconnected Port Cities in Colonial Asia: From the Gulf and Red Sea to Bombay, Madras and Calcutta

Readings: René J. Barendse, "Port Cities in the Gulf and the Red Sea During the Long Eighteenth Century (c. 1720–c. 1840): General Characteristics and Some Comparisons with Southeast Asia", in A. Where this fits / why it matters Port cities are where every endsem theme meets: merchants (S12), goods (S13), faith (S14), ships (S15), companies (S17), silver and opium (S18–19). Why port cities matter (lecture): they linked sea routes, inland markets and imperial states; they concentrated merchants, migrants, soldiers and labourers; they connected regional exchange to global commerce; and they reveal both Asian agency and colonial power. Two readings, two lenses: Barendse = trade networks and Asian autonomy in the Gulf and Red Sea; Kosambi & Brush = the urban form (morphology) of colonial Madras, Bombay and Calcutta.

20.1 Barendse: "the last stand of Asian autonomies" Must know

Barendse's central claim The "long eighteenth century" (c. 1720–1840) in the Arabian seas was what Anthony Reid calls the last stand of Asian autonomies: despite European colonialism, Asian trade remained equal or expanded. European power mattered but did not erase Asian commercial agency. The major rupture came later than colonial narratives suggest: the 1860s–70s.
Long 18th century Strong continuity in Asian shipping, trade and finance
Early 19th century Existing networks remain active across the Gulf, Red Sea and India
Mid-19th century Cotton boom (US Civil War), steam shipping and Suez accelerate change
Late 19th century British firms gain stronger structural advantage

Why the break came late

  • The great transformation: the 1860s cotton boom (financed by the same Bania merchants who had traded to Persia, the Red Sea and Africa; profits went into Bombay mills); steam (P&O; Arab and Indian shipowners could not compete; Raj contracts went to British firms); and Suez (1869), after which the Red Sea dominated and the Gulf became "a sleepy, placid backwater", a mare britannica.
  • Irfan Habib warns against studying a colonial economy "without perceiving colonialism"; but British residents in the Gulf overrated their own influence, so the European role is easily overvalued.
  • The 18th c. is now seen as an age of prosperity in the Middle East and India, not a "dark age" (Iran is the exception: the 1722 Safavid collapse and Nadir Shah's wars). In Bengal and Java, though, booming shipping came with falling living standards.
  • The critical events in the region were not European: the Safavid fall (1722), Oman's civil war (1720s–30s), Qajar rule, Wahhabi raids on Muscat (1820s, pushing the imams to Zanzibar), and Mehmed Ali's Egypt in the Hijaz and Yemen.
  • Rulers like Oman's imams and the Qajars played Europeans off against each other. Indian traders under the Union Jack were "easy hostages"; the British consuls' daily work was to intercede for them.

What port cities were like

  • Small: Basra 40–60,000 (the Gulf's largest); Muscat ~30,000; Jeddah ~40,000; Mocha (al-Mukka) ≤10,000; Kuwait 5–6,000; Aden only ~3,000 when Britain took it (1839), "a bunkering station in the middle of nowhere". Inland cities were far bigger (Isfahan ~200,000).
  • Reasons: food supply (Muscat's rice came from Kanara, later from British-controlled Bengal, so Oman had to stay friendly with Britain), epidemics, and heat (Bushire "the most detestable city I visited in all of Asia").
  • Some were shifting fairs tied to inland roads (Hudayda for the coffee fairs, Bandar Lingeh), but most had strong civic identity and ancient roots (Suhar, Aden, Bahrain). Hormuz (1620s) was not "blown away"; its people moved to Bandar Abbas.
  • Kuwait rose suddenly in the 1750s with a new caravan route to Aleppo and a sulphur mine (ballast).
Port cities as commercial nodes (lecture)
  • Ports mattered for distribution, information and credit as much as for size.
  • They linked hinterlands to oceanic markets and coordinated storage, pricing and re-export ("staple markets": one place sets prices for others; Gulf prices differed by only 4–5%).
  • Their importance lay in connections, not population: a node, not just a city.
  • Central rulers (Safavid, Ottoman) charged higher customs but protected roads; small sheikhdoms charged less. Merchants used strikes and bribed sheikhs against greedy officials.
  • Legal pluralism worked best in city-states where the ruler was a merchant (the al-Sabah of Kuwait).

Who made interconnection possible? People in motion

  • Indian merchants: Banias (most Gulf Banias were from Sind, not Gujarat), Bohras, Khatris, Marwaris, Parsis. They were the richest group in Mocha; temples were allowed in Iran but only private worship in Yemen.
  • Arab merchants, shipowners and coastal rulers (desert warlords turned shipowners); seafaring tribes like the al-Huwaila.
  • Iranian, Armenian, Jewish, Greek and other intermediaries (Greek firms had agents in both Bahrain and Leipzig).
  • Sailors, brokers, migrants, labourers, translators; Aden in 1830 was half Jewish; Mocha had Somali and Ethiopian merchants and slaves.
Infrastructure of connection: finance made trade work
  • Bills of exchange and interlocking banking lowered transaction costs: by the 1780s Pune firms drew bills directly on Istanbul (in the 16th c. this needed several stages: Pune → Cambay → Basra → Aleppo). By the 1730s the EIC ran its Mocha trade entirely on bills endorsable in Surat (Islamic bills and Indian hundis).
  • Insurance and credit made trade flexible: insuring cargo was rare in the 16th c. but common by the mid-18th.
  • Warehousing, road protection and harbour access shaped success.
  • Legal pluralism let merchants operate across communities.
What drove the Asian boom?
  • More farmland and rural demand (Egypt, Iraq, Iran's recovery); cheaper transport; a longer sailing season; lower interest rates and insurance.
  • China's economic expansion (demand for Gujarat cotton, Malabar pepper, Makassar trepang and opium; China was ~20% of Bombay's customs revenue by 1800).
  • New American demand, above all for slaves (Cuba, Brazil, USA): East African slave exports rose from ~3,000 a year to 20–30,000, emptying the hinterland; Zanzibar became a clove and nutmeg island using slave labour.
  • Mostly, middle-class demand: Red Sea trade quadrupled from the 17th to the 18th c. (Indian textiles, tobacco).
"Interconnection was not simply cosmopolitan exchange"

Lecture synthesis: "It also depended on unequal labour systems, coercion, and the violence of slavery." African slaves were preferred in the Gulf as supposedly "more resistant" to disease; seasonal workers rotated between highlands and ports.

Capital stayed in Asian hands: Chinese kongsi and the rise of Singapore ("as much a Chinese commercial enclave as a British one"); Zanzibar trade run by Bania families from Kutch Mandvi, Bhavnagar and Porbandar; Portuguese East Africa run by Kathiawar Banias and Goan firms.

Country trade and the "Anglo-Bania alliance"

  • Until the 1880s it makes sense to speak of an Anglo-Bania alliance and an "Anglo-Indian empire" in the Arabian seas.
  • By 1780 most Indian Ocean shipping was British-operated, but often financed and owned by Indian, Armenian and Arab consortia, insured by Indian underwriters, with Arab and Iranian freight (S18 country trade from the other side).
  • Later, Company officials' savings in agency houses (e.g. Forbes & Co., ~30 ships from Bombay) made British trade self-financing, remitted to London by bills: "the infamous 'drain'".
  • Why did British country trade rise? Lower customs, Royal Navy protection (cheaper credit), lower profit rates. The British campaign against the Qawasim ("pirates" of the Trucial Coast) mainly protected Bombay's Bania and Parsi ships.
  • The bulk of Gulf and Red Sea trade stayed local (about one long-distance ship to 20–30 short-haul ones); the dhow trade prospered into the 20th c.
Barendse's conclusion: beware both long continuities and sharp breaks K.N. Chaudhuri's Asia before Europe stops the Indian Ocean's history at 1757; that is wrong. Barendse agrees with Sugata Bose that in some ways the ocean became more of a unity under colonialism: mass Indian emigration (Mauritius as "little India", East Africa, Southeast Asia), Indian-manned garrisons and navy, and Gulf and East African agencies run from India until the 1880s. "The Indian Ocean became far more 'Indian'… than it ever was before." In the Gulf, the real "high tide of empire" after the 1870s was Ottoman, not British.

20.2 Kosambi & Brush: the colonial port-city model Must know

Their argument Madras, Bombay and Calcutta share a distinctive urban morphology (physical form) from their colonial past, not found in other Indian cities. 17th-c. factories inside existing Indian cities had little effect on them; but these three began as fortified factories at undeveloped village sites, fully controlled by the EIC, and grew into hybrid towns where Indian merchants, drawn by trade under English protection, lived segregated from Europeans. They became the bases for expansion inland (other colonial forms: cantonments, civil lines, hill stations).
Fort by the water, an open esplanade, segregated sectors, a CBD, and peripheral industry and military zones.

Three phases of colonial urban development

  1. Fortified factory town with "white" and "black" towns. The Indian town was walled (Madras) or ditched (Calcutta); in Bombay one wall enclosed both. The European town always lay south of the factory, perhaps for escape to ships.
  2. Expansion (mid/late 18th c.): stronger forts (ramparts, bastions, moats); esplanades cleared for gunfire ("defensive emptiness around power"); Europeans moved to suburbs 2–5 miles out (by horse, carriage and palanquin), copying suburbanisation in Britain. Fast in Madras and Calcutta, delayed in Bombay.
  3. CBD and industrial fringe (19th–early 20th c.): a Western-style central business district on the earliest English site; crowded Indian towns; an intermediate zone (Parsis, Asian immigrants, Eurasians); grand public buildings and railway stations on old esplanade land; coal-powered mills and slums on the fringe; troops moved to cantonments.
How empire became urban spaceForts concentrated administration, storage and security; esplanades created defensive emptiness; European elites moved to low-density suburbs; Indian districts stayed dense and commercially mixed. Space encoded hierarchy.

Conclusion (Kosambi & Brush): Madras and Calcutta grew in a radial-concentric way from their waterfront cores (westward and eastward respectively); Bombay's island site distorted the model. The social ecology of each city expressed the hierarchy of power and the segregation of rulers from the ruled.

Kosambi & Brush explain

Urban morphology and segregation · how forts, esplanades and CBDs evolved · how empire shaped land use and infrastructure · why the port city became a colonial landscape.
The main analytical tension Were colonial port cities mainly creations of empire, or older Asian commercial worlds reorganised under colonial rule? The readings suggest both continuity and restructuring: empire built on existing networks but reshaped urban space and power.
Big takeaway Networks made port cities function · Space made colonial power visible · Labour sustained trade and urban growth · Empire reordered rather than created everything. To understand colonial Asia, study both maritime networks and urban morphology.
Exam corner

Professor's discussion question: "Were port cities engines of empire, or sites where empire had to negotiate with older Asian worlds?" Plan: engines: forts, esplanades, CBDs, segregation, agency houses, Raj contracts (Kosambi & Brush). Negotiation: Asian capital, Bania partners, Indian finance of country trade, local rulers playing off Europeans (Barendse). Conclude: both; empire reordered rather than created.

Class activity (marked, 10 marks): seven groups took Rangoon, Colombo, Bombay, Aden, Hong Kong, Penang, Madras and answered: why the British chose the site; what it was before; one distinct feature; what let it flourish; who made it work; which networks it joined. Be ready to answer these for any port (e.g. Aden: taken 1839 as a coaling/bunkering station on the Suez route; Penang: 1786, Francis Light, free port; Hong Kong: ceded 1842 after the Opium War).

MCQ traps: "last stand of Asian autonomies" is Anthony Reid's phrase used by Barendse; long 18th c. = c. 1720–1840; Bombay's Native Town grew after the 1803 fire; Calcutta's CBD = Dalhousie Square; Madras's Black Town = Georgetown.
