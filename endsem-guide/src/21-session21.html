21.1 Chapter 10: the colonial state after 1857 Must know

Company Raj → Crown Raj (1858)

  • The Governor-General became the Viceroy.
  • A Secretary of State for India in London became the central authority.
  • The British Indian Army was reorganised; the colonial bureaucracy strengthened.
  • New mechanisms for cultivating Indian collaborators.
What stayed the same The fundamental imperial objective: (1) economic: India as a resource within the international capitalist economy; (2) strategic: India as a military resource for imperial defence and expansion. "1858 is institutional reorganisation, not an end to colonial exploitation."

The British Indian Army: an imperial institution

  1. Imperial: defended and expanded the empire abroad: Sudan, China, South Africa, Egypt, Afghanistan, Burma, Tibet, the Persian Gulf.
  2. Economic: shaped regional investment and recruitment (e.g. Punjab's irrigation canals).
  3. Domestic: maintained colonial order inside India.
"Martial races" and governance Recruitment favoured Sikhs, Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims and Pathans; Bengalis and Tamils were labelled "less martial"; regiments were deliberately mixed so no community dominated. Chain: military recruitment → state patronage → regional investment → uneven development. Discussion: how can a military classification become an economic policy?

India in the colonial world economy

India exported raw materials and farm goods (cotton, jute, tea, coffee, wheat, oilseeds) to Britain's industrial manufacturing, and manufactured goods came back as imports. Central question: was integration into the world economy the same as economic development? (Compare Roy in S17, who stresses gains from integration.)

The "drain of wealth"

Transfers included home charges, pensions, military spending, railway interest, debt interest, private remittances, merchant profits, shipping, banking and insurance.

  • Nationalist view (Dadabhai Naoroji, R.C. Dutt): these transfers were investible surplus lost to India.
  • British counter-view: some payments were legitimate returns on capital or services.
  • Reorganise the question: ask through what institutions and mechanisms surplus was transferred, not just whether wealth was "stolen".

Agriculture, markets and vulnerability

Cash crops (cotton, jute, indigo) → export markets → merchant credit → indebtedness → exposure to global price swings. The paradox: integration produced both boom and vulnerability: the cotton boom during the American Civil War then a sharp downturn; the jute boom in eastern India (1906–13); severe agrarian distress in the late 19th c. Lesson: integration did not necessarily mean economic security.

Railways: modernisation or colonial infrastructure?

They moved troops, distributed British goods, carried raw materials from hinterland to port, and paid guaranteed returns to British capital, even when unprofitable. The contradiction: technologically modern infrastructure could still serve an unequal economic structure. Ask: who designed it, who financed it, whose interests did it serve?

Governing through collaboration
  • The Raj relied on princes, landlords, local elites and nominated representatives.
  • Limited representation: 1861 Indian Councils Act; 1882 Ripon's local-government reforms; 1892 Indian Councils Act; 1909 Morley–Minto reforms.
  • Pre-colonial sovereignty was layered and shared; colonial rule imposed unitary sovereignty.
  • Colonial India held mostly subjects, not citizens. Core contradiction: participation expanded without full political sovereignty.
Census, community and colonial knowledge Caste, religion and community were classified → political patronage → separate representation → separate electorates (1909). The Morley–Minto reforms institutionalised separate electorates for Muslims and some other groups. Food for thought: did the colonial state merely record pre-existing identities, or also reorganise and politically institutionalise them?

21.2 Chapter 13: from elite politics to mass politics Must know

Before Gandhi (pre-1919)

Constitutional petitions · moderate politics · revolutionary violence · attempted armed insurrection (see S22) → none produced major political concessions.
→

Gandhi's method

Grounded in his South African experience and Hind Swaraj · mass mobilisation, not elite petitioning · disciplined non-cooperation · converted nationalism into mass politics.

From local struggles to all-India politics

Champaran 1917Peasants resisting compulsory indigo cultivation
Kheda 1918Resistance to revenue demands in distress
Ahmedabad 1918Labour dispute: textile workers vs industrialists

Method: local grievance → moral claim → mass mobilisation → national significance.

1919: the turning point

Rowlatt Act → mass protest → Khilafat alliance → violence and repression → Jallianwala Bagh, 13 April 1919 (379 deaths officially; over 1,200 reported injured). Colonial repression plus mass mobilisation deepened anti-colonial nationalism. Note: 1919 showed how hard mass mobilisation is to control once begun, which explains Gandhi's later decisions to suspend movements.

Gandhi, religion and nationalism

Gandhi linked territorial Indian nationalism with extra-territorial religious solidarity (the Khilafat cause for the Ottoman caliph).

Religion as vocabulary of solidarity

Could build anti-colonial unity under particular circumstances.
or

Religion as majoritarian politics

Religious identity used for majoritarian or minoritarian mobilisation.

Is religion necessarily incompatible with nationalism? Bose and Jalal: the outcome depended on how religious identity was politically articulated.

Non-Cooperation (1920–22): mass politics and its limits

  • Methods: boycott of colonial institutions; khadi and charkha as symbols; disciplined satyagraha; at Nagpur (1920) Congress was reshaped into a mass organisation.
  • A coalition of competing interests: peasants (rent and revenue relief), workers (wages, unions), industrialists (economic interests protected), all under Congress.
  • Central tension: mass mobilisation vs political control. Gandhi and Congress were often reluctant to let anti-landlord or anti-capitalist struggles become central.
Chauri Chaura and the limits of mass mobilisation 22 Feb 1922: peasant violence; a police station attacked; 22 policemen killed → Gandhi suspended Non-Cooperation. Consequences: widespread disappointment, Congress divisions, the Swaraj Party (C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru), and growing communal tension. Food for thought: who controls a mass movement, the leadership or the people who take part in it?

From Swaraj Party to Purna Swaraj

1923 Swaraj Party enters the legislative councils to obstruct from within
1920s Rising labour militancy, student activism, radical politics
1928 Simon Commission: unity against it, but disagreement over minority safeguards
1929 Congress adopts Purna Swaraj (complete independence)

21.3 Chapter 15: World War II and the crisis of empire Must know

The war economy

  • War spending: Rs 3.5 billion on defence, 1939–45; money supply rose from Rs 3 billion to Rs 22 billion.
  • Monetary expansion → shortages and inflation.
Bengal Famine, 1943–44: 3.5–3.8 million deaths Older explanation: "There was not enough food." Better explanation: "Some groups lost the economic ability to obtain food": purchasing power, prices, access, entitlement. Famine deaths tracked drastic falls in the exchange entitlements of vulnerable groups, not simply an aggregate food shortage.

1942–46: the crisis of colonial authority

1942Cripps Mission fails; Congress adopts Quit India (8 Aug 1942, "Do or die"); leaders arrested; decentralised resistance spreads.
1943–45Subhas Chandra Bose leads the INA: diverse, transnational, explicitly anti-British; defeated at Imphal and Kohima. The Rani of Jhansi Regiment (Dr Lakshmi Swaminathan), Singapore.
1945–46INA trials at the Red Fort (a Hindu, a Muslim and a Sikh defendant) spark mass protest; Congress defends the prisoners.
1946Royal Indian Navy mutiny: unrest inside the armed forces themselves.

Final question: was independence produced by one nationalist movement, or by the cumulative crisis of colonial political, economic and military authority? Lecture: "Mass nationalism created political power without eliminating social difference. The end of colonial rule cannot be reduced to a single movement."

Exam corner

Likely essay: "Was Indian independence the achievement of one nationalist movement or the result of a cumulative crisis of empire?" Plan: intro with the three questions; the colonial state (Crown Raj, army, drain, collaboration, separate electorates); Gandhi's mass politics and its limits (Champaran → Non-Cooperation → Chauri Chaura → Purna Swaraj); the war crisis (famine as entitlement failure, Quit India, INA, RIN mutiny); but social differences and communal politics persisted. Conclude: a cumulative crisis, with nationalism as the main but not the only force.

Short-answer candidates: "1858 was reorganisation, not an end to exploitation." · How did "martial races" become economic policy? · Did the census record or create identities? · Why was 1919 a turning point? · Why did Gandhi suspend Non-Cooperation? · Explain the Bengal famine using the idea of entitlements.

MCQ traps: Morley–Minto = 1909 (separate electorates); Jallianwala = 13 April 1919; Chauri Chaura = Feb 1922; Purna Swaraj = 1929; Quit India = 8 Aug 1942; INA defeated at Imphal–Kohima; famine deaths 3.5–3.8 million.
