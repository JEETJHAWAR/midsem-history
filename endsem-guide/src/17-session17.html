SESSION17

European Expansion in South Asia from the 17th Century: Trade, Merchants, Empire and Colonial Development

Readings: Ghulam A. Nadri, "The English and Dutch East India Companies and Indian Merchants in Surat in the Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries: Interdependence, Competition and Contestation", in A. Clulow & T. Mostert (eds.), The Dutch and English East India Companies (2018), ch. 5, pp. 125–149 (R13) · Tirthankar Roy, India in the World Economy (2012), ch. 7, "Colonialism and Development, 1860–1920", pp. 181–209 (RB6) · Lecture 17 PPT
Where this fits / why it matters Sessions 12–15 showed a mature, Asian-run Indian Ocean. Session 11 (Roy ch. 4) brought the companies in. This session asks how European expansion actually worked, in two stages: entry and exchange in 17th–18th-century Surat (Nadri), then the mechanisms of imperial transformation in 1860–1920 (Roy). Core argument: European expansion was gradual, not immediate domination; Indian merchants shaped early Company activity; colonial rule later reorganised markets through law and infrastructure; development under empire was real but uneven and coercive. "Read empire as a process, not a single event."

17.1 Surat before empire Must know

  • A major Mughal port (the "commercial hinge of western India", c. 1540–1750) linking inland production to overseas trade.
  • Connected to the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Southeast Asia and Europe; a hub for merchants, brokers, shippers and financiers.
  • Shows that South Asia was central before formal colonial rule.
  • Europeans entered an existing world: the English and Dutch joined a mature commercial system, depended on Mughal institutions and local political protection, and found Indian merchants already in control of credit, supply and market knowledge. European power at this stage was embedded, negotiated and limited.

17.2 Nadri: interdependence, competition and contestation in Surat Must know

Nadri's argument The EIC and the Dutch VOC depended heavily on Indian merchants as bankers, brokers, large suppliers and buyers. Merchants gained lucrative business plus social and political advantages. The relationship was one of interdependence and complementarity, but also competitive and at times sharply contested. Older views stressed European domination and Indian subordination; since the 1970s scholars (Das Gupta, Pearson, Om Prakash) have stressed partnership. Nadri adds a long timeline (into the 18th century) and the genealogy of broker families.

Setting up in Surat
  • EIC trade with Surat from the 1600s; VOC from the 1610s. Both wanted indigo and textiles.
  • Unlike the Portuguese (monopoly claims, the cartaz pass system, violent reprisals), the English and Dutch were pragmatic merchants.
  • EIC: silver for textiles, indigo, pepper. VOC: an intra-Asian trade bringing spices, Japanese gold, silver and copper, Java sugar.
  • Subordinate factories at Ahmadabad, Baroda, Broach, Cambay and Agra.
  • Mughal permission: Thomas Roe's embassy; Jahangir's farman (customs remission, no inland transit duties); Shahjahan's 1630s farmans to the Dutch. Mughal officials valued the companies as bringers of gold and silver and customs revenue.
Contestation and violence
  • Mughal officials traded to the Red Sea themselves, so they saw the companies as rivals: in 1619 Surat's governor barred EIC brokers from buying for Mocha and Aceh; nobles tried to monopolise indigo (1620s–30s).
  • Companies petitioned first and used naval power only when that failed. 1612: Henry Middleton plundered about 15 Surat ships; the VOC and EIC seized Red Sea ships in the 1620s.
  • Late 17th c.: European pirates near Aden → companies blamed and forced to provide convoys.
  • Broker Tapidas (1622) offered to win permission at Baroda, or to supply goods secretly.
Virji Vora: "the richest merchant of Surat"

Not a formal broker, but the principal buyer of Company imports: a monopsony (single buyer). He lent money (VOC 36,000 mahmudis; EIC Rs 30,000 in 1634), sold pepper (20,000 maunds, 1629) and bought coral, silver and gold. In 1634 the EIC called him "the sole monopolist of all European commodities". He forced the Company to carry Surat merchants' goods to Mocha, Jeddah, Gombroon and Basra.

Edward Knipe (1643): Vora bought Calicut pepper with cotton and opium and sold it to the EIC at double the price, yet "must not bee displeased in any case"; while he remained "so much our creditor, little or no profit" could be made. The Maratha sack of Surat (1663–64) hurt him, but he recovered.

Continuity and change across the 18th century

17th century: merchants were better placed, because the companies were short of funds and had to sell imports and buy exports quickly before ships sailed. Companies tried violence but could not tilt the balance.

18th century: a qualitative change. Companies split the broker's job: brokers (Dutch makelaars) sold imports; suppliers (leveranciers) provided exports; others handled local administration; two merchants jointly in each role. By 1740 the EIC downgraded "broker" to marfatia. Brokers became more dependent, though still essential.

Protection: the Dhanjishah case (1771)

Merchants worked with companies partly for protection (contractual, not just patronage): low customs at company ports, safety of person and wealth. In 1771 the Portuguese at Daman seized the ship of Dhanjishah Manjishah, a former EIC broker, for lacking a pass. The EIC said: he is our marfatia, our subject, and cannot go to a Portuguese court. Goa replied with its "ancient rights" and the treaty by which Bombay came to Britain as Catherine of Braganza's dowry. The EIC got Surat's nawab to seize Portuguese goods (Rs 48,808). This became a contest between sovereigns over passes and jurisdiction at sea.

17.3 Roy: colonialism and development, 1860–1920 Must know

Roy's argument Roy challenges a simple "drain and deindustrialisation" story and asks how empire actually changed India's economy. His starting point is market integration through institutional and technological change: railways, telegraphs, ports and legal uniformity linked markets. The colonial state was poor in revenue but strong in legislation, so its effects were mostly indirect: it created enabling conditions for private enterprise. Outcomes were uneven across regions and sectors.
The debate Roy enters
  • Hobson and Lenin: capitalism reached for the periphery to avoid falling profits at home.
  • Marxists (Paul Baran, Walter Rodney, André Gunder Frank): imperialism caused underdevelopment.
  • Indian orthodoxy (Bagchi, Irfan Habib, Aditya Mukherjee) is "fundamentally neo-Marxist": deindustrialisation, the drain, an exploitative class structure, and export crops "forced" on peasants.
  • Roy's reply: peasants responded to profit; measuring the drain is crude; and the real puzzle is that India forged ahead of the colonised tropics, with big factories, one of the largest rail and telegraph systems, and good banks, ports, universities and hospitals. "Far from deindustrializing, India industrialized." The so-called drain was "the price paid for the gains derived from market integration", mainly payments for services to trade, business, education and health.
A poor but legislating state
  • In the 1820s tax per head in the three presidencies was under one-tenth of England's and half or less of other colonies'. Land tax dominated.
  • A "night-watchman" state (Morris D. Morris) that legislated "to a degree unknown in Indian history".
  • It offered one umbrella of property and contract law, one official language, one leading currency, and uniform channels of knowledge.
  • A "failure" in direct development, but a "success" as a modern state creating the institutions that global capitalism needs.

Strategies of imperial law (Roy)

StrategyMeaningRelevance to India
AppropriationTaking land and labour directly (settler colonies, terra nullius, slavery)Less important; land and labour stayed private, free and Indian-owned
IncorporationRuling through communities and their own legal traditionsImportant early: religious codes as civil law. It failed because there were too many sects and customs, and litigation was slow and costly
StandardisationOne uniform set of rules, especially for contracts and commerceIncreasingly important later: the Contract Act 1872 (English/American models, no Indian custom); 1870–90 codification of trusts, property transfer, promissory notes, evidence and wills

Breach of contract was treated as a criminal offence in labour and indigo laws (1819, 1859, 1860); this caused scandal in the indigo disputes (Blue Mutiny, 1860) and with indentured workers bound for Assam. Lecture: these institutions "often strengthened capital more than labour".

Agriculture: uneven commercialisation

  • 1765 diwani; land gave over 80% of revenue. Cornwallis's Permanent Settlement (1793): zamindari rights to the old revenue elite; ryotwari (peasant owners) in the west, south and north.
  • Critics: ownership over user rights, absentee landlords, debt traps. Roy: low investment was mostly due to scarce water; canals worked only in the Ganges and Indus plains; less than 10% of land was irrigated; rain-fed yields up to four times lower.
  • At its peak only 10–15% of cropland grew non-food exports, in a few districts. Gains came only where land, clear rights, cheap water and railways coincided. "Uneven" is the key word.

Industrial growth under empire

  • Factory jobs: 100,000 (1860) → 2 million (1940), about 4% a year, concentrated in Bombay and Calcutta.
  • Bombay cotton mills: a Parsi merchant's mill of 1854; railways cut cotton transport from three months to one day; the 1860s cotton boom funded mills; 10 mills (1865) → 271 (1914), 260,000 workers. China was the main buyer of Bombay yarn until Japan took over.
  • Calcutta jute: George Acland's mill (1855); 5 mills (1869) → 64 (1913); a European enterprise for world packaging (capital from London), while cotton was Indian (personal capital).
  • Limits: regional concentration, reliance on imported machinery, persistent inequality.

Over 10 million artisans were still at work in 1900, so the nationalist claim that crafts "disappeared" is an overstatement.

Technology and knowledge transfer

  • Empire moved machinery, engineers, medicine and skills: the Botanic Garden, Fort William College, the Asiatic Society; Roxburgh, Prinsep, Piddington's "law of storms"; tea found in Assam by Robert Bruce (1839).
  • Learning happened in mills, railway workshops, arsenals, surveys and colleges. Europeans among mill supervisors: 100% → under 30% by 1925.
  • Costs of "buy British": old-fashioned mule spindles, no Japanese-style import substitution. "Access and dependence together."
Roy's conclusion Marxists are right that empire pursued market integration, but wrong to leap to a state-driven transfer of surplus that made India poor. Empire mattered through law, language (English as lingua franca) and knowledge. It let Mauritian planters reach Indian workers and Bombay mill-owners reach Lancashire foremen; "India's precocious industrialization cannot be explained otherwise". The cost was an explosion of contract disputes, and a deep contradiction: markets need freedom, while empire means subjection (exposed by the Great Depression).

Exam corner: the professor's discussion questions
  1. How did dependence become a route to later domination? (Entry → dependence → privileges → leverage; brokers downgraded to marfatia; protection created "subjects"; Surat castle 1759.)
  2. In what ways did Indian merchants shape European expansion? (Credit, supply, monopsony; Virji Vora's terms; brokers' politics.)
  3. Does Roy revise or reject the deindustrialisation thesis? (He revises it: some crafts declined, but industrialisation and artisanal reinvention happened.)
  4. Was colonial development modernisation, exploitation, or both? (Both: real gains through institutions, but uneven, coercive, contract laws favoring capital; the lecture links Shashi Tharoor's Oxford debate as the "exploitation" side.)

MCQ traps: cartaz = Portuguese pass; marfatia = the EIC's agent after c. 1740; Virji Vora was a Bania merchant-banker; the first lasting Bombay mill was 1854; jute = European capital, cotton = Indian capital; Contract Act 1872.
