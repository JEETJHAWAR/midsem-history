History Endsem: Model Answers

All the sample questions and answers in one place: the professor's own discussion questions from the Session 12–22 slides with answer plans, one full model answer for every session, and extra short-answer and essay plans.

How to use these answers

The professor's rules for answers
  • Read the question carefully; it tells you what answer it wants.
  • Match length to marks; sketch your points first.
  • Write a proper introduction and conclusion.
  • Short, clear paragraphs, not bullet points.
  • Examples of people, events, dates, scholars, methods and publications "will enhance your writing".
  • Marks depend on content, language and presentation. No fixed word count.
A reliable answer template (from the professor's two models)
  1. Thesis (1–2 sentences that answer the question directly).
  2. Point 1 with scholar + example + date.
  3. Point 2 with scholar + example + date.
  4. Point 3 (for essays) from a different region or period.
  5. "But": a critique or limit (the professor always includes one).
  6. Conclusion linked to a big course idea: connection, agency, continuity and change, "process not event".

The professor's model essay ended: history "advances not by total replacement, but by layered revision". Endsem answers score well when they show this kind of balance, for example "European power was real, but it was built on Asian networks".

What's inside
  1. Part A: the professor's own discussion questions from the endsem slides, with answer plans. She asked these in class, so they are the most likely exam questions.
  2. Part B: one full model answer for each session (12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22).
  3. Part C: ten more short-answer plans and four essay plans that mix sessions.

Don't memorise word for word. Learn the structure (thesis → points with scholar and example → "but" → conclusion) and the names, dates and examples.

PARTA

The Professor's Own Questions (from the slides)

Each lecture ended with discussion questions or "food for thought". These are the plans for answering them.

Session 12 · Mariners, Merchants and Monks in the Indian Ocean: From Connected Space to Connected Lives

Exam corner

Professor's question (Food for Thought): "Who connected the Indian Ocean more effectively before modern nation-states: rulers, merchants, mariners, or religious communities?" Strong answer: merchants and mariners made the routine connections (Geniza, merchant guilds, Qusman families), religious communities gave trust and moral ties, and rulers mattered mostly as patrons and protectors (Motupalli charter, Chola grants), rarely as naval powers (Chakravarti's mare liberum). Conclude that they worked together.

Other likely questions: (1) Why does Chakravarti call South Asia the centre of the Indian Ocean? (2) What do the Geniza letters tell us about merchant life? (3) How does al-Sharida challenge the land/sea divide? (4) Compare the merchant networks of c. 1100 and c. 1900: what continued and what changed?

Session 13 · Connecting Europe and Asia: Textiles, Spices and Perfumes across the Indian Ocean

Exam corner

Professor's discussion question: "Which commodity best explains Indian Ocean connectivity: textiles, spices, or perfumes? Compare portability, meaning and market reach." Plan: spices win on portability (value-to-weight, shelf life); perfumes on political meaning (diplomacy, Harun al-Rashid); textiles on market reach and social depth (Berenike to Java, everyday and elite, Ray). Best answer: textiles explain connectivity most fully because archaeology proves their spread and they reached ordinary consumers too, but conclude that the three worked as a bundle.

Other likely questions: How does archaeology confirm written sources on Indian textiles? Why does Ray say textiles were "not a mere luxury"? How were aromatics turned into political capital? "Europe entered an older system." Discuss.

Session 14 · Islam and the Indian Ocean World: From Maritime Mobility to Muslim Communities, Networks and Political Imaginaries

Exam corner

Class activity (likely essay angle): "You are the Zamorin of Calicut. The Portuguese want you to expel Muslim merchants and let them control maritime trade. What alliance would you build, and would your decision be religious, economic or political?" Answer: ally with Muslim merchants, sailors and the Kuññāli Marakkārs, since they are economically indispensable (Wade's mutual dependence) and supply ships. The decision is primarily political-economic (sovereignty and revenue), even though the 'ulamā' articulate it religiously (Kooria). Mention competing Hindu rulers such as Cochin, who sided with the Portuguese.

Other likely questions: "Islam does not travel alone." Discuss. Did Sufis spread Islam in the Indian Ocean before 1500? How does Lambourn's khutba network change our idea of political authority? Why did a ruler like Malacca's convert? How did refugees spread Islam?

Session 15 · Shipping, Navigation and Maritime Mobility: Technology and Power in the Indian Ocean World

Exam corner: the professor's five discussion questions
  1. Compare the disappearance of the Southeast Asian jong (16th c.) with the decline of Indian Ocean sail after 1869. What differs about the mechanism? (Coercion vs competition.)
  2. Burningham shows Gujarati and Parsi shipwrights were in some ways more skilled than Europeans into the 18th c. How does this complicate the narrative of Western technological ascendancy? (Wadia yard, Trincomalee, vadhera, Portuguese praise.)
  3. What does the persistence of dhows in the "unorganised sector" tell us about the limits of the steam revolution? (Dual economy; cheap, nimble cabotage.)
  4. How did the submarine telegraph work as a technology of political, not just commercial, control? (London centralisation beyond the 1858 reforms; price homogenisation.)
  5. Using Burningham's account of sewn-plank and lashed-lug engineering, challenge the assumption that pre-steam shipping was primitive. (Monocoque, sternpost rudder before 1180, jong vs cannon.)

Session 17 · European Expansion in South Asia from the 17th Century: Trade, Merchants, Empire and Colonial Development

Exam corner: the professor's discussion questions
  1. How did dependence become a route to later domination? (Entry → dependence → privileges → leverage; brokers downgraded to marfatia; protection created "subjects"; Surat castle 1759.)
  2. In what ways did Indian merchants shape European expansion? (Credit, supply, monopsony; Virji Vora's terms; brokers' politics.)
  3. Does Roy revise or reject the deindustrialisation thesis? (He revises it: some crafts declined, but industrialisation and artisanal reinvention happened.)
  4. Was colonial development modernisation, exploitation, or both? (Both: real gains through institutions, but uneven, coercive, contract laws favoring capital; the lecture links Shashi Tharoor's Oxford debate as the "exploitation" side.)

Session 18 · Silver, Tea and Opium in the Imperial Expansion I: The Bullion and the Tea

Exam corner

Professor's exit question: "How did commerce and finance contribute to the making of British power in Asia?" Plan: (1) country trade inserted Britain into Asian circuits (Furber); (2) Plassey and the diwani turned commercial presence into revenue (1757, 1765); (3) tea demand after the 1784 Commutation Act and Indian opium/cotton created one India–China–Britain system; (4) silver financed trade, war and debt, and flowed in many directions (Bowen); (5) but: the Company as a company declined, and private interests were the catalyst. Conclude: process, not event.

Other likely questions: What was "country trade" and why did it matter? Explain the "Company paradox". Does Bowen's evidence support Habib or Datta? Why did the Dutch lose the tea trade?

Session 19 · Silver, Tea and Opium in the Imperial Expansion II: Opium, Empire and the Global Political Economy

Exam corner

Likely essay: "'Without opium there would have been no empire.' Discuss." Plan: thesis (provocative but illuminating); Britain's tea/silver problem and the opium solution; India as the production zone (Bengal/Bihar factories, auctions); Southeast Asia's opium farms and kongsi (Singapore); the Opium Wars and Nanking (1842); the "free trade through monopoly" paradox; but Klimburg's critique, and empire also rested on land revenue (S18 diwani), law (S17 Roy) and the army (S21). Conclude: opium was a keystone of imperial political economy, not its only foundation.

Professor's questions: "Who controlled the commodity chain, and at which stages?" "Was this a war about drugs, or about who had the right to regulate trade?" "Chinese consumers: victims or agents?"

Session 20 · Interconnected Port Cities in Colonial Asia: From the Gulf and Red Sea to Bombay, Madras and Calcutta

Exam corner

Professor's discussion question: "Were port cities engines of empire, or sites where empire had to negotiate with older Asian worlds?" Plan: engines: forts, esplanades, CBDs, segregation, agency houses, Raj contracts (Kosambi & Brush). Negotiation: Asian capital, Bania partners, Indian finance of country trade, local rulers playing off Europeans (Barendse). Conclude: both; empire reordered rather than created.

Class activity (marked, 10 marks): seven groups took Rangoon, Colombo, Bombay, Aden, Hong Kong, Penang, Madras and answered: why the British chose the site; what it was before; one distinct feature; what let it flourish; who made it work; which networks it joined. Be ready to answer these for any port (e.g. Aden: taken 1839 as a coaling/bunkering station on the Suez route; Penang: 1786, Francis Light, free port; Hong Kong: ceded 1842 after the Opium War).

Session 21 · The Rise and Growth of Indian Nationalism: Modern South Asia, 1858–1946

Exam corner

Likely essay: "Was Indian independence the achievement of one nationalist movement or the result of a cumulative crisis of empire?" Plan: intro with the three questions; the colonial state (Crown Raj, army, drain, collaboration, separate electorates); Gandhi's mass politics and its limits (Champaran → Non-Cooperation → Chauri Chaura → Purna Swaraj); the war crisis (famine as entitlement failure, Quit India, INA, RIN mutiny); but social differences and communal politics persisted. Conclude: a cumulative crisis, with nationalism as the main but not the only force.

Short-answer candidates: "1858 was reorganisation, not an end to exploitation." · How did "martial races" become economic policy? · Did the census record or create identities? · Why was 1919 a turning point? · Why did Gandhi suspend Non-Cooperation? · Explain the Bengal famine using the idea of entitlements.

Exam corner

PARTB

One Model Answer for Every Session

Short answers are 10 marks; essays are 15 marks. Each answer follows the same shape: thesis → points with examples → "but" → conclusion.

Session 12 · Mariners, Merchants and Monks in the Indian Ocean

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Who connected the Indian Ocean more effectively before modern nation-states: rulers, merchants, mariners, or religious communities?

The Indian Ocean was connected less by geography than by the people who moved across it. Reading Chakravarti and al-Sharida together shows that merchants and mariners made the daily connections, religious communities gave them trust, and rulers mostly protected them.

Merchants and mariners built the routine links. The Cairo Geniza letters show Abraham Ben Yiju living in Mangalore (1132–49), trading pepper and iron with partners in Aden, while Indian shipowners such as Fatanswami carried the cargo. Centuries later, Qusman families from Najd linked the Arabian desert to Bombay and Calcutta through agents and letters (al-Sharida).

Religious communities supplied moral ties: the Chola kings funded a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam, and at Somnath in 1264 Hindu friends helped a Muslim shipowner build a mosque. Rulers acted mainly as patrons; the Motupalli charter (1245) protected merchants from pirates. But rulers seldom kept navies, so the sea stayed open (mare liberum), with the 1025 Chola raid as an exception.

Thus no single group connected the ocean. Merchants and mariners did most of the work, but it was the combination of trade, faith and protection that made the Indian Ocean a connected world.

Session 13 · Textiles, Spices and Perfumes across the Indian Ocean

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Which commodity best explains Indian Ocean connectivity: textiles, spices or perfumes?

All three linked Europe and Asia, but each did so differently. Comparing their portability, meaning and market reach shows that textiles explain connectivity most fully, while spices and perfumes add value and political meaning.

Spices were the most portable: high value for their weight and a long shelf life, so they suited long voyages. Khatun shows Arab merchants joining existing Asian markets through cabotage to obtain them. Perfumes carried the most political meaning: musk, camphor and ambergris appeared in tribute and court ritual, as at Harun al-Rashid's wedding, where perfumes in glass vessels displayed the Caliphate's wealth.

Textiles had the widest reach. H.P. Ray shows that Indian cotton reached Berenike on the Red Sea (Z-spun cotton), Fustat, Java and Sulawesi, served both everyday and elite buyers, and was organised by weavers' guilds and merchant bodies. Cloth also carried identity and ritual meaning. But Ray warns that value varied by region, so we should not generalise.

Textiles therefore best explain the depth of connection, though the three goods worked together. Their story also shows that Europe entered an older Asian system of exchange rather than creating it.

Session 14 · Islam and the Indian Ocean World

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "Islam became an Indian Ocean religion not simply because Muslims travelled, but because mobility created communities that local societies reshaped." Discuss.

Intro: Islam "does not travel alone"; the better question is who carried it and how communities became embedded (Wade; Kooria).

Settlement without states: monsoon stays turned visitors into residents. At Saymur (Chaul) 10,000 Muslims lived under a Hindu king with their own judge; at Calicut the Zamorin and Muslim merchants depended on each other.

Networks: Lambourn's khutba network tied Indian mosques to the Rasulids of Yemen without territorial rule; the Kazaruni Sufis had lodges from Cambay to Quanzhou; Cambay gravestones reached Pasai and Gresik.

Local remaking: Malacca's ruler converted through marriage to Pasai; Quanzhou's refugees of the 1360s carried Islam to Java; Kooria's ribāṭ at Ponnāni joined the Zamorin, 'ulamā', Marakkār navy and Mappila traders against the Portuguese.

But: Sufi roles before 1500 should not be assumed (Wade), and conversion was slow and elite-led (Eaton, Feener).

Conclusion: Islam was made and remade locally; mobility created the communities, and local societies gave them their shape.

Session 15 · Shipping, Navigation and Technology: Power in the Indian Ocean

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Compare the disappearance of the Southeast Asian jong in the 16th century with the decline of Indian Ocean sail after 1869. What differs about the mechanism?

Both cases ended with European shipping on top, but they happened in very different ways: the first through military coercion, the second through technological and economic competition.

When the Portuguese met the Javanese jong in 1512, it was bigger than their own ships and its four layers of planking resisted their cannon (Burningham). The jong was not beaten by better design. Sustained Portuguese attacks on trade routes made large jongs too risky, and by the late 16th century smaller vessels replaced them. Here dominance came from force.

After 1869 the mechanism was competition. Steam, iron hulls, new charts and the telegraph (from 1870) made transport cheaper, faster and centrally controlled, and the Suez Canal finished off timber sailing ships on the main routes (S. Bhattacharya). Asian shipyards lost their role, creating dependency.

But neither change was total: dhows and katias survived in the "unorganised sector" well into the 20th century. The comparison shows that technology is inseparable from power, and that European dominance began with coercion long before it had a real technical edge.

Session 17 · European Expansion in South Asia: Trade, Merchants, Empire

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. How did dependence become a route to later domination in European expansion in South Asia?

European expansion began from weakness, not strength. In Surat the English and Dutch companies depended on Indian merchants, but repeated bargaining slowly turned this dependence into leverage and then political power.

Nadri shows that in the 17th century the EIC and VOC needed Indian credit, supplies and brokers. Virji Vora lent them money and bought nearly all their imports, so he could dictate prices; an EIC officer wrote in 1643 that he "must not be displeased in any case". Broker families such as the Parekhs managed Company trade for generations.

In the 18th century the balance shifted. The companies split the broker's role and renamed him a marfatia, and merchants increasingly relied on Company protection: in 1771 the EIC treated its broker Dhanjishah as its own subject against the Portuguese. A Parekh agent even helped the English take Surat castle in 1759. By 1860–1920, Roy shows, empire was reshaping markets through law and infrastructure.

But this was not a smooth plan: Mughal power and Indian merchants limited the companies for a long time. The lesson is that European power was negotiated before it was institutionalised; empire was a process, not a single event.

Session 18 · Silver, Tea and Opium I: The Bullion and the Tea

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. How did commerce and finance contribute to the making of British power in Asia?

Intro: British power was not simply "trade then conquest"; it grew from commerce, private enterprise, war, revenue and finance working together (Furber; Bowen).

Commerce: country trade inserted Britain into intra-Asian circuits; private traders and agency houses grew inside and around the Company's monopoly. Tea demand, boosted by the 1784 Commutation Act, made China central, while India supplied opium and cotton.

From trade to territory: Plassey (1757) was a conspiracy with Bengal's bankers as much as a battle; the diwani (1765) gave land revenue, turning the Company into a government (the "Company paradox").

Finance: Bowen shows silver did not stop after 1760: in 1785–1820 more went to India (£8.5 m) than to China, largely to pay for war and debt; silver also moved from Canton to India and, after 1810, back to Britain.

But: the Company as a business declined, and private interests rather than a master plan drove much of the change.

Conclusion: commerce and finance were the engine of British power; empire was a process, and silver its flexible fuel.

Session 19 · Silver, Tea and Opium II: Opium, Empire and the Global Economy

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "Without opium there would have been no empire." Discuss with reference to Trocki.

Intro: Trocki's claim is deliberately provocative; it shifts the history of opium from drugs to political economy.

Why opium mattered: Britain's tea problem (little Chinese demand for British goods) was solved by Indian opium, which redirected silver. In Bengal and Bihar the Company ran contracts, factories (Patna) and Calcutta auctions, turning a crop into a fiscal commodity.

Southeast Asia: opium farms (licensed monopolies) in ports like Singapore, run by Chinese kongsi, gave colonial states steady revenue: "the trap" that captured kongsi economies.

Force and rules: the Opium War and Treaty of Nanking (1842) turned commercial power into imperial privilege (Hong Kong, treaty ports, extraterritoriality): free trade outside, monopoly inside.

But: Klimburg questions Trocki's evidence; empire also rested on land revenue (the diwani, S18), law (Roy, S17) and the Indian army (S21); Chinese consumers had agency.

Conclusion: opium was a keystone of empire's finances and networks, though not its only foundation.

Session 20 · Interconnected Port Cities in Colonial Asia

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Were colonial port cities engines of empire, or sites where empire had to negotiate with older Asian worlds?

Colonial port cities were both. They displayed and organised imperial power, but they ran on Asian networks that empire had to work with rather than replace.

As engines of empire, Kosambi and Brush show that Madras, Bombay and Calcutta were built around a fort, an open esplanade and segregated "white" and "black" towns, later adding a European-style business district, mills and cantonments. In Calcutta, Fort William and the Maidan formed a monumental core, while Indians crowded into Burra Bazaar. Space encoded hierarchy.

As sites of negotiation, Barendse shows that in the Gulf and Red Sea Asian trade stayed strong until the 1860s. British-run ships were often financed by Indian and Armenian partners, Bania merchants were indispensable, and local rulers such as Oman's imams played Europeans off against each other. This "Anglo-Bania alliance" lasted until the 1880s.

But these networks also rested on slavery and coerced labour, and after steam and Suez British firms gained the upper hand. Port cities were therefore older Asian commercial worlds that empire reorganised in space and power, rather than pure creations of empire.

Session 21 · Modern South Asia, 1858–1946: Colonial State, Mass Nationalism, Imperial Crisis

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. Was Indian independence produced by one nationalist movement, or by the cumulative crisis of colonial authority?

Intro: Bose and Jalal treat state formation, mass nationalism and wartime crisis as one connected process; independence came from their combination.

A state built on extraction: after 1858 the Crown reorganised rule but kept India as an economic and military resource (the drain debate, railways with guaranteed returns, "martial races"); collaboration and separate electorates (1909) governed subjects, not citizens.

Mass nationalism: Gandhi turned local struggles (Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad) into national movements; 1919 (Rowlatt, Jallianwala) was a turning point; Non-Cooperation built a mass Congress; Purna Swaraj (1929) set the goal.

But nationalism had limits: Chauri Chaura showed leaders could not fully control the masses, and religion and class divided the coalition.

War crisis: inflation and the Bengal famine (an entitlement failure), Quit India, the INA trials and the 1946 naval mutiny eroded authority from within.

Conclusion: nationalism was the main force, but independence came from a cumulative crisis of political, economic and military authority.

Session 22 · Indian Revolutionaries Abroad: Connectivity, Silence and Memory

Write it like this (short answer, 10 marks)
Q. Why does Aspengren argue that focusing only on connectivity "risks romanticising exile"?

Recent global histories celebrate Indian revolutionaries abroad as builders of worldwide networks. Aspengren argues that this picture is partly the historian's own construction, made with hindsight, and that it hides the long silences, fears and failures of exile.

His case is two exiles in Sweden. Virendranath Chattopadhyaya arrived in Stockholm in May 1917 for a peace conference that never met; with German money gone and police urging him to stay out of politics, he fell silent and turned to translation and trade. Har Dayal, a founder of the Ghadar Party, wrote desperate letters in 1918, then called revolutionary propaganda "foolish and useless" and turned to adult education. Following Edward Said, exile was restricting and painful, not only creative.

But the revolutionaries still mattered: their plots provoked repression such as the Rowlatt Acts, which sparked Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha. Most of what we know also comes from surveillance files, so even silence reaches us through the empire's eyes.

Aspengren therefore asks for a humane history that includes despair as well as connection, a reminder that concepts shape what historians see and that writing history is an ethical act.

PARTC

More Answer Plans

Ten more short answers and four essays that mix several sessions. Write each one as paragraphs, not bullet points.

Short-answer plans (10 marks)

1. Why does Chakravarti place South Asia at the centre of Indian Ocean history? Thesis: geography and evidence, not Indocentrism. Points: enclosed ocean; no single-season voyage, so the coasts became stopovers and transshipment points; two seaboards plus deltas; Geniza and Chola evidence. But: he warns against replacing Eurocentrism with Indocentrism. Conclude: "a hinge, not an edge".
2. What do the Cairo Geniza letters reveal about Indian Ocean trade? Trust and information ("one who is present can see…"); goods (pepper, iron, cinnamon, cloth) and prices; risk (shipwreck at Bab-el-Mandeb); cross-cultural life (Ben Yiju's marriage, Tinbu's friendship). But: chance survival, a Jewish merchant viewpoint.
3. How did textiles connect the Indian Ocean world? (Ray) Deep cotton history; only some cloth travelled; Berenike Z-spun evidence, Fustat, Newberry (Gujarat); guilds and charters; Java gift rituals; cloth as writing material. But: values differed by place; avoid generalising.
4. "Islam does not travel alone." Discuss. (Wade/lecture) Bundle of people, trade, law, language; carriers (merchants, Sufis, refugees); khutba networks; Calicut under the Zamorin; conversion of Malacca's ruler by marriage. But: Sufi role before 1500 unproven. Conclude: Islam was "made and localised".
5. Compare the two mechanisms of European maritime dominance. 16th-c. coercion (jong destroyed by disruption, not inferiority) vs 19th-c. competition (steam, iron, telegraph, Suez). Evidence: Burningham, Bhattacharya. But: dhows survived. Conclude: technology is politics.
6. How did Indian merchants shape European expansion in Surat? (Nadri) Credit and supply; Virji Vora's monopsony; broker dynasties (Parekhs, Kishandas, Parsis); 18th-c. change (split roles, marfatia, protection). But: companies used violence and later gained leverage. Conclude: interdependence then hierarchy.
7. Why did silver keep flowing to Asia after 1760? (Bowen) Tea after 1784; cotton for Canton; war (Wellesley, Tipu); debt relief; private flows; then reverse flows after 1810. Habib vs Datta. Conclude: silver became a flexible imperial resource.
8. What was an opium farm, and why does it matter to Trocki? Licence/monopoly; revenue upward, risk downward; Chinese kongsi; Singapore. Shows empire as fiscal circuits; "free trade through monopoly". But: Klimburg's critique.
9. Describe the morphology of colonial port cities. (Kosambi & Brush) Fort, esplanade, white/black towns, CBD, intermediate zone, industrial and military fringes; three phases; Madras, Bombay, Calcutta differences. But: built on Asian commerce (Barendse).
10. Why does Aspengren say focusing on connectivity can romanticise exile? Retrospective construction; Chattopadhyaya's lost platform; Har Dayal's letters and turn to adult education; Said; surveillance sources. But: exiles shaped the climate (Rowlatt). Conclude: history as an ethical act.

Essay plans (15 marks)

Essay 1. "The Indian Ocean was connected by human mobility, not geography alone." Discuss with reference to merchants, mariners and religious communities. Intro: monsoon as condition, people as agents. Body: mariners (Socotra visitors, sewn ships, al-Sirafi); merchants (Geniza, ainnurruvar, Qusman families); religious communities (Chudamani vihara, Somnath mosque, khutba and Kazaruni networks); rulers as patrons (Motupalli). But: geography still set the rhythm; connection could be unequal (slaves, coercion). Conclude: geography made connection possible; people made it real, continuing from 500 to 1950 in changing forms.
Essay 2. "European expansion in South Asia was negotiated before it was institutionalised." Discuss. Intro: process not event. Negotiated: Surat interdependence, Mughal farmans, Virji Vora, brokers (Nadri); Plassey as conspiracy with Indian bankers (Furber). Institutionalised: diwani, Regulating Act, law and standardisation (Roy), forts and esplanades (Kosambi & Brush), Crown Raj (S21). But: violence existed early (Middleton 1612), and Asian autonomy lasted in the Gulf (Barendse). Conclude with the lecture's line "trade, law, technology and local agency all belong in the same story".
Essay 3. How did commodities (silver, tea, opium) make British power in Asia? Tea demand and the Commutation Act; country trade and bills on London (Furber); silver for trade, war and debt, then reverse flows (Bowen); the opium circuit and opium farms, the Opium Wars, "free trade through monopoly" (Trocki); port cities as nodes (S20). But: territorial revenue and the army also mattered; Klimburg's critique. Conclude: empire as commodity circuits plus territory.
Essay 4. Was colonial rule in India "modernisation, exploitation, or both"? Roy: market integration, industrialisation, artisan reinvention, knowledge transfer. Bose & Jalal: drain, railways guaranteeing British returns, vulnerability of export agriculture, famine as entitlement failure, unitary sovereignty without citizenship. Bhattacharya: peripheralisation through steam. Conclude: both, unevenly; ask whose interests institutions served.
