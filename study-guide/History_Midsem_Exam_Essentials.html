SESSION1

Why Study History?

E.J. Hobsbawm, "The Social Function of the Past: Some Questions", Past & Present (1972) · Hobsbawm, On History (1997), ch. 3 (covered through the PPT)
In one lineEvery society uses its past. The real question is how: as a pattern to copy, a model to choose from, or a process that explains how we got here. The past is often selected, reshaped or even invented for present needs.

Who is Hobsbawm? A famous British Marxist historian who wrote on class, nationalism, capitalism, revolution and empire. His 1972 essay started as a conference paper in 1970. It mostly raises questions rather than giving final answers ("It is easier to formulate questions than answers").

1. The core argument Must know

"The past is a permanent dimension of the human consciousness" In simple words: no person or society can escape its past.
  • Everyone is aware of a past because we all live with people older than us (parents, grandparents).
  • Every society has a past. Even brand-new colonies are made of people from old societies.
  • To belong to a community means placing yourself in relation to its past, even if you reject it.
  • So the question is not whether societies have a past, but how they use it: as authority, a moral guide, a political weapon, an identity, or a way to understand change.
  • The historian's job is to study this "sense of the past" and how it changes. The past is not just inherited; it is organised and used.

"We swim in the past as fish do in water, and cannot escape from it."

2. Picture it: three ways a society uses its past

The faster a society changes, the more it moves from left to right.

3. Past as PATTERN: traditional societies Must know

  • For most of history, the past was the pattern for the present. Each generation tried to copy the one before and felt it had failed if it did not.
  • Old customs, ancestors' authority, sacred texts and old law were treated as rules. History here is not a story of progress but a storehouse of precedents ("what was done before").
  • In societies without writing, law = custom, and old documents gain almost spiritual authority.
A village claims its common lands because "we have always used them", or even cites an old colonial court decision as proof. The past works like a charter (licence) for the present.

How does a traditional society change? (key point)

Traditional societies are NOT frozen Hobsbawm calls the idea that traditional society never changes "a myth of vulgar social science". Change does happen, but it is absorbed in ways that do not openly challenge tradition.

Four ways change sneaks in

  1. Gradually: so slowly that nobody notices.
  2. Hidden (concealed): done without being admitted.
  3. Renamed: "new wine in old bottles". Nomads who traded camels began trading lorries and saw it as the same old business.
  4. Stretching the framework: old myths and rituals are stretched to fit the new thing.

Three mechanisms for innovation without breaking tradition

  1. The official "past" is only a selection of everything remembered, so there are gaps (interstices) where new things can enter unnoticed.
  2. Technology is the most flexible area. Tradition-bound peasants happily took up horses or new weapons without feeling they were breaking with the past.
  3. Really big innovations are made acceptable by disguising them as a return to a forgotten past, or by a higher authority such as a religious revelation or a prophet.

Tradition is strong not because it blocks all change, but because it can absorb change while seeming to stay the same.

What if change is too fast?

This kind of tradition needs slow change. If change is too fast, tradition survives only by:

  • Isolation: the Amish in the USA cut themselves off.
  • Accepting one huge forced step at great human cost: the forced conversion of Mexican Indians to Catholicism after the Spanish conquest.

"Tradition" itself changes

What "has always been done" changes over time without people noticing.

What was "traditional" for Bulgarian peasants in 1850 was very different from earlier centuries, yet they still felt traditional.

Such a past fits ideas of cycles or decline, but not the idea of continuous progress.

4. Past as MODEL: when change speeds up Must know

  • When change becomes too fast, the past "must cease to be the pattern of the present, and can at best become the model for it".
  • Key difference: a pattern is copied automatically; a model is consciously chosen, selected and adapted.
  • The past becomes a "mask for innovation": calls to "go back" actually produce something new.
  • Big insight: when a society starts loudly demanding restoration, continuity has already broken. The past has moved from lived tradition to a conscious reference point.

Restoration, and the problems with Zapata (key point)

Hobsbawm is sceptical of the word "restoration", because you can never simply bring back the past. It always involves selection, rebuilding or reinventing.

Example: Zapata and the peasants of Morelos (Mexico)

The peasants led by Zapata wanted to restore their society to what it had been about 40 years earlier, before the rule of Porfirio Díaz took their lands. Why it could not work as a real restoration (3 problems):

  1. Memory problem: they had to reconstruct things nobody could remember exactly (e.g. the precise boundaries of the old common lands), so they ended up imagining "what ought to have been".
  2. It could not be cut out: the change they hated was not "a bullet lodged in the flesh" that could be surgically removed. It was part of a much wider change in society.
  3. Bigger effects than intended: the movement released forces far larger than its aim. The peasants became a revolutionary power, and the attempt to turn the clock back became a social revolution that helped create "a historically unprecedented new Mexico".

(Hobsbawm uses John Womack's biography of Zapata, 1969.) Lesson: trying to go back to the past can produce something completely new.

Effective restoration

Tries to bring back the actual old practices and institutions.Calls to restore "old-time religion" (everyone in church); 19th-century British radicals wanting to bring back small peasant farmers.
vs

Symbolic restoration

Brings back only the feeling, symbol or meaning, not the real old world (often because what people want back, like "greatness" or "freedom", is too vague).Rebuilding Warsaw's bombed old town after WWII; pulling down the Stalin monument in Prague; Churchill insisting the bombed Parliament be rebuilt exactly as before.

5. The invented past: artefact or "fabrication" Must know

  • If the past people appeal to is very remote, it becomes just a language for present wishes: an artefact or, "in less flattering terms, a fabrication".
  • Such a past is only weakly linked to real history but is politically useful: it can be arranged into a story that justifies the present. Even recent, made-up traditions can be very powerful.

Examples of remote / invented pasts

  • English radicals' "free Anglo-Saxons before the Norman Yoke" and "Merrie England".
  • "Charlemagne" used as a symbol for European unity since Napoleon, though nobody wants 9th-century Europe back.
  • The new country "Ghana" took the name of an old empire from a different place.
  • Zionism's "return" to pre-diaspora Israel in practice denied 2,000 years of actual Jewish history.

Nationalism: the clearest case

Renan: nations "get their history wrong". Their goals are new, but they describe them as historic (especially claims to territory), and they revive old things on purpose.

Welsh "druids"; Hebrew revived as a spoken language; the Nazis' medieval-style castles. Hobsbawm: "History is the most easily worked raw material" for making nations. (Indian nationalists' "golden age", S4.)

Contrast: in truly traditional societies, restoring a remote past is meant literally. Peruvian peasants (to the 1920s) rose up to restore the Inca Empire; for them the Incas were "yesterday".

6. Past as PROCESS: modern societies Must know

  • People reject the past only when innovation is seen as unavoidable and good, i.e. as "progress". In modern culture "new" and "revolutionary" (as in adverts) mean "better".
  • People accept technological change far more easily than social or moral change (family, sex, religion). "Has there ever been a serious Luddism against bicycles or transistor radios?" Popular religion accepted cheap printed holy pictures but fought changes to holy texts.
  • The past now works as a process: the story of development and evolution that explains how the present came to be. Bagehot's Physics and Politics (1872); modern "modernisation" theory.
  • Even conservatives start thinking historically ("even conservative thought becomes historicist").
  • Paradox: "the more change dominates social life, the more important history becomes" as a tool for understanding it.
The problem of the future Even people designing a totally new future look for examples in the past. Socialists pointed to "primitive communism" as a precedent. But history shows tendencies, not exact outcomes. Marx was sure socialism would replace capitalism but said almost nothing about what socialism would look like. So "History ceases to be of use at the very moment when we need it most." We then dip into the past for useful precedents. What happened to the poor displaced by 19th-century railways helps us think about 20th-century motorways; medieval "student power" helps us think about modern universities.

7. Genealogy: searching for ancestors Must know

Genealogy means more than family trees: groups place themselves in a line of ancestors to gain continuity, prestige, legitimacy and comfort for their insecurity. New nations, new elites and revolutionary movements all want heroic ancestors, even symbolic ones.

Revolutionaries claiming Spartacus, Thomas More and Winstanley as ancestors (although Marxist theory says Roman slave revolts could not lead to communism); schoolbooks teaching Boadicea, King Alfred and Joan of Arc; newly rich people buying aristocratic pedigrees; Stalin-era claims that Russians invented everything.

8. Chronology and historical thinking (key point) Must know

For modern historians, dates matter

Chronology = putting events in the right order, with dates and duration. Modern history assumes history is about change in one direction, so order matters.

Anachronism (putting something in the wrong time) is an "alarm signal" for historians.

Macbeth acted in modern dress shocks us today, but did not shock audiences in Shakespeare's time.

In traditional uses, dates don't matter

When the past is a pattern, model or moral lesson, the moral or symbolic meaning of a story matters more than when it happened.

Romans and Moors marching together in Spanish Easter processions; only pedants care whether Horatius came before Mucius Scaevola; Masada and the Maccabees matter to modern Israelis for their meaning, not their dates.
  • When real chronology is brought into a sacred or mythical past (e.g. scholarly dating of Homer or the Bible), it disturbs society and is a sign of transformation.
  • Yet dates still matter for practical purposes (law, administration, ritual). A lawyer looks for a precedent to use now; a historian cares about how different past circumstances were.

9. On History (1997), ch. 3: what can history tell us about today? Must know

Link between the two texts: the 1972 essay asks how societies live with the past; On History asks how historians use the past to explain the present. You cannot understand the present without its history. History is a tool of demystification (removing false beliefs).

History CAN

  • Show the roots of present institutions
  • Show how nations, identities and traditions were built
  • Reveal that many "new" things are part of longer developments
  • Separate real breaks from recycled rhetoric
vs

History CANNOT

  • Give mechanical lessons
  • Predict exactly what happens next
  • Replace political judgement
  • Give a ready-made programme for action

Q. Explain Hobsbawm's idea of the past as pattern, model and process.

Hobsbawm (1972) argues that "the past is a permanent dimension of human consciousness": every society uses its past, but how it uses it depends on how fast it changes.

In traditional societies the past is a pattern: people copy inherited customs, and law equals custom. Yet these societies are not static (static tradition is "a myth of vulgar social science"). Change is absorbed gradually, hidden, or renamed, as when nomads treated lorries as part of their camel trade. Innovation enters through gaps in the remembered past, through technology, or disguised as a return to a forgotten past. If change is too fast, tradition survives only through isolation (the Amish) or at great cost (the forced conversion of Mexican Indians).

When change accelerates, the past becomes a model, consciously chosen. Loud calls for "restoration" show that continuity has already broken. Zapata's peasants tried to restore their lands of 40 years earlier, but they had to imagine what they could not remember, the change could not be cut out "like a bullet", and their movement became a revolution that created a new Mexico. Restoration may also be only symbolic, like rebuilding Warsaw.

In modern societies the past becomes a process of progress that explains the present; "new" means "better", and even conservatives think historically.

But the past is often selected or invented, especially by nationalism. History cannot predict the future, though it explains how the present was made.

Historical Methods, Sources & Practices

Matthew Lange, Comparative-Historical Methods (2013), ch. 1 · Charlotte Backerra, "Source criticism for cultural history", Rethinking History (2024) + Lecture 2
In one lineHistorians work like detectives: they find sources, check whether they can be trusted (Backerra), interpret them, and explain why things happened, often by comparing cases (Lange).

1. What are historical methods? Must know

  • Historical methods are systematic ways of studying the past. History is analytical, not just descriptive: historians explain why things happened, not only what happened.
  • Steps: ask a question → find evidence → evaluate sources → compare societies → explain change → present an argument.
  • Source vs evidence: a source is any trace left from the past (letter, coin, tomb, photo). It becomes evidence only when a historian checks it and uses it to answer a specific question. Almost anything can become a source if it helps answer a question (Backerra).

Types of sources

Primary = made at the time (diaries, letters, court records, newspapers, photos, inscriptions, coins, travel accounts). Secondary = later analysis (scholarly books and articles). Tertiary = summaries (encyclopaedias, textbooks).

Xuanzang's diary is primary; a historian's book on Xuanzang is secondary; an encyclopaedia entry is tertiary.

Practice: oral history and archives

Oral history (Oral History Association): preparation → interview → preserve → give access. The key ethic is informed consent. Archival research (American Historical Association): plan → research on site → cite properly. The key ethic is provenance (where a document came from).

2. Lange: comparative-historical analysis (CHA) Must know

Lange's argument CHA has produced some of the most famous social science (Adam Smith, Tocqueville, Marx, Weber, Barrington Moore, Tilly, Skocpol). From 1986 to 2010 CHA books won a quarter of the American Sociological Association's best-book awards, yet there was no textbook explaining the method. Lange fills that gap. CHA is special because it combines different methods and balances the particular and the general. Its big topic is social change: state building, nationalism, capitalism, industrialisation, revolutions, democratisation, empire, globalisation.

Within-case methods (the "historical")

Look deep inside one case over time:
  • Causal narrative: a detective-style account of causes
  • Process tracing: following the chain that links A to B
  • Pattern matching: testing theories against one case's evidence
How exactly did the French monarchy collapse?
+

Comparative methods (the "comparative")

Compare several cases to find similarities, differences and causes. Types: narrative, Millian, Boolean, statistical. CHA mostly uses small-N (a few cases).Compare the French, Russian and Chinese revolutions.

The 4 defining elements of CHA

  1. Within-case methods
  2. Comparative methods
  3. Social-scientific outlook: believes real causes can be found (postmodern views that deny this are "anti-methodological")
  4. Big units: states, classes, economies, religions. Tilly: "big structures, large processes, huge comparisons"

What is NOT CHA (and why)

  • Said's Orientalism: compares and narrates, but is postmodern (denies social-science insight)
  • Acemoglu et al. on colonial legacies: only statistics, no within-case study
  • Tilly's Contentious French: deep study of France but no comparison

They are excellent works; each just lacks one element.

Two famous examples

Reinhard Bendix, Kings or People (1978): compared how 6 countries (England, France, Germany, Japan, Russia, China) moved from kings to popular rule; each path was unique (ideographic).

Theda Skocpol, States and Social Revolutions (1979): revolutions happen when the state breaks down AND peasants can rebel, but only for non-colonial agrarian states in a certain period (a balance of general and particular).

Why combining methods matters

Comparison pushes you towards general explanations; deep study of one case pushes you towards the particular. Using both stops you from over-generalising ("all revolutions are caused by X") and from treating every case as totally unique. That is why CHA sits in the middle, and is criticised from both sides.

3. Backerra: source criticism for cultural history Must know

Backerra's case study: early modern diplomatic letters (examples to use)

Old political historians used diplomats' letters only to find out "who said what to whom, when". Cultural historians also ask about rank, ceremony, symbols, space, language and emotions, i.e. "what did it mean?"

Layout and labels

Wider blank margins = higher-ranking reader. Letters were folded and sealed in special ways. "Secret" letters held key information (sometimes in code); "private" meant only the king and ministers could read it, not "personal".

Ceremony and language

Ceremony filled half of a diplomat's instructions (who visits whom first showed rank). The emperor wrote to George II in Italian, the king replied in French, and in German when writing as a vassal. Latin titles were changed to downgrade "majesty".

Networks, space, weather, emotion

Newspapers show the envoy Kinsky fencing and hosting balls in London (social networks missing from official letters). British envoys lived in a house outside Vienna's walls, which let them hold Protestant worship. An envoy writing "I never knew what Cold was" likely meant the frozen negotiations. Displays of emotion are read as performances.

Cultural history widens the sources

Not only government archives but rituals, symbols, emotions, objects, newspapers, personal letters and everyday life. Peter Burke: cultural history is a way of looking, not a separate field. Backerra asks historians to openly say "I" or "we" instead of pretending to be neutral.

Q. "Lange shows how historians explain; Backerra shows how they know." Discuss.

Historical method is both a science of evidence and an art of interpretation, and the two readings cover each side.

Lange explains how historians build causal explanations through comparative-historical analysis. CHA has four elements: within-case methods (causal narrative, process tracing), comparison across cases, a social-scientific belief that causes can be found, and big units like states and classes. Skocpol, for example, found that revolutions need both state breakdown and peasant power. CHA balances unique (ideographic) and general (nomothetic) explanation. This is why works such as Said's Orientalism do not count as CHA.

Backerra explains how historians decide whether evidence can be trusted, through source criticism (Droysen's heuristic, analysis, interpretation, presentation). Cultural historians use the same method with new questions: in diplomatic letters, wide margins showed rank, the choice of language showed status, and ceremony filled half the instructions.

But comparison can over-generalise, and presentation is still neglected. Together they make the full workflow: find → check → compare → explain.

Global and Regional Histories: Scale, Perspective, Context

Thomas D. Hall (ed.), Comparing Globalizations (2018), ch. 1 (Hall) & ch. 8 (on "place", via the lecture) + Lecture 3
In one lineGlobal history asks how places are connected; regional history asks how a place works inside. Historical understanding is strongest when we ask both questions together, because the scale you choose changes the story.

The problem

  • Events don't happen at only one level.

3. Wallerstein's world-system Must know

What it says

Immanuel Wallerstein (The Modern World-System, 1974): since the 16th century the world economy has been one system with a division of labour:

  • Core: rich, skilled, capital-heavy production
  • Semi-periphery: in between
  • Periphery: raw materials, cheap labour
Europe as the core; colonies as the periphery.

"A world-system is not the system of the world"

It means "a system that is a world" (a self-contained world), which can be smaller than the whole globe. It is the largest unit of social change: empires, states, societies and families exist inside it. The parts shape the whole, and the whole limits the parts.

(S8 link: Wallerstein put South Asia in the "semi-periphery"; Washbrook disagreed, and Bayly and Ludden called it Eurocentric.)

4. Hall: "Comparing Globalizations" Must know

Hall's main claims
  1. There is not one globalization but MULTIPLE globalizations.
  2. Globalizing processes have long, deep histories; they became "truly global" only in the last century or so. Today's globalization has new features, but they didn't appear from nowhere: "How can one tell what is new if one does not already know what is old?"
  3. Different world-systems arose at different times (many since the Neolithic, about 12,000 years ago).
  4. Comparison is more useful than one straight-line story, and what is worth comparing depends on your purpose.

Andre Gunder Frank (with Gills, 1993)

vs

Chase-Dunn & Hall (1997)

MANY world-systems of different kinds since the Neolithic (written "world-systems", with a hyphen).

Hall jokes the debate looks like "nit-picking about a hyphen", but the hyphen marks a real difference. Janet Abu-Lughod (Before European Hegemony, 1989) showed a linked world system from Europe to China in 1250–1350, before Europe dominated.

Hall's lessons about comparison

Apples and oranges

It's fine to compare apples and oranges if you are discussing fruit; add baseballs if you are discussing round objects. The purpose decides what is comparable.

Context matters

Narrow views give detail but miss the big picture. A cartoon of a young stock-market whiz asking "you mean they can go down?": narrow experience limits understanding. Bigger contexts can show that "constants" are actually slow-changing variables.

Move between levels

Some local processes can't be seen from far away, and very slow changes (like climate change) can be invisible in short spans of data. Know when to treat something as fixed and when as changing.

Turning points and comparison across time

Moments when a boundary is crossed and everything changes, e.g. the rise of the state. If you study only recent decades, you can't even ask such questions. Julian Go compares the British (19th c.) and American (20th c.) empires at the same "stage" and finds America is not exceptional.

Three debates in Hall's volume (short) (1) Afroeurasia was linked early (Abu-Lughod, against Wallerstein). Jared Diamond: Eurasia's east–west axis helped plants and animals spread. (2) Similar stages of social evolution happened in parallel in the Old and New Worlds (Sanderson). (3) Small luxury trade (silk, gems) could have big effects at both ends of a network (Turchin & Hall), so luxury goods can matter as much as bulk goods. Many authors study systems "from the bottom up", from the periphery.

5. Hall ch. 8: nation, region, globe Must know

  • A historical "place" can be seen at three levels: nation, region, globe. Each shows different patterns, so historians must move across scales.

  • Always had many regions inside it
  • Old ties to Asia, Africa and the Indian Ocean
  • Can't be reduced to one national story
  • Shows inner diversity + global connection

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. Why should global and regional histories be read together? Use India as an example.

Global history studies how places are connected (trade, migration, empire); regional history studies how a place works internally. Each alone is weak: global history can become too abstract, regional history too isolated.

Hall argues that there were multiple globalizations with long histories, not one modern one: "how can one tell what is new if one does not know what is old?" Wallerstein's world-system ("a system that is a world") links a core and a periphery, while Frank sees one 5,000-year system and Chase-Dunn and Hall see many. Such big models need regional detail to test them, and the right comparison depends on the purpose.

India shows why both are needed. It contains many regions, yet it was linked to Africa, Arabia and China through the Indian Ocean, which is both a region and a global arena. Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1498 looks like the start of colonialism at the national scale, but regionally he was only a new player, guided by a Gujarati pilot.

Since modern borders mislead us about the past, good history moves across scales and asks both "how was it connected?" and "how did it work inside?"

1. The starting problem: ancient India "without history"? Must know

  • For medieval India there were Muslim court chronicles, so the British knew it fairly well. But ancient India seemed to have no proper history books, so it had to be rebuilt from indirect sources: literature, inscriptions, coins, monuments, sculpture.
  • Sreedharan: the traditional Hindu view did not treat everyday history as "useful knowledge"; there was a vague, exaggerated sense of age. In 1808 the pundit Mrtyunjay Sharma wrote a Bengali history with kings ruling for lakhs of years.

2. Orientalists / Indology Must know

Indology = the systematic study of India's history, languages, literature, religions and culture.

Why did the British study India? (5 influences)

  1. Enlightenment: differences between peoples come from culture, not nature, which led to tolerance and curiosity (Voltaire admired India and China).
  2. Romanticism: fascination with ancient, mysterious places.
  3. A new scientific, critical way of handling sources.
  4. Comparative scholarship.
  5. Ruling needs: officials had to know Indian languages, law and culture. Governor-General Warren Hastings was the great patron: he learned Persian and loved the Gita.

3 meanings of "Orientalism"

  2. A style of thought (dividing the world into "West" and "East")

5. Marxist "new history" Must know

What made it "new" Not mainly new evidence, but "a re-reading of the sources with a different set of questions": class relations, modes of production, land and farming, surplus (who takes the extra produce), caste and economy, state formation. History became a social science.

D.D. Kosambi (the founder)

  • History = "the presentation, in chronological order, of successive developments in the means and relations of production" (tools, and who controls work).
  • Even without dates, we can know how people lived. He used archaeology, fieldwork, tribes, language and coin weights: India is full of "living survivals" of the past.
  • From tribe to caste: the plough (spread by Brahmin settlement) broke up tribes, and caste became the new way to organise labour.
  • Buddhism and Jainism had economic roots: new traders and landowners wanted peace; ahimsa saved cattle needed for ploughing.

R.S. Sharma · Romila Thapar · Irfan Habib · Bipan Chandra

  • R.S. Sharma: Indian Feudalism (land grants to Brahmins and officials, decline of towns and trade after the Guptas); Sudras in Ancient India; Urban Decay (from archaeology).

  • Irfan Habib: Mughal empire's heavy land-revenue demand crushed peasants, who revolted; the empire was "its own grave-digger".
  • Bipan Chandra: economic nationalism; attacked the Cambridge School (nationalism was a people's movement); communalism was a product of colonialism.

  • From kings and battles to society and ordinary people

Q. Was the shift from colonial and nationalist history to Marxist "new history" a major advance?

Nationalist history answered with pride: Jayaswal's ancient republics, Mookerji's "fundamental unity", and Dutt's and Naoroji's drain theory, which began economic history. But it often glorified a golden age "as compensation for the humiliating present" (Thapar) and fed communal history.

It moved history from kings to society, used archaeology and destroyed colonial myths.

Limits: class could become a rigid master explanation and the jargon was heavy. The Cambridge School stressed elite bargaining, and Subaltern Studies (Guha, 1982) showed that even Marxists missed peasants' own politics.

Conclusion: a major advance in scope and method, but history keeps being rewritten from new angles.

2. The Chinese pilgrims (Tansen Sen) Must know

Sen's argument Buddhism's spread from India to China (from the 1st century CE) started huge cross-cultural exchange: monks, traders, artisans and doctors moved across Central and Southeast Asia. The pilgrims brought texts, ritual objects and detailed records. Their writings made China see India as a sacred, even ideal land of "civilised" people. That was rare praise, because Chinese writers usually called foreigners barbarians. Their records are valuable for: (1) Buddhist doctrine, rituals and monasteries; (2) social and political conditions in India; (3) how cultures saw each other; (4) the hardships of travel and the monk–merchant relationship.

Why did they come? To collect scriptures, visit places linked to the Buddha's life, learn Sanskrit and study with Indian teachers, then bring texts home for translation.

Faxian (left Chang'an in 399, aged over 60)

  • Came for Vinaya texts (rules for running monasteries), which were scarce in China.
  • Route: across the Taklamakan desert → Central Asia → Indian holy sites → Sri Lanka → a dangerous sea voyage home via Southeast Asia (sailed from Tamralipti, stayed 2 years in Sri Lanka).
  • Recorded Buddhist legends and relic worship (the Buddha's bowl at Peshawar, the tooth relic in Sri Lanka), which spread such ceremonies to China.
  • Monks and merchants helped each other: monks travelled with caravans and ships; traders profited from demand for ritual goods; monasteries gave traders lodging.
  • One of the earliest outside accounts of Gupta India. His book: A Record of the Buddhist Kingdoms.

Xuanzang (c.600–664), "the greatest Chinese traveller"

  • Unhappy with poor Chinese translations ("when words are wrong, the meaning is lost"), he wanted original texts and Indian teachers.
  • Brought back 657 texts; Emperor Taizong built the Big Wild Goose Pagoda to house them.
  • Met King Harsha at Kanauj (637/638), which led to diplomatic relations between Harsha and Tang China. So he was a pilgrim and a diplomat.
  • Studied at Nalanda; gave India the Chinese name "Yindu"; surveyed caste, education, cities, food and law.
  • His notes help archaeologists today (Bamiyan, Nalanda); R.S. Sharma used his reports of decaying towns.

Yijing (635–713)

  • Travelled mainly by sea (671–695) and studied at Srivijaya (Sumatra) on the way.
  • Wrote about 40 daily practices in Indian monasteries to correct Chinese "errors", and biographies of 56 Chinese monks who went to India.
  • Allowed sensible compromise. Indians don't use chopsticks, but the rules don't forbid them, so Chinese monks may keep them.
  • Showed India within a wider Asian Buddhist world (links to Southeast Asia).

The "borderland complex"

Chinese monks felt China was on the edge ("borderland") of the Buddhist world, far from the Buddha's land. Faxian's companion stayed in India, wishing "not to be reborn in the borderland". At Nalanda, monks asked Xuanzang why he would go back to a land where "Buddhas are never born"; he replied that China was civilised and the Dharma must spread.

The pilgrims tried to recreate an Indian Buddhist world inside China so others need not travel. Yijing: "a second Rajagriha in China".

What the pilgrims teach historians (lecture): Buddhist networks between monasteries, universities (Nalanda), city life, trade routes shared by monks and merchants, diplomacy between courts, and cultural interaction. "Religion became a vehicle connecting Asia."

Q. How useful are foreign travellers' accounts as sources for Indian history?

The Chinese pilgrims were eyewitnesses. Faxian (399–413) described Gupta India, relic worship and his sea voyage home. So these accounts must be corroborated with inscriptions, coins and archaeology. Used critically, they also show that India was connected to Asia, not isolated.

Baghdad to Canton: Commerce and Culture in the Medieval Indian Ocean

In one lineLong before Europeans came, the Indian Ocean was a regular, lasting system of "premodern globalisation" from East Africa to China, run by monsoon winds, ports, merchant networks and shared rules (especially Islamic law), with India at the centre.

1. The big argument Must know

  • The Indian Ocean was a major arena of premodern globalisation: not modern capitalism, but a dense, regular system of exchange over long distances.
  • It was patterned and lasting, not occasional, linking East Africa, the Middle East, India, Southeast Asia and China, and it worked before European naval control. This challenges Eurocentric stories.
  • Trade carried both luxury goods (spices, silk) and bulk goods (textiles, grain, horses, metals, timber), which mattered just as much.
  • India sat in the strategic middle: central in geography, productive in goods and influential in culture; both a destination and a relay point.

2. Picture it: the monsoon "timetable"

Ships could cross only at certain times, so merchants sailed in segments. Goods from Baghdad to Canton changed ships at least 3 times.

Monsoons shaped everything

Seasonal winds set routes and sailing calendars. Most merchants did one segment, not the whole route.

A Gujarati merchant sails on the summer monsoon to Malacca with cotton cloth and indigo, swaps for silk, spices and porcelain, and returns on the winter monsoon. Or he goes west to Aden (horses, incense) or Malindi (gold, slaves).

Nature shaped exchange: the Indian Ocean world was ecological as well as commercial.

Danger, distance, risk

  • Before the Chinese magnetic compass (c.12th c.) sailors used the stars; fog was as deadly as storms. Longitude could not be measured until the 18th century.
  • Shipwreck, piracy, storms; a short trip could take over a year.
  • Trade was profitable because it was risky: scarcity raised the value.
  • Travellers' exaggerations are themselves evidence of how scary distance felt (source criticism).

4. The story in 4 phases

Phase 1: Tang China + Abbasid caliphate, direct sea trade (7th–9th c.)

  • Arab ("Ta-shih") and Persian ("Po-ssi") ships sailed direct to China; trade diasporas (Persians, Jews, Nestorian Christians) settled along the Chinese coast. By 758 Muslims in Canton were numerous enough to sack the city.
  • 750: the Abbasids moved Islam's capital to riverside Baghdad: "This is the Tigris; there is no obstacle between us and China."
  • The route (from the Akhbar): Basra and Siraf → Oman → Malabar (one month) → across the Bay of Bengal → Kedah → Strait of Malacca → Vietnam → Canton. About 4 months of sailing in theory, over a year in practice.
  • At Canton: the government held goods for 6 months, took a 30% duty, and bought first. The Akhbar admired Chinese literacy and pensions and described tea (the West's first mention).
  • Marvels story: traders enslave a kind East African king; years later, now a Muslim, he welcomes them back: "Muslims shall know they may come to us like brothers." This shows slavery and Islam's power to bind traders.
  • Collapse: in 878, Huang Chao's rebels sacked Canton and killed about 120,000 foreign merchants. Trade moved north to Quanzhou (Zaitun).
  • "Sinbad's way" (Gulf route) was preferred over the Red Sea (pirates, reefs) and the overland Silk Road (slow, dangerous).

Phase 2: the Mongol window and Ibn Battuta (13th–14th c.)

  • The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258; for about a century a chain of Mongol states kept routes open (used by the Polos and Ibn Battuta).
  • Ibn Battuta (born Tangier, 1304), a qadi, travelled about 74,000 miles. In Delhi under Muhammad bin Tughluq (1325–51), who recruited foreign scholars with huge rewards, newcomers gave the sultan costly gifts paid for with merchants' loans, repaid from his bigger return gift (credit + patronage). Battuta became qadi of Delhi, ran up a 55,000-dinar debt, then was sent as ambassador to China.
  • He saw Samudera (Sumatra, 1345), the first Muslim-ruled place in Southeast Asia, at the start of the conversion that made Indonesia the world's largest Muslim nation.
  • Shared commercial code: a Muslim importer in Cairo and his supplier in Cambay followed the same religious and commercial rules. Customs: 2.5% for Muslims, 5% for Christians and Jews, 10% for others. The hajj united the trading world (Jeddah became a great market). Yet the ocean was "no Muslim lake": Calicut had a Hindu ruler (the zamorin).

Phase 3: Zheng He's treasure fleets (1405–1433)

  • Chinese ships had iron nails, watertight compartments, rudders and the compass; Indian Ocean dhows were sewn with coconut fibre (more flexible on reefs; little iron available).
  • Zheng He, a Muslim eunuch admiral, led 7 voyages of about 300 ships and 30,000 men to Malacca, India, Hormuz and East Africa. The purpose was diplomatic, military and symbolic, not trade. He made Malacca safe from pirates and from Siam, and brought back giraffes.
  • After 1433 Confucian officials stopped the voyages; later big ships were banned. Bernstein: centralised China could switch off contact, while the decentralised Indian Ocean had "Darwinian" competition among ports.
  • Modern China uses Zheng He as a symbol of "peaceful" policy, but his fleets also killed those who refused homage: a political use of the past (S1).

Phase 4: Tomé Pires and Malacca on the eve of the Portuguese (c.1500) Must know

Cambay ↔ Malacca: the main axis

"Cambay with her right arm reaches towards Aden, and with the other towards Malacca." Pires praised the Gujaratis: "they have the cream of the trade… our people who want to be clerks and factors ought to go there and learn." Praise from a proud Portuguese "spoke volumes".

Why Malacca succeeded

Founded c.1400 by Parameswara, a prince of Palembang (Srivijaya's line). Pires counted 84 languages; four harbourmasters handled different regions.

  • Low duties (at most 6%; less for settled merchants)
  • Honest courts and officials
  • A transparent auction, a "medieval eBay"
Success came from institutions, not just location. It fell to the Portuguese in 1511.
Islam and trade Parameswara converted to Islam (marrying a Pase princess, for protection). Islam's law covers commerce, so becoming Muslim "did wonders for one's credit rating". Southeast Asia was converted not by conquerors but by cloth and spice merchants from Cambay and Calicut who married local women and raised their children Muslim.

5. Why India was central (lecture: 6 reasons) Must know

1. Intermediary

Western and eastern circuits met at Indian ports, where cargo was sorted, taxed and re-shipped. Centrality can come from connecting, not only producing.

2. Gujarat & Cambay

One of the most skilled trading regions, linked to the Gulf, Red Sea, East Africa and Southeast Asia.

3. Textiles

"If spices were glamorous, cloth was indispensable." Indian cotton reached ordinary consumers and was exchanged for spices, silk and porcelain.

4. Malabar & pepper

Calicut was built around pepper; Chinese demand was huge. Asian markets were powerful in their own right (decentring Europe).

5. Ibn Battuta's India

The Delhi Sultanate attracted scholars from across Islam: India as a hub of talent, credit and patronage in the Muslim world.

6. Delhi and the coast

Inland power and sea trade were linked but separate. Coastal wealth depended on security, fair taxes and efficient ports, under both Muslim and Hindu rulers.

India as a cultural carrier: Indian Muslim traders carried Islam east, and trade networks came before political conversion: "religious change often happens through households and trade, not just armies." Malacca mattered largely because of India: Gujarati and Malabari traders were among its most important merchants.

Conclusion Premodern globalisation was real long before Europe; Europe entered an already-connected world as a late participant, not its creator; India was central in geography, economy, institutions and culture; later empires built on, disrupted and took over these structures. "To study the history of trade is to study the making of connected worlds." (But it was not all "peaceful": slavery, piracy and violence existed.)
Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. Why was India central to the Indian Ocean trading system before 1500?

Bernstein shows that the medieval Indian Ocean was a form of premodern globalisation, a regular system linking East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia and China, organised by monsoons, ports, merchant networks and shared institutions. India sat at its centre, but not just because of geography.

First, monsoon voyages were done in segments, so ships from the west and east met at Indian ports, and India acted as the intermediary where cargo was sorted, taxed and re-shipped. Second, India was a great producer: its cotton textiles were "indispensable" across Asia, and Malabar pepper was in huge demand in China. Third, the Gujarati merchants of Cambay were so skilled that Tomé Pires told the Portuguese to learn from them; they dominated Malacca, which had 84 languages. Fourth, Indian Muslim traders spread Islam to Southeast Asia through trade and marriage, and Delhi under Tughluq drew scholars like Ibn Battuta.

But centrality also depended on institutions: safe ports, fair taxes and shared Islamic commercial law (customs of 2.5%, 5% or 10%), as Malacca's success shows.

So Europe entered an already-connected world; it did not create it.

Silk Route Connections I: Overland Networks ("Comparing Two Lenses")

1. What is the "Silk Road"? Must know

Popular view vs reality

Popular view: one overland road from China to the West, mainly for silk and luxuries.

Britannica (lecture): about 6,400 km from Xi'an, along the Great Wall, around the Taklamakan desert, over the Pamirs, through Afghanistan to the Mediterranean. Few people travelled the whole way; goods passed from middleman to middleman.

The name was coined in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, who drew it as one line on a map (he was planning a railway).

The readings show: many routes carrying people, religions, ideas, goods, states and ecologies.

How it worked (lecture)

  • Goods moved by trade but also as gifts and dowries. Han rulers kept peace by giving silk to the Xiongnu nomads.
  • Cosmopolitan centres: Chang'an, Samarkand, Dunhuang
  • Religions spread: Buddhism, Christianity, Manichaeism, Zoroastrianism
  • Art: the Dunhuang caves, Sogdian paintings in Samarkand

3. Hansen: Chang'an, the cosmopolitan end of the Silk Road Must know

Hansen's argument Chang'an was a truly international city, home to monks, merchants, craftsmen, officials and refugees. But few bulk goods came overland; trade volumes were smaller than popular memory suggests. What mattered most was the movement of people, skills and ideas. Her evidence is mainly archaeological.

The city

  • Capital of the Han, Sui and Tang (618–907) dynasties; about 1 million people
  • A planned grid of 109 walled wards with a night curfew
  • Two markets: Eastern (local goods) and Western (foreign goods, inns, Sogdian traders)
  • The Tang Code banned high officials from markets (trade was seen as "polluting"); officials set prices
  • Law for foreigners: same nationality → their own law; different nationalities → Chinese law

Foreigners and religions

  • Foreigners came through treaties (Turks, 631), as hostage princes, or as refugees (Persian princes after the Muslim conquest of Iran)
  • Zoroastrian temples near the Western Market
  • The Nestorian stele (781) records a Christian monk, Aluohan, arriving in 635
  • The Big Goose Pagoda (for Xuanzang's texts) survives

Archaeological evidence (her key examples)

Sogdian tombs: mixing of cultures

An Jia (d. 579), a sabao (headman of the foreign community): his tomb is Chinese in shape but has a Zoroastrian fire altar and a stone bed instead of a coffin. The carvings show diplomacy, not commerce.

Shi Wirkak (d. 579): a stone "house" with a bilingual Sogdian–Chinese epitaph and the soul crossing the Zoroastrian Chinwad bridge to paradise.

Lesson: migrants adapted Chinese practices and mixed them with their own ("hybridity").

Pawn tickets and the Hejiacun hoard

Pawnshop tickets (660s–680s, later reused to make a figurine): ordinary people pawned clothes at 5% a month, and about a quarter of the customers were women. This is everyday life, not grand trade.

Hejiacun hoard (found 1970): over 1,000 items, but only a few imports (gems, a glass bowl, a Persian coin); most were made in China by migrant Sogdian craftsmen.

Fewer than 50 Roman coins found in all of China (many used as charms); silk was often used as money (to pay soldiers). Objects correct the myth of endless luxury caravans.

Decline: change over time

  • An Lushan rebellion (755–763), led by a Sogdian-Turkish general. Afterwards the Tang blamed Sogdians; in one account people with "big noses" were killed, the first time a Chinese ruler targeted a minority.
  • Tibetans looted Chang'an (763); in 787 minister Li Mi cut support for 4,000 foreign envoys who had settled there.
  • Stereotype vs reality: tales show Sogdians as fabulously rich jewel merchants, but most were soldiers, farmers, envoys and craftsmen. A stereotype is not proof of a rich trade.
  • 843–845: foreign religions (Manichaeism, Buddhism, Zoroastrianism, Christianity) banned. Land routes declined, and travel shifted to the sea. Huang Chao's rebels killed foreigners at Guangzhou (878) and burned Chang'an; the Tang ended in 907.

4. Bhattacharya: Bengal and the Southern Silk Road Must know

Bhattacharya's argument New policies, India's Look/Act East and China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), have revived memories of old routes once called "frontier" or "periphery". She argues Bengal was central: a hub linking the delta, rivers, Himalayas, Tibet, Burma (Arakan) and Yunnan (China) with the sea. It was "peripheral" only from the viewpoint of rulers far from the sea (Delhi, Beijing). The "Silk Route" was a web of land, river and sea paths (a "Southern Silk Road" or even a "Cotton Road").

Useful concepts she uses

  • The periphery is constructed (Yang Bin, van Schendel)
  • Zomia (James Scott): the stateless uplands of Southeast Asia
  • Tirthankar Roy: Bengal as "parallel globalizations"
  • Braudel's longue durée (long term) vs short "episodic" political history
  • Wallerstein put South Asia in the "semi-periphery"; Washbrook, Bayly and Ludden criticised this as Eurocentric

Bengal's history in "episodes"

  • Buddhism flourished under the Palas (Tamralipti port, visited by Xuanzang)
  • The Senas revived Brahmanism
  • Ilyas Shahi sultans (from 1342) broke from Delhi: two centuries of autonomy
  • Zheng He's fleets reached Pandua (1415) and Gaur (1432)
  • Colonial era: Plassey 1757, Calcutta as "London of the East", partitions in 1905 and 1947

Four old trade corridors: (1) Tibet–Bhutan–Nepal–Bengal; (2) Yunnan–Arakan–Bengal; (3) Ganga river networks; (4) Assam–Bengal. Horses came through Himalayan passes; Tibet traded gold, salt, musk and wool. China is "Cina" in the Mahabharata, and the Arthashastra mentions Chinese silk; the Chinese envoy Zhang Qian noted India–China trade before the 2nd century BC. Main goods: silver, horses, cotton cloth, cowries.

Case 1: cotton, the "Cotton Road"

Chinese writers praised Bengal's fine cotton (muslin), and China kept buying Indian cotton even after growing its own (Stephen Dale). Chinese names for the route include the "Road of the Shu Cloth".

Why "Silk Road" and not "Cotton Road"? Richthofen's name stuck, and Bengal was imagined as a periphery.

Case 2: cowrie shells

Small shells used as cheap money from the Maldives through Bengal to Yunnan, mentioned by Faxian, Xuanzang, Yijing, Ibn Battuta and Tomé Pires. In Bengal a chicken cost about 448 cowries. Where cowries spread maps how far the Southern Silk Road reached.

The professor said you may skip: the detailed lists of route towns (Bhamo, Myitkyina and so on), the 15th-century Chinese sources on Bengal, the post-1947 transition paragraph, and the conclusion's details on Siliguri and Nathu La.

Colonial rule restructured the routes

  • Colonialism restructured mobility rather than simply expanding it: new institutions, railways, steamships, borders
  • Warren Hastings sent missions to Bhutan and Tibet (Bogle, 1774) to tap trade; the British annexed the Duars (1841–65)
  • Tea in Assam; the Permanent Settlement; Bengal's textile industry declined, and wealth flowed out to Britain
  • The Younghusband mission to Tibet (1904); the Ledo (Stilwell) Road to China (1942)
  • Moving the capital to Delhi in 1912 made Bengal "peripheral"

After 1947: walls go up

Partition (1947), the 1962 India–China war, Communist China and the Cold War built "huge walls". Regional groups such as ASEAN (1967) and SAARC (1985) split South and Southeast Asia. "The cowrie connections were soon chapters of a forgotten history."

Her critical conclusion: the political use of the past States use history selectively (facts and legends) for diplomacy. BRI treats the "Silk Route", which "at no point in history was a single route", as one road led by China, pushing South Asian routes to the margins. Act East also treats the North-East as a periphery run from Delhi. There is a gap between policy and real history. This is Hobsbawm's symbolic restoration and invented past (S1).

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. "There is no single Silk Road." Discuss with reference to Hansen and Bhattacharya.

The term "Silk Road" was coined by Richthofen in 1877 as one line from China to Europe, but the readings show a web of routes carrying people, religions and many goods, with few people travelling the whole way.

Hansen uses a city lens. Through archaeology in Chang'an (An Jia's Chinese tomb with a Zoroastrian altar, the Hejiacun hoard, pawn tickets) she shows a cosmopolitan city where foreigners mixed cultures, but where few goods travelled far. Most items were made locally by migrant craftsmen, so people and skills mattered more. After the An Lushan rebellion (755) and the bans of 843–845, the city declined and trade moved to the sea.

Bhattacharya uses a regional lens. Bengal was a hub linking rivers, mountains and sea to Yunnan and Southeast Asia, shown by trade in cotton (a "Cotton Road") and cowrie shells. Colonial rule restructured these routes, and 1947 and 1962 closed them, making Bengal "peripheral".

Today, China's BRI (2013) and India's Act East (2014) use the Silk Road memory selectively, a political use of the past. So the Silk Road was multiple and changing, and what we see depends on the lens and scale we choose.

Silk Route Connections II: Trade, Religion and Buddhism Across the Sea

Tansen Sen, "Buddhism and the Maritime Crossings" (2014) + Lecture 9
In one lineBuddhism reached China by sea as well as by land, carried by monks on merchant ships. Trade and religion supported each other, Southeast Asia was first only a stop-over, and later Srivijaya and Sri Lanka became major Buddhist hubs.

1. Sen's main argument: 6 claims Must know

  1. Critique of the "Silk Road" focus: scholars studied only the overland routes; one book on Buddhism's spread had not a single chapter on the sea. The term "maritime Silk Road" is misleading.
  2. Buddhism and sea trade were tied together, just as on land.
  3. "Long-distance", not "contact" transmission (Erik Zürcher's idea): in the first centuries CE, maritime Southeast Asia was only a "transit zone" where monks changed ships.
  4. India–China sea networks were cosmopolitan: Indian, Sogdian and Central Asian merchants and monks, Sri Lankan nuns, Southeast Asian kings.
  5. Sri Lanka was not only the home of Theravada; it was also a centre of relics and Tantric Buddhism.
  6. Sea networks lasted longer than land networks (into the 19th–20th centuries).

2. Why "Maritime Silk Road" is a misleading name Must know

Sen's 3 objections

  1. Silk was not the main cargo at sea. Early trade was in beads, precious stones, pearls; later in bulk goods like incense, pepper, spices, porcelain.
  2. It over-stresses China. Ports and ships from Southeast Asia, India and the Middle East were just as important, and the Chinese became active in Indian Ocean shipping only from the 10th century.
  3. It ignores Southeast Asia, whose rise was crucial for the sea route.

Alternative: see maritime Asia as a multi-centred world, not a China-centred extension of the Silk Road.

The sea was busy long before Buddhism

  • 2500–1500 BCE: Austronesian migrations linked south China, Southeast Asia and Indonesia
  • 1st millennium BCE: metal and wet-rice farming spread; Southeast Asian sailors were as important as Indian ones (they exported tin and gold)
  • 1st c. CE: Funan (Mekong delta), with its port Oc Eo, trans-shipped goods between India and China
  • Indo-Roman trade: Roman coins in Tamil Nadu, in exchange for pepper and textiles
  • Han China's ports (Jiaozhi, Hepu, Guangzhou) received ivory, pearls and incense; with these traders came Buddhist preachers and texts

3. Land or sea first? Sen's way out of an old debate

The old debate

Sen: the debate is "irrelevant"

There were no monasteries on either route before the 4th century, by which time Buddhism was already in China. Evidence shows both: Buddhist carvings at Mount Kongwang (coast) point to the sea; Buddha figures in Sichuan tombs point to land. Multiple routes, not one.
"Long-distance" vs "contact" transmission (Zürcher) Contact transmission works like a relay race: each region adopts Buddhism, builds monasteries, then passes it on. Long-distance transmission works like a long-haul flight: monks and texts go straight from India to China, and the places in between are only stop-overs. Zürcher showed that Central Asian towns became Buddhist centres only from the mid-3rd century; Sen applies the same idea to the sea.

Better question (Sen): study how land and sea were complementary, connected and sometimes competing. Many monks used both. Mahayana grew on the land routes; Theravada later on the sea routes. The lecture calls this balanced view (sea did not "replace" land) one of the article's greatest strengths.

4. Monks and merchants: partners, but not always friends Must know

Lecture: religion did not just travel alongside trade; it became part of trade itself. Buddhism spread because trade networks already existed.

Partnership (the usual view)

James Heitzman: in India, monasteries stood near trade towns and routes, in mutual support with merchants. Xinru Liu applied this to India–China.

The Buddhist "seven jewels" (saptaratna) offered to monasteries created demand for pearls, coral and lapis lazuli from India.

Antagonism (Sen's addition)

Merchants had their own gods and loyalties; some were Brahmanical or later Muslim, and they might compete over the trade in relics and texts.

In a storm in 411, "Brahmin" merchants on Faxian's ship blamed him for bad luck and wanted to leave him on an island. His patron threatened to report them to the Chinese ruler, and they backed down.

7. Sri Lanka reinterpreted Must know

Lecture: "perhaps the most significant contribution of this article" Most histories call Sri Lanka simply the centre of Theravada. Sen shows it was also a transit point (from the 4th c.), a store of relics, a staging point for pilgrims, a centre of Mahayana and Tantric Buddhism, and a diplomatic actor. Faxian, Vajrabodhi, Amoghavajra and Atisa all passed through. Its monasteries were crossroads, not isolated.

Relics = power

Faxian saw the Bodhi tree sapling and the tooth relic festival. Kings believed relics had magical and political power:

  • Candrabhanu, a Malay king, attacked Sri Lanka (1247, 1262) to get relics
  • Kublai Khan sent an envoy (1284), perhaps for the alms bowl
  • Zheng He captured a Sri Lankan ruler (1410), probably wanting the tooth relic

Tantra and the Abhayagiri monastery

Vajrabodhi worshipped the tooth relic for 6 months at Abhayagiri before sailing to China (719–20). Amoghavajra went there in 741 to collect Tantric texts and returned with a royal mission (746). Around 800 the head of Vikramashila (India's top Tantric monastery) was a Sri Lankan monk, Jayabhadra.

Ratu Boko, Java (792): copied, but adapted A Javanese inscription names "the Abhayagiri Vihara of the Sinhalese", a second Abhayagiri in Java (de Casparis). Its platforms resemble Sri Lanka's (Miksic) but face a different direction (north–south, not east–west), showing local adaptation, not simple copying (Degroot).

Later Theravada networks (11th–15th c.): when the Cholas invaded Sri Lanka, monks fled to Myanmar; after King Vijayabahu I freed the island, Sri Lankan and Burmese monks restored Buddhism together. Close ties followed with Myanmar, Thailand and Cambodia. Modern echo: Anagarika Dharmapala (1864–1933) travelled by sea to spread Buddhism worldwide.

Q. How did trade and religion support each other in the maritime spread of Buddhism? (Sen)

Tansen Sen argues that Buddhism reached China by sea as well as by land, and that this spread was closely tied to trade. He rejects the label "maritime Silk Road", since silk was not the main cargo and the label wrongly makes China the centre.

Merchants gave monks passage, funded monasteries and carried relics and texts; monasteries gave merchants lodging, trust and blessings. Offerings of the "seven jewels" created demand for pearls and coral (Xinru Liu). The Indian ship-owner Nandin, who carried Gunavarman and Sri Lankan nuns to China, shows this link. But the relationship was not always friendly: "Brahmin" merchants on Faxian's ship wanted to abandon him in a storm.

Following Zürcher, Sen shows that Southeast Asia was first only a transit zone; the first Buddhist inscription there was set up by a sea captain, Buddhagupta. Later Srivijaya (1,000 monks, according to Yijing) and Sri Lanka (relics and Tantric teachers like Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra) became hubs, and knowledge flowed back to India with Atisa.

Limitation: he relies on Chinese sources and gives little voice to local people or women. Still, he shows that religion became part of trade itself.

Religions that came from outside

The study of religion has shifted from looking only at doctrines to seeing religion as a social and historical thing.

Plurality ≠ Pluralism

Plurality = a fact: many religions exist. Pluralism = a value or practice: communities negotiate living together, through accommodation, interaction, borrowing, competition and even conflict.

Key question: how do people live with religious difference?

A town with a temple, a mosque and a church has plurality; neighbours sharing festivals shows pluralism.

Every religion is diverse inside

No religion is one single thing. Each has schools, sects, rituals, regional versions and rival authorities. Boundaries exist but are not fixed or uniform.

Hinduism: Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta, bhakti. Islam: Sunni, Shia, Sufi. Sikhism: Khalsa and Sahajdhari.

Religion as lived experience

Religion is more than belief: belief → practice → experience → social life (food, worship, pilgrimage, death rites, festivals, family customs). Keep apart religion as doctrine (texts) and religion as lived practice.

Texts draw sharp lines, but Hindus and Muslims both visit Sufi shrines.

Religion and society are intertwined

Religion is tied to caste, community, family, gender, economy and politics.

A temple is also an economic institution, a centre of redistribution, a seat of power, an employer and a marker of identity.

Tradition is not static

Tradition → adaptation → innovation. Traditions reinterpret old practices, create new movements and meet other traditions.

Bhakti, the Kabir Panth, the Ramakrishna Mission, Ambedkar's turn to Buddhism.

The colonial lens hardened identities

Missionaries studied Indian religions through a Judeo-Christian model (one God, one book, clear membership), which did not fit India (many ideas of God, regional variety, overlapping identities). From 1871 the British census put everyone into fixed boxes.

Fluid identities became "Hindu or Muslim or Sikh". Only in the 20th century did scholars adopt a pluralist view.

Part 2: Madan's main points Must know

Madan's argument
  1. India has religious plurality at two levels: between religions and within each religion (sects and movements).
  2. Calling Indian religions "tolerant and plural" and foreign ones "uniform and rigid" is wholly misleading: any religion with millions of followers across regions becomes internally plural (Islam and Christianity in India too).
  3. Dharma ≠ "religion": dharma means duty, moral and cosmic order, a whole way of life (law, family, society). In India religion traditionally includes the secular.
  4. Plurality is a fact; whether it produced pluralism is a separate question. Traditional "pluralism" was often a ranking.

Q. Distinguish religion as doctrine from religion as lived practice in India.

The lecture and Madan argue that religion is more than belief: it moves from belief → practice → experience → social life. So historians must separate doctrine from lived religion.

Doctrine means the official teachings in sacred texts and from religious authorities: the Vedas and Manusmriti, the Quran and sharia (upheld by the ulama), or the Sikh Khalsa code. Doctrine tends to draw clear boundaries between religions.

Lived religion is what people actually do: food rules, festivals, pilgrimage, local saints. In practice boundaries are porous. Hindus and Muslims visit the same Sufi shrines, the Mapilla Muslims of Kerala kept local customs, and Madan calls this everyday mixing "folk pluralism". Religion is also embedded in society: a temple is an economic and social institution.

The difference matters. The colonial census (from 1871), using a text-based Judeo-Christian model, put people into fixed boxes and hardened identities. Studying lived religion shows that Indian religions were plural and changing, as at Nagapattinam, where Buddha images were carried in processions like Hindu gods.

1. What does "connected space" mean? Must know

"Connection does not mean sameness; it means structured interaction across distance." The Indian Ocean linked East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, Southeast Asia and China.

Five forms of connection

  1. Routes: shaped by wind (monsoon), geography and custom
  2. Shared trade practices: common norms of credit and exchange
  3. Migration: people settled and carried skills and languages
  4. Religious networks: belief and pilgrimage
  5. Political and military encounters: war and diplomacy are also connection

Why it matters in world history

  • One of the oldest arenas of exchange
  • Long-distance trade long before European empires
  • Goods, people, technology, languages and religion moved
  • Central to Islam, India, Southeast Asia, China and East Africa
  • Decentres Europe: Indian Ocean history is not a "prelude" to European expansion

2. Chaudhuri: unity and disunity (622–1750) Must know

The question Fernand Braudel wrote the history of the Mediterranean (1949) as one sea with its societies, in terms of space, time and structures. Chaudhuri (author of Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean, 1985, and Asia Before Europe, 1990) asks: can we do the same for the Indian Ocean? Sceptics said no: it has no real unity, no clear time frame, and Asia is "too complex and diverse", so they avoided the question.
Chaudhuri: similar at the big level, different at the small level.

Unity in the large vs similarity in the small

From maths (Wittgenstein): a Möbius strip, a cylinder and a twisted cylinder look the same if you cut out a small square of each ("alike in the small"), but their overall shapes differ. So matching small details don't prove a shared overall form, and differing details don't disprove it.

Word by word, the Rig Veda, the Quran and Confucian texts differ. But Arab sailors could sail from Suez and Basra to China, so at the level of transcontinental trade the ocean had real unity.

Why so few Asia-to-Asia comparisons?

In the past, Asian writers were curious about their neighbours. In the 19th century, with Europe's power, Asian historians began comparing themselves with Europe (feudalism, capitalism). Their outlook turned from latitudinal (Asia ↔ Asia) to longitudinal (Asia ↔ Europe).

The time problem: Arasaratnam's critique

S. Arasaratnam said Chaudhuri proved geographic unity but not chronological unity for "750–1750". Chaudhuri replied that his period starts at 622 (the rise of Islam), not 750. The deeper point: unity of space and unity of time may not match. South Asian periods don't fit an ocean timeline; Islamic historians see a time unity but deny that "Asia" is a unit.

His theory (in short)

History is an interaction of six domains: real and perceived space, real and perceived time, real and perceived structures, plus a 7th, the "logical space". He mixes four strands: Braudel, structuralism, semiotics (study of signs) and set theory (maths). Critics called him a "latter-day Dr Faustus" drawing randomly on everything.

Chaudhuri's four conclusions

ConclusionIn simple words
1. InvarianceThe "ground floor" of daily life (food, clothes, houses, religious principles, technology) stayed basically the same while varying within limits. Stomach-lining cells are replaced every few days, but the stomach keeps the same function.

The "ground floor" of daily life (good examples) Must know

Food

  • Every society separates "food" from "non-food", but what counts differs; there are also famine food, festive food and sacred food

Dry zone vs wet zone

Nomads and farmers

What was new: organisation

  • Joint-stock finance (many investors, shared risk)
  • Monopoly charters
  • Forts, factories (trading stations), armed ships
  • A single company could send credible diplomatic missions to Indian courts
Roy's strongest point: organisation mattered as much as military force.

Three problems of the English Company

  1. Monopoly hard to enforce: rival merchants and "interlopers"; bribes to local kings
  2. Private trade by its own employees, which it could not control ("the more capable the officer, the bigger the conflict of interest")
  3. Embassies of limited use: Mughal orders didn't reach the south; local governors blocked them; disputes over customs and the right to mint coins

What was traded: spices (early 1600s) → Indian cotton textiles, saltpetre, Chinese silk (1650–1750) → Chinese tea (late 1700s), paid for by Indian opium. Indian cloth also paid for spices in Indonesia and slaves in Africa. Trade ran on American silver, cheaper in Europe and in great demand in India. By mid-century the EIC had about 50% of the textile market.

(d) Europeans depended on Indians

Indian agency Om Prakash: Europeans "had no option but to operate within the given" Asian system. Asian traders controlled almost everything except the final sale and sea shipment: overland, river and inter-regional trade.

Bankers

Turned imported silver into coins, lent money, changed currencies and sent money by promissory notes (hundis). The Jagatseths of Bengal; bankers even supplied military intelligence.

Brokers

Took company advances and gave out cloth orders to many sub-contractors; knew the market and language. Relations mixed dependence and distrust.

Kasi Viranna (Madras); Kola Venkadri, jailed by the English for secret talks with Golkonda; Amirchand, who plotted against Siraj ud-Daula (1756) and was punished by Clive; Ananda Ranga Pillai's diary describes the hard job.

"Contract" was new

Hindu and Islamic law dealt with debt, not sales contracts, so the word "contract" was probably brought by Europeans. Written contracts fixed quantity, price, quality and advances; they helped bookkeeping and carried moral force.

Coastal shipping and technology

Indian communities owned coastal boats (dhows, dhonis). Technology moved both ways: Europeans copied Indian cloth printing; India learned from European navigation (shipyards like Narsapur Peta; European-style anchors).

(e) Port towns: Madras, Bombay, Calcutta

From tenants to landlords

In Surat and Masulipatnam the English were only tenants sharing rights. In Madras (c.1640), Bombay (c.1660) and Calcutta (c.1690) they were landlords with their own laws, courts and forts. Indian merchants and artisans moved in for safety.

Founders like Francis Day, Aungier and Job Charnock clashed with London directors; local officers rebelled (Winter in Madras, 1665; Keigwin in Bombay, 1679), so town governors got the power to make laws.

Why they mattered (lecture)

New political-commercial spaces: more independent of inland powers, ocean-facing, with commercial law that attracted capital, and bases for growing company power. Roy calls them a major turning point.

Maratha raids in the 1740s made Calcutta a safe haven; its population doubled.

Trade → empire (the "bridge point" of the lecture) Trade did not automatically create empire, but it created the conditions: forts piled up power; companies blurred trade and sovereignty (merchant + state); Mughal decline opened space; port cities became bases for conquest. Violence mattered, but Europeans still depended on Indian brokers, bankers and shipwrights: empire grew through entanglement, not replacement. (And Asian trade before 1500 was not purely peaceful either.)

Discussion: if Europeans depended on Indians, what was new? Not better commerce, but organisation (joint-stock companies, charters), armed shipping and forts, and above all port cities with their own law, which, as the Mughals collapsed, became bases for conquest.

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "European expansion transformed, but did not create, Indian Ocean connectivity." Discuss.

Intro: "Connected space" means structured interaction across distance, not sameness. The Indian Ocean was connected long before 1500.

Before Europe: monsoon trade from Baghdad to Canton with India at the centre (Bernstein); monks on merchant ships (Sen); Tamil temples from Java to Quanzhou (Guy). Chaudhuri shows "unity in the large" across four civilisations from 622, with a shared ground floor of daily life.

Europeans arrive: the Portuguese (1498) used violence and the cartaz, but never fully controlled trade (Steensgaard vs strong Asian states). The EIC and VOC brought joint-stock organisation, but depended on Indian bankers and brokers such as Kasi Viranna and Amirchand (Roy).

Transformation: port towns (Madras, Bombay, Calcutta) with their own law; Mughal decline after 1707; Plassey and Buxar turned trade into empire.

But: connection was unequal (force, slavery, coerced weavers). "Connected for whom?"

Conclusion: Europe changed who held power, but the network itself was much older.
