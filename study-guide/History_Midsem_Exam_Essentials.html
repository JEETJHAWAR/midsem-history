SESSION1

Why Study History?

Hobsbawm, "The Social Function of the Past" (1972) + On History ch. 3 (via the PPT)
In one lineEvery society uses its past. The question is not whether it does, but how: as a pattern to copy, a model to choose from, or a process that leads to today.

Who: Eric Hobsbawm, a British Marxist historian. Key quote: "The past is a permanent dimension of human consciousness." In simple words, no person or society can escape its past; we live inside it "as fish do in water".

Picture it: three ways a society uses its past

The faster a society changes, the more it moves from left to right.

Key ideas

1. Past as PATTERN

In traditional societies, each generation copies the last one. Custom is law.

But they are not frozen. Hobsbawm calls "static traditional society" a myth. Change happens quietly, hidden inside old forms.

Nomads who traded camels started trading lorries and saw it as the same old business. The new thing was fitted into the old pattern.

2. Past as MODEL

When change becomes too fast, people can no longer copy the past automatically. So they pick parts of it and say "let's return to that".

Key point: loud calls to "go back" show that the old way has already broken.

Zapata (Mexico): peasants of Morelos fought to restore their old village lands. They could not truly go back, and their fight turned into a revolution that created a new Mexico.

3. Restoration: effective vs symbolic

Effective = trying to bring back the real old practices. Symbolic = bringing back only the feeling or symbol of the past.

Effective: "bring back old-time religion" (everyone in church). Symbolic: rebuilding Warsaw's old town after WWII, or pulling down the Stalin statue in Prague.

4. Invented (fabricated) past

If the past is very old, it becomes a story used for today's goals. It can be partly made up but still powerful. Nationalism is the best example: nations "get their history wrong" (Renan).

The new country "Ghana" took the name of an old empire from a different place. Indian nationalists praised a "golden age" (Session 4).

5. Past as PROCESS (modern societies)

Modern people like change ("new" = "better"). The past still matters, now as the story of how we got here (development, progress).

Paradox: the more things change, the more we need history to understand change.

People accept new technology (phones, bicycles) easily but resist social change (family, morals).

6. Genealogy (finding ancestors)

New groups want heroic ancestors to look legitimate.

Revolutionaries claiming Spartacus as an ancestor; schoolbooks teaching national heroes like Joan of Arc.

What history CAN do (On History)

Show where today's institutions came from; separate what is really new from what is old; expose invented traditions. It explains how the present was made.
vs

What history CANNOT do

Predict the future exactly; give ready-made lessons or a political programme. "History ceases to be of use at the very moment when we need it most."
Hobsbawm · Past & Present, 1972Zapata (Mexico)Warsaw = symbolic restoration"myth of vulgar social science"
Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. Explain Hobsbawm's idea of the past as pattern, model and process.

Hobsbawm (1972) argues that "the past is a permanent dimension of human consciousness": every society uses its past, but in different ways depending on how fast it changes.

In traditional societies the past is a pattern: people copy inherited customs. Yet these societies are not static. Change is quietly absorbed, as when nomads treated lorries as just another form of their camel trade.

When change speeds up, the past becomes a model, chosen on purpose. Calls for "restoration" show that continuity has already broken. Zapata's peasants tried to restore their old lands but ended up making a revolution. Restoration can be effective or only symbolic, like rebuilding Warsaw after WWII.

In modern societies the past becomes a process, the story of progress that explains the present.

But the past is often selected or even invented for present needs, as in nationalism. History cannot predict the future, but it helps us understand how the present was made.

Historical Methods & Sources

Matthew Lange, Comparative-Historical Methods (2013), ch. 1 · Charlotte Backerra, "Source criticism for cultural history" (2024)
In one lineHistorians work like detectives: they collect sources, check whether they can be trusted, interpret what they mean, and explain why things happened, often by comparing cases.

Picture it: how a historian works

1. Heuristics
Find sources
"What survives?"
→
2. Criticism
Check them
"Is it genuine? Is it biased?"
→
3. Interpretation
Understand meaning
"What did it mean then?"
→
4. Synthesis / Presentation
Write the story
"How does it all fit?"

These are Droysen's 4 steps (1868): heuristic → source analysis → interpretation → presentation.

Key ideas

Source vs evidence

A source is anything left from the past. It becomes evidence only when a historian uses it, after checking it, to answer a question.

An old coin is a source; when you use it to prove that a king ruled in 300 CE, it becomes evidence.

Primary / secondary / tertiary

Primary = made at the time. Secondary = later analysis. Tertiary = summaries.

Primary: Xuanzang's travel diary, an inscription. Secondary: a historian's book about Xuanzang. Tertiary: an encyclopedia entry.

Source criticism

External: is it genuine (paper, ink, handwriting)? Internal: who wrote it, for whom, when, why?

James Mill (S4) never visited India and trusted hostile reports, which is a failure of source criticism.

Common mistakes to avoid

Anachronism = judging the past by today's ideas. Whig history / teleology = telling the past as a straight march towards today.

Calling an ancient king "anti-democratic" judges him by modern values.

Lange: comparative-historical analysis (CHA) Must know

What it is: explaining big social changes (revolutions, states, nationalism, capitalism) by combining two things:

Within-case (the "historical")

Look deep inside one case over time and trace step by step how A led to B (process tracing).How exactly did the French monarchy collapse?
+

Comparison (the "comparative")

Look across several cases to find what they share and where they differ.Compare the French, Russian and Chinese revolutions.

Its 4 defining elements

  1. Within-case methods
  2. Comparative methods
  3. Believes real causes can be found (social-science outlook)
  4. Studies big units: states, classes, economies (Tilly: "big structures, large processes, huge comparisons")

Two kinds of explanation

Ideographic = explains one unique case. Nomothetic = finds general laws for many cases. CHA sits in between.

Bendix (Kings or People): every country's path was unique (ideographic). Skocpol: revolutions happen when the state breaks down and peasants can rise up (more general).

Backerra: source criticism for cultural history Must know

Argument: the basic method (Ranke and Droysen) is about 150 years old and still works. But cultural historians ask new questions: not just "what happened?" but "what did it mean?" They read symbols, ceremony, language, space and emotions. The last step, presentation, is still not thought about enough.

Backerra's example: the same letter answers different questions depending on what you ask.
Droysen 4 steps (1868)Ranke: archives, source criticismLange: CHABackerra: cultural historySkocpol / Bendix
Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. "Lange shows how historians explain; Backerra shows how they know." Discuss.

Historical method is both a science of evidence and an art of interpretation, and the two readings cover each side.

Lange explains how historians build causal explanations through comparative-historical analysis. It combines deep within-case study (process tracing) with comparison across cases, and focuses on big units like states and classes. For example, Skocpol compared revolutions and found they needed both a weak state and peasants able to revolt. CHA sits between unique (ideographic) and general (nomothetic) explanation.

Backerra explains how historians decide whether evidence can be trusted, through source criticism (Droysen's heuristic, analysis, interpretation, presentation). She shows that cultural historians use the same method but ask new questions about meaning: in diplomatic letters, wide margins showed rank, and the choice of language showed status.

But both have limits. Comparison can over-generalise, and presentation is still neglected. Together they form the full workflow: find sources → check them → compare → explain.

SESSION3

Global vs Regional History (Scale)

Thomas Hall (ed.), Comparing Globalizations, chs. 1 & 8 + Lecture 3
In one lineGlobal history asks how places are connected; regional history asks how a place works inside. Good history uses both, because the scale you choose changes the story.

Picture it: zooming in and out

The same event looks different at each ring. No single scale is enough.

Key ideas

Wallerstein's "world-system"

From the 1500s the world economy became one system with a core (rich, skilled production), a semi-periphery and a periphery (raw materials, cheap labour).

"A world-system is not the system of the world": it is a system that is a world in itself, often smaller than the whole globe.

Europe as core, colonies as periphery. (He put South Asia in the semi-periphery; critics like Bayly called this Eurocentric.)

Hall: MULTIPLE globalizations

There was not one globalization that began recently. There were many, with deep histories going back thousands of years.

Why care? "How can one tell what is new if one does not know what is old?"

Trade across the Indian Ocean (S7) was a globalization 1,000 years before the internet.

The big debate: one system or many?

Frank: ONE world system growing for 5,000 years. Chase-Dunn & Hall: MANY world-systems at different times and places.

One family tree vs many separate families that sometimes intermarry.

Comparison depends on purpose

You can compare apples and oranges if you are talking about fruit. What you compare depends on your question.

Compare the British and American empires to ask whether America is "exceptional" (Julian Go says it is not).

Nation borders mislead (Hall ch. 8)

"India", "China" or "Bangladesh" did not exist in their modern form in the past. So think in nation, region and globe.

Bengal's history spans today's India and Bangladesh (S8).

Why India is the perfect test case

India has many regions inside it and links outside (Africa, Arabia, Southeast Asia). The Indian Ocean is where regional and global meet.

Example to memorise: Vasco da Gama (1498) at 3 scales

ScaleHow his arrival at Calicut looks
NationalThe start of Europeans in India, which later led to colonial rule
RegionalJust a new player in an ocean already full of Gujarati, Arab and Chetti merchants (a Gujarati pilot even guided him)
GlobalA new sea route around Africa (the Cape) that bypassed the old Red Sea and Venetian route

Lesson: Europe changed an already-connected world; it did not create connection.

Wallerstein: core/peripheryHall: multiple globalizationsFrank: one systemChase-Dunn & Hall: many systems
Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. Why should global and regional history be read together? Use India as an example.

Global history studies how places are connected (trade, migration, empire), while regional history studies how a place works internally (local society and culture). Each alone is weak: global history can become too abstract, and regional history too isolated.

Hall argues there were multiple globalizations with long histories, not one modern one, and Wallerstein showed how a world-system links a core and a periphery. But such big models need regional detail to test them.

India shows why both are needed. It contains many regions (Gujarat, Bengal, Malabar), yet it was linked to Africa, Arabia and China through the Indian Ocean. Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1498 looks like the start of colonialism at the national scale, but at the regional scale he was only a new player in a crowded ocean.

So history is strongest when it moves across scales: modern nation borders mislead us about the past, and we must ask both "how was it connected?" and "how did it work inside?"

SESSION4

Indian Schools of Historical Thought

E. Sreedharan, A Textbook of Historiography, chs. 14–16 + Lecture 4 (Said, Cambridge School). Very likely essay topic.
In one lineIndian history has been written by different groups, each answering the one before: Orientalists recovered ancient India, colonial writers called it backward, nationalists defended it, Marxists looked at economy and class, and Subaltern historians looked at ordinary people.

Picture it: the chain of schools (learn this order)

Each school reacted to the one above it. The Cambridge School and the Subaltern school both challenged nationalist history, from opposite sides.

1. Orientalists (Indology) Must know

Problem they solved: the British thought ancient India had "no history" (no proper written history books). So history had to be rebuilt from texts, languages, inscriptions, coins and ruins.

What they achieved

  • William Jones founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784).
  • He found that Sanskrit is related to Greek, Latin and English (the Indo-European language family).
  • He matched the Greek "Sandrocottas" with Chandragupta Maurya, giving the first firm date, 325 BC (the "sheet anchor" of Indian chronology).
  • Prinsep read the Brahmi script of Ashoka's inscriptions (1834); Cunningham started archaeology (ASI).

The problems

  • From "shared language" they jumped to a shared "Aryan race", which was later used to justify British (white) superiority and an Aryan–Dravidian divide.
  • Exaggeration ("Sanskrit more perfect than Greek").
  • Edward Said (Orientalism, 1978): this knowledge was also power. It helped Britain classify and rule India. Translating Hindu law books helped British courts govern Indians; Jones himself was a judge.

2. Colonial / imperialist history Must know

James Mill, History of British India (1818)

He never visited India, knew no Indian language, and used only hostile sources. He called Hindu society one of the "rudest" states of mind.

He split history into Hindu, Muslim and British periods (still used today). His book was the textbook for British officers at Haileybury College.

A perfect example of bad source criticism (S2).

The colonial "story" in 3 myths

  1. Static India: it never changed.
  2. Oriental despotism: cruel kings and powerless villages.
  3. So Britain brings progress, which justifies colonial rule.

Others: Elphinstone (fairer, "Tacitus of India"), Elliot & Dowson (attacked Muslim rule), Vincent Smith (textbook writer).

3. Nationalist history Must know

What they did (strengths)

  • Defended Indian civilisation and showed a "golden age".
  • K.P. Jayaswal, Hindu Polity (1918): ancient India had republics, not just despots.
  • R.C. Dutt and Naoroji: the drain of wealth to Britain, which began economic history.
  • Started regional history (e.g. south India by Nilakanta Sastri).
but

Problems (weaknesses)

  • Exaggeration and chauvinism: e.g. claims of aeroplanes in the epics.
  • Used sources "like a lawyer trying to win a case" (Basham on Jayaswal).
  • Thapar: glorified the past as "compensation for a humiliating present".
  • Hindu-centred, which fed communal history.

4. Marxist "new history" Must know

Big idea: not new sources, but new questions about the old ones: Who produced what? Who owned land? Who worked for whom?

D.D. Kosambi (the founder)

History = "successive developments in the means and relations of production", i.e. tools, and who controls work.

The iron plough cleared forests, tribes broke up, and caste grew as a new way to organise farm labour.

R.S. Sharma · Romila Thapar · Irfan Habib

Sharma: "Indian feudalism" (land grants to Brahmins and officials, declining towns). Thapar: Ashoka's dhamma was a social and political policy; no massive "Aryan invasion". Habib: Mughal peasants were crushed by the land-revenue demand.

Why it was an advance

From kings and battles to ordinary people, the economy and society; used archaeology and other disciplines; destroyed the "static India" and "Aryan race" myths.
but

Criticism

Class can become a "master explanation" for everything, ignoring religion, culture and ideas; heavy jargon; Marxists themselves disagreed.

5. Cambridge School vs Subaltern Studies Must know

Cambridge School (Anil Seal, Gallagher)

The freedom movement was mainly Indian elites competing for power, jobs and patronage, through local politics and factions.Critics: this ignores ideals and mass anger against British rule.
vs

Subaltern Studies (Ranajit Guha, 1982)

"Subaltern" (from Gramsci) = people of low rank. Peasants, workers and tribals had their own politics; they were not just followers of leaders.Peasant revolts in Awadh; how peasants saw Gandhi as a miracle-worker (Shahid Amin).

Guha's point: both colonial and nationalist history are "elitist". One says the British made India; the other says Indian leaders freed it. Neither gives credit to ordinary people.

Write it like this: the professor's essay (15 marks)
Q. Was the shift from colonial and nationalist history to Marxist "new history" a major advance?

Intro: Each school of Indian history reflected the politics of its time (Hobsbawm: the past is used for present needs).

Colonial history (James Mill, 1818) described India as static and despotic to justify British rule, and used biased sources. Nationalist history (Jayaswal's republics, Dutt's drain of wealth) answered with pride and opened economic history, but often exaggerated and glorified a "golden age".

Marxist history was an advance because it asked new questions of old sources: production, land and class (Kosambi on the plough and caste; Sharma on feudalism; Thapar on Ashoka). It moved history from kings to ordinary people and used archaeology and other disciplines.

But it could make class a rigid master key, ignoring culture and religion. The Cambridge School stressed elite politics, and Subaltern Studies (Guha) argued that even Marxists missed peasants' own politics.

Conclusion: it was a real advance in method and scope, but not the final word. History keeps being rewritten.

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. How useful are foreign travellers' accounts as sources for Indian history?

Where Indian records are scarce, foreign accounts are very useful, but travel writing mixes observation, memory, interpretation and opinion, so it is both evidence and interpretation.

The Chinese monks were eyewitnesses. Faxian described Gupta India and his sea voyage home; Xuanzang described Harsha's Kanauj and Nalanda and brought back 657 texts; Yijing recorded monastery life and studied at Srivijaya. They also show monks travelling with merchants. But they saw India through a Buddhist lens.

Al-Shahrastani systematically classified Indian religions, but he never visited India, knew no Sanskrit, relied on older Arabic books and made errors (e.g. "Brahma" as a man). Al-Biruni, who learned Sanskrit and used original texts, is more reliable.

So historians must corroborate these accounts with inscriptions, coins and archaeology. Used critically, they also show that India was connected to Asia, not isolated.

SESSION7

Baghdad to Canton: Trade in the Medieval Indian Ocean

William Bernstein, A Splendid Exchange, ch. 4 (2008) + Lecture 7
In one lineLong before Europeans came, the Indian Ocean was a busy "premodern globalisation" from East Africa to China, run by the monsoon winds, merchants and shared rules, with India at the centre.

Picture it: the monsoon "timetable"

Winds reverse each season, so merchants sailed in segments (stages). Goods from Baghdad to Canton usually changed ships at least 3 times, often in India.

Key ideas

A "system", not random trips

Trade was regular and lasting. It moved goods (cotton, pepper, silk, horses, porcelain), people (merchants, pilgrims, judges), ideas (Islam, law) and institutions (taxes, credit, contracts).

A Gujarati merchant sails to Malacca with cloth, swaps it for spices and porcelain, and returns on the next monsoon.

Islam as "trade infrastructure"

From Cairo to Malacca, Muslim merchants followed the same law and customs, which built trust at a distance. The hajj connected the whole network.

Customs duty: Muslims 2.5%, Christians and Jews 5%, others 10%. Southeast Asia became Muslim through merchants who married locally, not through armies.

Why India was central (not just geography)

  1. Middle-man: ships from the west and east met at Indian ports
  2. Producer: cotton cloth ("if spices were glamorous, cloth was indispensable") and Malabar pepper
  3. Gujarat / Cambay: expert merchants (even the Portuguese Tomé Pires praised them)
  4. Cultural carrier: Indian Muslim traders spread Islam east

Malacca: success through good rules

Founded c.1400 by Parameswara. Low taxes, honest courts, transparent prices drew traders; Pires counted 84 languages spoken there.

Asia's Singapore of the 1400s: success came from good institutions, not only location.

The story in 4 quick stages

StageWhat happened (just remember the bold)
1. 7th–9th c.Tang China + Abbasid Baghdad (750): direct sea trade Basra → India → Canton. In 878 Huang Chao's rebels massacred foreign merchants at Canton.
2. 13th–14th c.Mongol period. Ibn Battuta (Moroccan judge) worked in Delhi under Muhammad bin Tughluq; he travelled the whole Muslim world.
3. 1405–1433Zheng He's huge Chinese fleets (7 voyages) reached India and Africa, then China stopped sea voyages.
4. c.1500Malacca at its height (Tomé Pires) → captured by the Portuguese in 1511.
Sources: each one has its own angle (source criticism again) Akhbar al-Sin wa'l-Hind (c.851, Arab merchants): route details. · Marvels of India (10th c.): mixes real sailing life with fantasy. · Marco Polo: accurate but likes marvels. · Ibn Battuta: a Muslim judge, little interest in trade. · Tomé Pires (1511): precise numbers, admired Asian trade.
Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. Why was India central to the Indian Ocean trading system before 1500?

Bernstein shows that the medieval Indian Ocean was a form of premodern globalisation, a regular system linking East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia and China. India was at its centre.

First, because of the monsoon, voyages were done in segments, and ships from the west and east met at Indian ports. India was the middle-man, where goods were sorted, taxed and re-shipped. Second, India was a major producer: cotton textiles bought across Asia, and Malabar pepper. Third, Gujarati merchants of Cambay were so skilled that the Portuguese Tomé Pires told his countrymen to learn from them. Fourth, Indian Muslim traders spread Islam to Southeast Asia through trade and marriage.

But geography alone does not explain it: good ports, fair taxes and shared Islamic commercial law mattered too (as at Malacca).

So Europe entered an already-connected world; it did not create it.

SESSION8

Silk Route Connections I: Two Lenses on the Silk Road

Valerie Hansen, The Silk Road, ch. 5 (Chang'an) · Jayati Bhattacharya, "Old Routes, New Dreams" (2021, Bengal–China) + Lecture 8
In one lineThere was no single "Silk Road" but many routes. Hansen looks through a city (Chang'an); Bhattacharya looks through a region (Bengal). The lens you pick decides which Silk Road you see.
Start here: what is the "Silk Road"? The name was invented in 1877 by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen. He drew it as one line from China to Europe. In reality it was a web of many routes, few people travelled the whole way, and goods passed from hand to hand. Much more than silk moved: people, religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism), ideas and many goods.

Hansen: the CITY lens

Chang'an (modern Xi'an), capital of the Tang dynasty, was a cosmopolitan city of about 1 million people, home to foreigners, monks, merchants and refugees.

Main point: few goods travelled the whole way; people and ideas mattered more.

Evidence: archaeology (tombs, hoards, documents).

vs

Bhattacharya: the REGION lens

Bengal was not a "periphery" (edge) but a hub linking rivers, mountains and sea to China and Southeast Asia (the "Southern Silk Road").

Main point: centres and peripheries are made by politics, not by nature.

Evidence: trade goods like cotton and cowrie shells.

Hansen: Chang'an (key examples)

Sogdians (Central Asian traders)

Merchants from Samarkand who settled in China. Their leaders, called sabao, were headmen of foreign communities.

The tomb of An Jia (d. 579) is Chinese in shape but has a Zoroastrian fire altar: mixing of cultures ("hybridity").

Hejiacun hoard (treasure found in 1970)

Over 1,000 items, but only a few imported ones; most were made in China by migrant craftsmen.

This proves Hansen's point: skills and people travelled more than goods. Fewer than 50 Roman coins have been found in all of China.

Rise and fall

Chang'an grew under the Tang, then suffered: the An Lushan rebellion (755), followed by attacks on Sogdians; foreign religions banned (843–845); rebels burned the city. Trade shifted to the sea.

Everyday life too

Pawnshop tickets show ordinary people (a quarter of them women) pawning clothes, not grand luxury trade.

Bhattacharya: Bengal (key examples)

Cotton, not just silk ("Cotton Road")

Bengal's fine cotton cloth (muslin) was sold to China for centuries.

So why do we say "Silk Road" and not "Cotton Road"? Because Bengal was later imagined as a "periphery".

Cowrie shells (money)

Small shells used as cheap money from the Maldives through Bengal to Yunnan in China. Where cowries spread shows how far the Southern Silk Road reached.

Faxian, Xuanzang and Ibn Battuta mention them; in Bengal a chicken cost about 448 cowries.

Colonial rule changed the routes

The British restructured trade (railways, tea, new borders). Then 1947 (Partition) and the 1962 India–China war closed old routes, and Bengal became a "border" zone.

Today: old routes, new politics

China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI, 2013) and India's Act East Policy (2014) both use the memory of old routes, but selectively, for their own goals.

This is Hobsbawm's "use of the past" (S1) in action.
Big lessons (use in any Silk Road answer)
  1. No single road: many routes, by land, river and sea.
  2. Scale matters: a city view and a regional view show different things (S3).
  3. Centres and peripheries are made: Bengal was called "peripheral" only from Delhi's or London's point of view.
  4. Connectivity changes over time: it grows, shrinks, reroutes and gets "revived" for politics.

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. "There is no single Silk Road." Discuss with reference to Hansen and Bhattacharya.

The term "Silk Road" was coined by Richthofen in 1877 as one line from China to Europe, but the readings show a web of routes carrying people, religions and many goods.

Hansen uses a city lens. Through archaeology in Chang'an (the Sogdian tomb of An Jia, the Hejiacun hoard) she shows a cosmopolitan city where foreigners mixed cultures, but where few goods travelled long distances. People, skills and ideas mattered more. The city declined after the An Lushan rebellion, and trade moved to the sea.

Bhattacharya uses a regional lens. Bengal was a hub linking rivers, mountains and sea, with trade in cotton and cowrie shells reaching Yunnan. It was later made "peripheral" by colonial borders, Partition and the 1962 war.

Today, China's BRI and India's Act East use the Silk Road memory selectively, a political use of the past. So the Silk Road was multiple and changing, and what we see depends on our lens.

SESSION9

Silk Route Connections II: Buddhism Across the Sea

Tansen Sen, "Buddhism and the Maritime Crossings" (2014) + Lecture 9
In one lineBuddhism also travelled from India to China by sea, carried by monks on merchant ships. Trade and religion helped each other, and Sri Lanka and Srivijaya became key Buddhist centres on the way.

Key idea 1: why "Maritime Silk Road" is a misleading name

Sen's 3 objections

  1. Silk was not the main cargo at sea. It was beads, pearls and precious stones, and later incense, pepper, spices and porcelain.
  2. It makes China look like the centre, but Indian, Southeast Asian and Arab ships mattered just as much. China became active at sea only from the 10th century.
  3. It ignores Southeast Asia, whose sailors had linked the seas since about 2500 BCE.

Sen's alternative

See maritime Asia as a multi-centred world (many centres), not a "Chinese road".

And stop arguing over whether Buddhism reached China first by land or by sea: the evidence shows both. Land and sea complemented each other.

Key idea 2: monks + merchants

Lecture: religion did not just "go along with" trade; it became part of trade itself.

Example: the ship-owner Nandin (5th c.)

An Indian ship-owner who carried the monk Gunavarman and groups of Sri Lankan nuns to China, and helped translate texts.

But not always friendly

Sen adds that some merchants were hostile.

In a storm (411), "Brahmin" merchants on Faxian's ship wanted to throw him off as bad luck. His patron saved him.

Key idea 3: Southeast Asia was first just a "transit zone"

1st–4th c.
Just a stop-over: ships changed crews and took supplies. No monasteries yet.
→
5th c.
First Buddhist inscription, by the sea captain Buddhagupta (Kedah, Malaysia)
→
7th–13th c.
Srivijaya (Sumatra) becomes a big Buddhist learning centre
A flight stop-over: Buddhism flew "direct" from India to China (long-distance transmission, Zürcher's idea). The places in between only became Buddhist later.

Key idea 4: Srivijaya and Sri Lanka as hubs

Srivijaya (Palembang, Sumatra)

  • Yijing: over 1,000 monks there; Chinese monks should study there 1–2 years before going to India.
  • Atisa, an Indian monk, went there to learn (1012), then went to Tibet: knowledge flowed back to India too.
  • Its kings gave gifts to Nalanda and Nagapattinam (S10).
&

Sri Lanka

  • Not just the home of Theravada: also Tantric Buddhism and relics.
  • Kings wanted its relics for power: Kublai Khan sent an envoy (1284); Zheng He (1410) probably wanted the tooth relic.
  • Its monastery design was copied in Java (the Ratu Boko inscription, 792), adapted locally.

Strengths of Sen's article

Connected history, not separate national ones; the sea as an active space; uses many kinds of evidence (archaeology, inscriptions, texts); land and sea seen as complementary.
but

Limitations (lecture)

Relies heavily on Chinese texts; Southeast Asians appear only as middle-men, with little local agency; treats all merchants as one group; women are almost absent.
Old vs new view of the Silk Roads (lecture table, in short) Old: one road, over land, luxury goods, China–Europe, centred on states and empires. → New: many roads, land and sea, people and ideas too, many centres, networks.
Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. How did trade and religion support each other in the maritime spread of Buddhism? (Sen)

Tansen Sen argues that Buddhism reached China by sea as well as by land, and that this spread was closely tied to trade. He also rejects the label "maritime Silk Road", because silk was not the main cargo and the label makes China the centre.

Merchants gave monks passage on ships, funded monasteries and carried relics and texts. Monasteries gave merchants lodging, trust and blessings, and Buddhist rituals such as offering the "seven jewels" created demand for pearls and coral. The ship-owner Nandin, who carried Gunavarman and Sri Lankan nuns to China, shows this link. But relations were not always friendly: merchants on Faxian's ship wanted to abandon him in a storm.

At first Southeast Asia was only a transit zone. Later Srivijaya (where Yijing found 1,000 monks) and Sri Lanka (relics and Tantric teaching) became hubs, and knowledge even flowed back to India (Atisa).

Limitation: Sen relies mainly on Chinese sources. Still, he shows that religion became part of trade itself.

SESSION10

Religions in India: Encounters, Adaptations, Travels

T.N. Madan, India's Religions (Introduction) · John Guy, "Tamil Merchants and the Hindu-Buddhist Diaspora" · H.P. Ray, "A 'Chinese' Pagoda at Nagapattinam" + Lecture 10. The professor's model short answer comes from here.
In one lineReligion in India is plural, diverse inside, and lived (not just texts). When people travelled across the ocean, religion travelled with them and changed on the way.

Part 1: How to think about religion in India (Madan + lecture)

Plurality ≠ Pluralism

Plurality = a fact: many religions exist. Pluralism = a value or practice: people actively live together with difference.

A city with a temple, a mosque and a church has plurality. Neighbours attending each other's festivals is pluralism.

Every religion is diverse inside

No religion is one single thing. Madan calls Hinduism a "federation of faiths": no single founder or book.

Hinduism: Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta, bhakti. Islam: Sunni, Shia, Sufi orders. Sikhism: Khalsa and Sahajdhari.

Doctrine vs lived religion

Doctrine = what holy texts and priests say. Lived religion = what people actually do: food, festivals, pilgrimage, shrines.

Texts may draw sharp lines, but Hindus and Muslims both visit Sufi shrines; the Mapillas of Kerala kept local customs.

Religion is mixed into society

Religion connects with caste, family, gender, economy and politics.

A temple is also an employer, a bank, a centre of power and a marker of identity.

Dharma is not "religion"

The Western word "religion" means a private belief. Dharma means duty, moral order and a whole way of life, covering law, family and society.

Colonial census (from 1871) hardened identities

The British used a Christian model of religion (one God, one book, clear membership) and put everyone into fixed boxes in the census.

Fluid identities (e.g. people who were partly Hindu and partly Muslim in practice) became "Hindu or Muslim or Sikh".

Religions born in India

Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism
+

Religions that came from outside

Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Zoroastrianism (Parsis), Bahá'í

Census 1991 (Madan): Hindus about 82%, Muslims about 12%, Christians 2.3%, Sikhs 2%, Buddhists 0.8%, Jains 0.4%. Islam came first through traders (Kerala coast), then conquest (Sind 712); it spread mostly peacefully, through Sufis and trade.

Did India's plurality produce pluralism? (Madan)

Traditional "pluralism" was a ranking

The Rig Veda says "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names", but traditional Hinduism tolerated others by ranking them below itself.

Vivekananda: all religions are true, but Vedanta is the highest. The Arya Samaj rejected other religions (exclusivist).

Most pluralist: Gandhi

Gandhi said all religions are equally true and equally imperfect, and he refused to rank them. He drew on the Jain idea of anekantavada (truth has many sides).

Also Dara Shikoh (Mughal prince) called Hinduism and Islam "twin brothers".
Two kinds of pluralism (Madan's key conclusion) Folk pluralism = ordinary people naturally living with difference in everyday life. Intellectual or secular pluralism = an idea promoted by elites and the state (e.g. sarva dharma samabhava, "equal respect for all religions"). Madan says there is a gap between the two.

Part 2: Religion on the move (Guy + Ray)

Old view: "diffusion"

India → Southeast Asia, one way. India gives, others copy.
→

New view: "circulation"

India ↔ Sri Lanka ↔ Southeast Asia ↔ China, in many directions, and religion changes as it moves.

Who carried religion? Merchants (created the movement) · Brahmins (rituals for kings) · Buddhist monks (monasteries, teaching) · Kings (patronage) · Artisans (statues). Pattern: merchant → settlement → community → temple.

John Guy: Tamil merchants carried Hindu and Buddhist culture

Purnavarman (West Java, c.450)

A local king wrote a Sanskrit inscription with Vishnu's footprints, which were also his own.

India was not simply copied: he used Vishnu to make himself look powerful. Indian ideas + local politics = something new.

Inscriptions mark the trade routes

Kutei (Borneo, 4th c.): the earliest Indian-style inscriptions. Klong Thom (Thailand): a goldsmith's stone with the earliest Tamil inscription. Takuapa (9th c.): a temple tank built by Tamil merchant guilds.

Tamils in China

By 750 Guangzhou had 3 monasteries for Indian Brahmins. In Quanzhou (1281) a Tamil–Chinese inscription dedicates a Shiva temple; its pillars were later reused in a Chinese Buddhist temple.

Merchant guilds

Groups like the Ainnurruvar and Manigramam traded overseas, had their own guards, and paid for temples.

H.P. Ray: Nagapattinam, one port with many religions

The Buddhist monastery (Cudamanivihara)

Built at Nagapattinam (Tamil coast) by the king of Srivijaya (Sumatra) with land given by the Chola kings (c.1005). Demolished in 1867.

Hundreds of Buddha bronzes were found. Some Buddha statues were carried in temple processions like Hindu gods, showing religions borrowing from each other.

A multi-religious coast

Nagapattinam also had a Shiva temple (which received gifts from Srivijaya and gold from China), later a Sufi dargah (Nagore) and a church (Velankanni).

Ray: not "first Buddhism, then Hinduism" but many religions side by side. This also challenges the old idea (Cunningham's) that Buddhism died out in India around the 7th–8th century.
The lecture's overarching argument (memorise) Indian religions became important across Asia not because they stayed the same, but because they could be carried, translated and reinterpreted in new places. Madan → religion is social and plural · Guy → it travelled through merchant networks · Ray → it created multi-religious landscapes.
Write it like this: the professor's short answer (10 marks)
Q. Distinguish religion as doctrine from religion as lived practice in India.

The lecture and Madan argue that religion is more than belief: it moves from belief → practice → experience → social life. So historians must separate doctrine from lived religion.

Doctrine means the official teachings in sacred texts and from religious authorities, such as the Vedas and Manusmriti, the Quran and sharia, or the Sikh Khalsa code. Doctrine tends to draw clear boundaries between religions.

Lived religion is what people actually do: food rules, festivals, pilgrimage, local saints. In practice boundaries are porous. Hindus and Muslims visit the same Sufi shrines, the Mapilla Muslims of Kerala kept local customs, and Madan calls this everyday mixing "folk pluralism". A temple is also an economic and social institution.

The difference matters. The colonial census (from 1871), using a text-based Christian model, put people into fixed boxes and hardened identities. Studying lived religion shows that Indian religions were plural, mixed and changing, as at Nagapattinam, where Buddha images were worshipped like Hindu gods.

The Indian Ocean as a Connected Space

K.N. Chaudhuri, "Unity and Disunity of Indian Ocean History" (1993) · Tirthankar Roy, "The Indian Ocean Trade, 1500–1800" (2012) + Lecture 11
In one lineThe Indian Ocean was connected but not the same everywhere ("unity in the large, disunity in the small"). Europeans entered and changed this working system, and trade slowly turned into empire.

"Connected space" means structured interaction across distance, not sameness. It works through routes (the monsoon), shared trade customs, migration, religious networks, and even war and diplomacy.

Chaudhuri: the BIG picture (622–1750) Must know

Chaudhuri: similar at the big level, different at the small level.

Unity in the large, disunity in the small

Two things can look different up close but similar overall (or the reverse). Word by word, the Rig Veda, the Quran and the Confucian texts differ. But Arab sailors could sail from Basra to China, so as a trade system the ocean was one unit.

Different shops in one big market: each sells different things, but it is still one market.

The "ground floor" of daily life barely changed

Food, clothes and houses stayed similar for centuries (invariance).

Bread zone vs rice zone; the Muslim pilgrim's white unsewn cloth (ihram) has the same ritual meaning as a Brahmin's unstitched dress; dry zones (brick houses, wheat) vs wet zones (bamboo houses, rice, fish ponds).

Time span: 622 to 1750

From the rise of Islam (622) and the Tang dynasty (618) to the Battle of Plassey (1757), a whole "life cycle".

Four big expansions shaped it: Islam, China, Turko-Mongol nomads, and Europe.

A critic (Arasaratnam) wrote "750–1750"; Chaudhuri corrected it to 622.

Unity is partly in the historian's mind

Chaudhuri admits that "one Indian Ocean" is a mental construct: historians choose the unit and period that fit their question.

"Asia" only makes sense when contrasted with "Europe".

1. A crowded world before Europe

India was the middle of the "great arc" of Asian trade, and a producer and market itself: it exported cotton cloth, rice and indigo, and imported silver, gold and horses.

Merchants: Gujarati Muslims (Cambay), Mapillas (Malabar), Chulias and Telugu Chettis (Coromandel). "Europeans entered a crowded, working world, not an empty stage."

2. The Portuguese: violent but limited

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, guided by a Gujarati pilot. Later voyages brought bombardment and massacres. They forced ships to buy passes (cartaz) and held forts (Goa, Hormuz, Malacca).

But they never fully controlled trade and declined after 1640. (Steensgaard: they lived off others' profits instead of making their own.)

3. Companies: power through organisation

English EIC (1600), Dutch VOC (1602), French (1664). What was new: joint-stock money (many investors share risk), monopoly charters, forts and armed ships.

Roy: organisation mattered as much as guns.

4. Europeans depended on Indians

Indian bankers (who minted coins and lent money, e.g. the Jagatseths), brokers (who bought cloth from weavers) and shipbuilders did most of the work. Relations mixed need and distrust.

The broker Amirchand plotted against the nawab Siraj ud-Daula in 1756; Ananda Ranga Pillai's diary describes a broker's hard job.

5. Port towns: Madras, Bombay, Calcutta

Unlike Surat (where they were only tenants), here the English were landlords, with their own laws, courts and forts. Indian merchants moved in for safety.

Madras c.1640, Bombay c.1660, Calcutta c.1690.

6. From trade to empire

After Aurangzeb died (1707), the Mughal empire broke up. The companies fought each other (Carnatic wars) and the English won Bengal: Plassey 1757, Buxar 1764.

Trade did not automatically cause empire, but it created the conditions for it.

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "European expansion transformed, but did not create, Indian Ocean connectivity." Discuss.

Intro: "Connected space" means structured interaction across distance, not sameness. The Indian Ocean was connected long before 1500.

Before Europe: monsoon trade from Baghdad to Canton with India at the centre (Bernstein); monks on merchant ships (Sen); Tamil temples from Java to Quanzhou (Guy). Chaudhuri shows "unity in the large" across four civilisations from 622.

Europeans arrive: the Portuguese (1498) used violence and passes but never fully controlled trade. The EIC and VOC brought joint-stock organisation, but still depended on Indian bankers and brokers (Roy).

Transformation: port towns (Madras, Bombay, Calcutta) with their own law; Mughal decline after 1707; Plassey and Buxar turned trade into empire.

But: connection was unequal (force, slavery, coerced weavers). "Connected for whom?"

Conclusion: Europe changed who held power, but the network itself was much older.
