SESSION6

India through the Eyes of Foreign Travellers: Travel Writing as a Historical Source

Readings: Tansen Sen, "The Travel Records of Chinese Pilgrims Faxian, Xuanzang, and Yijing: Sources for Cross-Cultural Encounters between Ancient China and Ancient India", Education About Asia 11:3 (2006), pp. 24–33 (OT3) · M.S. Khan, "A Twelfth Century Arab Account of Indian Religions and Sects", Arabica 30:2 (1983), pp. 199–208 (OT4)
Where this fits / why it matters Session 4 showed that ancient India seemed to lack "genuine" historical records. So how can historians reconstruct periods with no Indian records? One answer is foreign travellers. This session applies Session 2's source criticism to two traditions: Chinese Buddhist pilgrims (eyewitnesses with a religious purpose) and an Arab scholar (al-Shahrastani, who never came to India). The core lesson: travel writing is both evidence and interpretation. It also opens Part C of the course: travellers reveal a history of connections, not isolation.

6.1 Why study foreign travellers? Must know

Possible sources for India's past: archaeology (physical remains), inscriptions (official records in stone or metal), coins (rulers, dates, trade), literary texts (religious and courtly works), and foreign travellers (outsider accounts of everyday life).

Foreigners often noticed everyday details (diet, dress, city life) that local writers took for granted and rarely recorded, because they were observing an unfamiliar society.

Learning objectives (lecture)

  1. Explain what travel writings are as historical sources.
  2. Identify different motivations behind foreign travel (pilgrimage, trade, faith, curiosity).
  3. Compare Chinese and Arab accounts.
  4. Evaluate their strengths and limitations (eyewitness insight vs bias, language gaps, selective focus).
  5. Appreciate how foreign accounts help reconstruct Indian history where indigenous records are scarce.

What is travel writing? Must know

A description of a society by someone who travelled outside their homeland, blending firsthand experience with the traveller's own outlook. It records geography, economy, cities, religion, customs, trade, political institutions, food and everyday life. It combines four layers:

1 · ObservationWhat the traveller directly witnessed
+
2 · MemoryRecalled later, often years afterward
+
3 · InterpretationFiltered through their own worldview
+
4 · Personal opinionJudgements about people and rulers

Method: how to read a travel account Must know

Travel narratives are not objective mirrors of reality. Historians ask:

QuestionWhy it matters
Why was the traveller visiting?Purpose shapes what they noticed and recorded.
Who was the intended audience?A report for a court differs from private notes (Xuanzang wrote for Emperor Taizong).
What languages did they know?This limits what they could read, ask or verify (al-Biruni knew Sanskrit; al-Shahrastani did not).
What sources did they use?Direct observation, hearsay, or earlier written accounts.
What did they ignore?Absences are as telling as inclusions (al-Shahrastani omits Jainism and the Vedas).
What cultural assumptions shaped them?Their beliefs coloured their judgements (al-Shahrastani's Islamic idea of "prophecy").
Advantages
  • Eyewitness observations: direct testimony often unavailable in Indian sources.
  • Information unavailable elsewhere: details locals had no reason to record.
  • Cross-cultural comparisons: India compared with places they knew.
  • Independent perspective: distance from local politics can mean less bias.
Limitations
  • Language barriers and dependence on interpreters: meaning lost or distorted.
  • Religious bias: faith shaped how customs were judged.
  • Limited geographical experience: most saw only a fraction of the subcontinent.
  • Exaggeration: marvels and dangers overstated.
  • Selective observation: they noticed what struck them, not what was typical.

6.2 The Chinese pilgrims (Tansen Sen) Must know

Sen's framing argument The spread of Buddhism from India to China from the 1st century CE triggered cross-cultural exchanges with a profound impact on Asian and world history, involving missionaries, traders, artisans and medical professionals across Central and Southeast Asia. Chinese pilgrims introduced new texts and doctrines, carried ritual paraphernalia, and wrote detailed accounts. These records created a unique Chinese perception of India, for some a sacred, even Utopian realm, for others a land of "civilized" and sophisticated people. Since Chinese discourse usually described foreigners as uncivilised barbarians, this elevated India's status. The pilgrims' journeys became channels for wider cultural exchange, far beyond the transmission of religious texts alone.

Why these records are valuable (Sen): (1) meticulous accounts of Buddhist doctrines, rituals and monastic institutions in South, Central and Southeast Asia; (2) vital information on social and political conditions in South Asia and the kingdoms on the routes; (3) insight into cross-cultural perceptions and interactions; and (4) evidence of the hardships of long-distance travel, commercial exchange, and the pilgrim–merchant relationship.

Why Chinese pilgrims came (lecture): primary motivation Buddhism, to collect scriptures, visit sacred places linked to the Buddha's life, study Sanskrit, learn from Indian monks, and carry texts back for translation projects in Chinese monasteries.

Faxian (337?–422?) Must know

  • One of the first and perhaps the oldest Chinese monks to travel to India: he left Chang'an in 399, aged over 60, and returned 14 years later, aged about 77.
  • Route: across the Taklamakan desert → Central Asia → India's pilgrimage sites → Sri Lanka → a perilous sea voyage back via Southeast Asia.
  • Purpose (stated in the opening of A Record of the Buddhist Kingdoms): to obtain Vinaya texts (monastic rules). Texts like the Lotus Sutra had been translated by the 3rd–4th centuries, but Vinaya texts, essential for running monasteries, were scarce. (Vinaya + Sutras + Abhidharma = the Tripitaka, the "three baskets".)
  • In Central Asia: in Loulan, people dressed like Chinese but followed Indian customs, and monks read Indian books; in Khotan, every family had a small stupa before its door and monasteries hosted travelling monks.
  • Highlights Buddhist practices and legends: at Taxila, the Buddha (in a previous life) gave his body to a starving tigress; conception at Kapilavastu, birth at Lumbini, nirvana at Kushinagara.
  • Relic veneration: the Buddha's alms-bowl at Peshawar, the tooth relic ceremony in Sri Lanka led by the king. These records spread similar ceremonies to China and created demand for relics.
  • Monks and merchants were mutually beneficial: monks hitchhiked on merchant caravans and ships; traders profited from new demand for ritual goods; monasteries gave traders lodging and health care, and traders gave donations.
  • In 408/409 he sailed on a merchant ship from Tamralipti (eastern India) to Sri Lanka, stayed about two years, then sailed home through Southeast Asia: proof of maritime channels linking India and China, and that sea travel was perilous and navigation rudimentary.
  • A Record of the Buddhist Kingdoms was the first eyewitness account in Chinese of Buddhist practice and pilgrimage sites in Central and South Asia. It was written in the third person, apparently by someone to whom he recounted it (translated by James Legge).
  • Borderland complex (Antonino Forte): Chinese clergy felt melancholy at living in the "borderland", far from the Buddha's land. His companion Daozheng stayed in India: "I wish that I not be reborn in the borderland."
  • Impact on Chinese views of India: Li Daoyuan (d. 527), commenting on the Shui jing, wrote, using Faxian, that south of Mathura is Middle India (Madhyadesha), whose people are rich and "dress and eat like people in China", which was high praise in Chinese terms. (Wordplay: both used Zhongguo, "Middle Kingdom", for Middle India and for China.) This view of India as civilised lasted until about the 10th century.

Lecture summary: Faxian's main interests were Buddhist monasteries, Vinaya texts, pilgrimage centres and maritime travel. His historical contributions: one of the earliest outside accounts of early Gupta India; Buddhist institutions; trade routes linking India, Central Asia and China; Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.

Xuanzang (600?–664): "the greatest Chinese traveller to India" Must know

  • A leading Indophile who promoted Buddhism and the image of India as a holy land, and lobbied his patrons, Tang emperors Taizong (r. 626–49) and Gaozong (r. 649–83), for diplomatic exchange.
  • His book, The Records of the Western Regions Visited During the Great Tang Dynasty, was written for his royal patrons as much as for the clergy, so he appears as both a pious pilgrim and a diplomat for Tang China. (His biography by Huili and Yancong was completed in 664.)
  • Context: by 627 Buddhism was rooted in China; almost all basic texts had been translated; Chinese schools (e.g. Tiantai) were emerging; China transmitted Buddhism to Korea and Japan.
  • Motives: visit sacred sites; dissatisfaction with Chinese translations: "When words are wrong, the meaning is lost, and when a phrase is mistaken, the doctrine becomes distorted." He wanted original texts and Indian teachers.
  • Success: brought back 657 texts and is one of the three best translators in ancient China. Emperor Taizong built the Big (Great) Wild Goose Pagoda in Chang'an to house them.
  • He left without official authorisation, which may explain why he sought audiences with foreign rulers (protection, or to impress Taizong). This gives rare insight into the politics of Central and South Asian courts.
  • Noted Indic influence in Central Asia: Agni, Kucha and Khotan used modified Indic scripts.
  • Fascicle two, a general survey of India: the correct Chinese name for India should be "Yindu" (still used); geography, climate, measurement, concepts of time, urban life and architecture, caste, Brahmin education, Buddhist teaching, law and economy, social norms, eating habits, natural and manufactured products.
  • Fascicle five: Kanauj, capital of Harshavardhana, reached in 637/638. Harsha's empire stretched from north-west Bengal to the river Beas and had restored peace and prosperity to north India for the first time since the Guptas. Harsha had heard of a "compassionate" ruler in China; Xuanzang praised Taizong (reduced taxes, mild punishments). This meeting led to diplomatic relations between Kanauj and the Tang court, acknowledged by Tang official scribes. His motive may have been that the pilgrimage sites and Nalanda lay in Harsha's empire.
  • Borderland dialogue at Nalanda: monks asked why he would return to China, "a borderland where… the Dharma [is] despised; the Buddhas are never born in that country". He replied that the Dharma must be spread, and that China was civilised, with laws and cultured people. This is rare evidence of Indian perceptions of China.
  • His meticulous notes on Bamiyan and Nalanda help modern archaeologists. His reports of urban decay in the Ganges basin (Kushinagara, Vaishali) were used by R.S. Sharma (Indian Feudalism) for early medieval decline.

Lecture summary: he observed cities, universities (Nalanda), politics, society, economy, and religion (Buddhist, Hindu and Jain side by side). Historians value him because he is among the richest sources for 7th-century India; his observation was detailed, systematic and wide-ranging; and his account is often cross-checked against and confirmed by archaeology and inscriptions.

Yijing (635–713) Must know

  • Travelled mainly by sea, 671–695; studied in Srivijaya (Sumatra) on the way. Less studied than Faxian and Xuanzang.
  • Two works, completed and sent home from Srivijaya: A Record of Buddhism as Practised in India Sent Home from the Southern Seas (how doctrine and monastic rules were practised in India), and Memoirs of Eminent Monks who Visited India… (biographies of 56 Chinese monks who went to India in the 7th century, some overland via Central Asia and Tibet, others by sea, some never returning).
  • He wanted to correct "errors" in Chinese practice. He described 40 practices in Indian monasteries (from cleansing after meals to ordination rules) and compared them with China. He allowed compromise: Indians eat with the right hand and never use chopsticks, but since the Vinaya neither allows nor forbids chopsticks, the Chinese may keep them, "for if we obstinately reject their use, people may laugh or complain".
  • Example biography: Xuanzhao went via Tibet and Nepal; Emperor Gaozong sent him back to India for longevity drugs and physicians; he died before returning.
  • His aim: "If you read this Record of mine, you may, without moving one step, travel in all the five countries of India", and to "build a second Rajagriha in the Divine Land of China". All the pilgrims tried to recreate an Indic world in China, so followers could make pilgrimage without the journey and dispel the borderland complex.

Lecture: Yijing focused on monastic discipline, Buddhist education, daily religious life and translation. His contribution: he shows India within a larger Asian Buddhist world, connecting it to Southeast Asian Buddhist centres.

What Chinese pilgrims teach historians (lecture) Their writings reveal Buddhist networks (links between monasteries across Asia), universities (Nalanda), urban life, trade routes (shared by monks and merchants), diplomacy (contacts between courts), and cultural interaction. Key message: religion became a vehicle connecting Asia, moving monks, texts and ideas along the same routes as merchants and goods.

6.3 Arab scholars and al-Shahrastani (M.S. Khan) Must know

Why were Arab scholars interested in India? Supporting

Trade (sea and land routes); the expansion of Islam into South Asia; scientific curiosity (Indian mathematics, astronomy and philosophy fed the translation movement in Baghdad); and comparative religion. Comparative religion has been "rightly acclaimed as one of the great contributions of Muslim civilization" (Franz Rosenthal), because of Muslims' attachment to their own faith, their early direct contact with other world religions, and their interest in philosophy. Religious debates were held at Baghdad.

early IslamTradition holds that Malik bin Dinar, a companion of the Prophet, reached South India (the earliest Islam–Hinduism contact). Muslims met Buddhists in Samarqand (conquered 711) and Central Asia.
712Conquest of Sind and Punjab brings close Hindu–Muslim relations.
771A Hindu scientist, probably from Sind, visits the court of Abbasid Caliph al-Mansur in Baghdad.
786–803The Barmakids (viziers of Indian, originally Buddhist, origin): Yahya bin Khalid sends an envoy to India to collect information on its religions. The report survives in Ibn al-Nadim's Kitab al-Fihrist ("Madhahib al-Hind").
10th–11th c.Al-Maqdisi, Iranshahri, Gardizi and al-Marwazi (in Persian), al-Biruni (critical, from Sanskrit sources), Qadi Sa'id al-Andalusi (Tabaqat al-Umam, Toledo, 1068).
12th c.Al-Shahrastani (1086–1153), Kitab al-Milal wa'l-Nihal, chapter "Ara' ahl al-Hind" (Views of the people of India).

What al-Shahrastani wrote Supporting

  • Indians are divided into Brahmins, Naturalists and Dualists; the majority are "Sabeans". All fall into five groups: Brahmins, followers of spiritual beings, proponents of heavenly bodies, idol-worshippers, and philosophers.
  • Brahmins are named after a man called "Brahma" (an error); they deny prophecy (four arguments, which Shahrastani rebuts with his own case from the Qur'an). Sub-sects: followers of the Buddha, proponents of meditation (experts in astrology; "meditation… kills [a man] instantly"), adherents of metempsychosis (transmigration).
  • Spiritual beings (divine messengers in human form without a written book): sects Basawiya (Vasudeviya), Bahuwadiya (Mahadeviya), Kabaliya (Kapalika), Bahaduniya. Planet worshippers: sun and moon worshippers.
  • Idol worshippers, five sects: Mahakala worshippers (the great idol at Ujjain), tree worshippers (Vrksa-bhaktas), Dakini followers, water worshippers, fire worshippers (Agnihotra).
  • Philosophers: supposedly followers of "Qalanus", a student of Pythagoras who settled in India (a garbled version of Kalanos, the Indian sage who accompanied Alexander); God is pure Light; ends with an Alexander story in which Indian sages are superior.

Khan's evaluation: how reliable is al-Shahrastani? Must know

Strengths / merits
  • Preserves medieval knowledge that might otherwise be lost.
  • Systematic classification of Indian religions into clear categories.
  • Comparative religion: places Indian belief alongside other traditions.
  • Faithful, objective reporting of what his sources said; he does not denigrate Indian religions or attack the Upanishads or Gita.
  • Unique information on Buddhism not found in other Arabic sources: reward and punishment, the eternity of the world, five virtues (comparable to the Four Noble Truths), lists of ten virtues and ten vices (an expanded Eightfold Path); "Shakamin" = Sakyamuni; Bodhisattvas.
  • Correctly understood why Brahmins "reject prophethood": avatara (God himself descends; no book needed) differs from the Semitic prophet (a human who brings God's book).
Weaknesses / demerits
  • Never visited India; no Sanskrit or Pali; his account is "mainly literary", based on secondary Arabic compilations (paraphrased from Ibn al-Nadim, al-Maqdisi and especially Gardizi, who used Jayhani). He did not use al-Biruni.
  • Inherited errors: Brahma as a man's name; Buddhism treated as a branch of Hinduism (as medieval Europe called Islam a Christian heresy); no knowledge of Mahayana/Hinayana.
  • Omissions: nothing on the Vedas or Jainism; describes sects rather than religions; his material reflects roughly the 8th century.
  • Factual errors: chronology (Buddha "5,000 years" before the Hijra, actually about 1,182); astronomy (Saturn rather than Jupiter as the largest planet); meditation mixed with black magic; the false Pythagoras link.
  • Islamic categories imposed: he defends prophecy with the Qur'an and criticises idol worship as treating idols as divine, unaware of symbolic worship (pratikopasana), which al-Biruni understood.

Khan's fair conclusion: it would be "futile to expect scientific accuracy from an author of the twelfth century". He should be judged by the Arabic information available in the mid-12th century. The chapter mixes fact and fiction, and except for al-Biruni, Arab authors had little or no access to original Indian sources.

"Which source would historians trust more? Why?" Al-Biruni, because he has direct observation, language competence, original texts and a critical method. But al-Shahrastani is still valuable as evidence of how knowledge about India circulated in the Islamic world through literary traditions. A source can be poor for "what India was like" yet excellent for "what 12th-century Muslims believed about India", which is Backerra's point that the question decides the value of a source.

Reading foreign accounts critically Ask: Who is speaking? Why did they travel? What did they observe (vs hear)? What could they not understand? What evidence supports their claims? Can archaeology confirm them? The key concept is corroboration: check one account against others before trusting it, especially against inscriptions (official, dated, less shaped by outside bias), archaeology (independent physical evidence), indigenous literature (the Indian perspective missing from outsiders), and coins (independent evidence of rulers and trade).
Larger historical significance (lecture) Foreign travellers show that India was deeply connected with Asia, not an isolated civilisation; that knowledge travelled alongside merchants; that Buddhism linked India and China; that Islamic scholarship connected India with West Asia; and that ideas moved across political boundaries. Travel writing reveals the history of connections, not isolation.
Summary of Session 6 Where Indian records are scarce, foreign accounts fill gaps, but travel writing combines observation, memory, interpretation and opinion, so it is evidence and interpretation at once. The three Chinese pilgrims (Faxian, Vinaya, 399–413, returning by sea; Xuanzang, 657 texts, Harsha and Kanauj, diplomacy, Nalanda; Yijing, by sea via Srivijaya, 40 monastic practices, 56 monks) give first-hand, Buddhist-coloured evidence, show monk–merchant symbiosis and the "borderland complex", and raised India's image in China. Al-Shahrastani (12th c.) classified Indian religions systematically but from secondary Arabic sources, without Sanskrit, with errors and Islamic categories; al-Biruni is the critical, primary counterpoint. Historians must corroborate with inscriptions, archaeology, coins and Indian texts. Together these sources reveal connections, not isolation.
