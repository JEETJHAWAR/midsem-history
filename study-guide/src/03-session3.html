3.1 Why study history at multiple levels? Must know

The problem

  • Historical events do not occur at only one level.
  • Local experiences are shaped by wider systems.
  • Large systems are built from regional interactions.
  • National history alone is often too narrow.

The principle

  • Historical processes unfold at multiple levels.
  • Scale shapes the questions we ask, and different scales produce different explanations.
  • No single scale is always sufficient.
  • History is not naturally contained within the nation-state.

3.3 Wallerstein and world-systems analysis (WSA) Must know

In plain words: what is a "world-system"? Immanuel Wallerstein (The Modern World-System, vol. I, 1974) argued that the modern world economy since the 16th century is one system divided by an international division of labour into a core (capital-intensive, high-skill production), a semi-periphery and a periphery (labour-intensive, low-skill suppliers of raw materials). The key phrase: "A world-system is not the system of the world, but a system that is a world", and it can be (and usually has been) smaller than the whole globe. A world-system is the largest unit of social evolution: empires, states, non-state societies and families all exist inside it, with a recursive relationship in which the parts shape the whole and the whole constrains the parts.

(In S8, J. Bhattacharya notes that Wallerstein placed South Asia in the "semi-periphery". David Washbrook rejected this, pointing to strong states, finance and a dynamic bourgeoisie, and Bayly and Ludden called Wallerstein Eurocentric.)

3.4 Hall, ch. 1, "Comparing Globalizations": the argument Must know

Hall's core claims
  1. Reject the idea of one single globalization; there are MULTIPLE globalizations.
  2. Globalizing processes have long historical depth. They are ancient, becoming "truly global" only in the last century or so. Contemporary globalization has new aspects, but these did not appear de novo: "how can one tell what is new if one does not already know what is old?" If processes are cyclical, you need a long view to tell what is new from what is the same process intensified.
  3. Different world-systems emerged at different times (many world-systems since at least the Neolithic, c. 12,000 years ago).
  4. Comparison is more useful than one linear story. What counts as an appropriate comparison depends on the purpose of comparing, not on the objects themselves.

The great debate: one system or many? Must know

Andre Gunder Frank (with Barry Gills, 1993)Christopher Chase-Dunn & Thomas Hall (1997)
ONE expanding world system for 5,000 years (written without a hyphen: "world system")MANY world-systems of various kinds since at least the Neolithic (with a hyphen: "world-systems")

Hall jokes that this debate "may appear to be nit-picking about whether or not world-system has a hyphen" and has caused "no end of headaches for copy editors", but the hyphen marks an important theoretical distinction. Others extended WSA into the past: Janet Abu-Lughod, Before European Hegemony: The World System A.D. 1250–1350 (1989), traced Europe-to-China interconnections in the 13th–14th centuries.

Hall's lessons about comparison Supporting

  • Apples and oranges: it is fine to compare apples and oranges if you are discussing fruit; add baseballs if you are discussing spherical objects. The purpose decides what is comparable.
  • Context matters: narrow comparisons give detail and nuance but need larger contexts. The cartoon of the young stock-market whiz who asks "you mean they can go down?" shows how narrow experience restricts analysis. Progress in the social sciences comes from discovering the larger contexts, which can reveal that supposed constants are actually slow-changing variables.
  • Move between levels: some local processes cannot be understood from very broad contexts. Know when to treat something as constant and when as a variable. Very slow changes (e.g. climate change) can become invisible in short spans of noisy data.
  • Emergent change and inflection points: moments when a boundary is crossed and a whole set of phenomena changes, e.g. the "emergence of the state" (like a machine's purpose, which physics and chemistry cannot explain). Because "pristine" states arose several times, there may be systematic processes. If you study only recent decades (as Sklair does), you cannot even frame questions about the rise of states or the modern nation-state.
  • Julian Go compares the 19th-century British and 20th-century American empires using "world-system time" (equivalent phases of hegemonic cycles), showing that the "American Empire" is hardly exceptional.

Three implicit debates in the volume
  1. The unification of Afroeurasia (Abu-Lughod, against Wallerstein's view that the European world-system was barely connected to Asia and Africa). Jared Diamond (Guns, Germs, and Steel, 1997): Eurasia's east–west axis eased the spread of plants and animals. Turchin, Adams and Hall (2006) found this holds empirically; the Andes are a north–south exception that follows similar climatic logic via altitude.
  2. Stephen Sanderson: parallel sequences of social evolution in the Old and New Worlds over similar time-spans.
  3. Turchin & Hall (2003), building on Teggart's Rome and China (1939): synchronised cycles show why low volumes of luxury trade (silk, gems) can have large effects at both ends of a network. Luxury goods can matter as much as the bulk goods Wallerstein emphasised, and this highlights the role of Central Asian pastoral nomads.

WSA is a hallmark of multi- and inter-disciplinary work (anthropology, archaeology, geography, philosophy, political science, sociology, world history). It often studies systems "from the bottom up", from the periphery to the centre (frontiers, indigenous peoples; Hall & Fenelon argue indigenous movements were the original social movements).

3.5 Hall, ch. 8: nation, region and globe Must know

  • Historical "place" can be understood at three levels: nation, region, globe. Each scale reveals different patterns.
  • Historians must move across scales.
  • Nation-state boundaries can be misleading for earlier periods. "India", "China" or "Bangladesh" did not exist in their modern form in the past.
  • This is especially useful for Indian history.

3.6 Why India is central to this discussion Must know

India as a test case

  • India has always contained multiple regions.
  • It has long-standing ties to Asia, Africa and the Indian Ocean.
  • Its history cannot be reduced to one national narrative.
  • It shows both internal diversity and global connectedness.
  • It is ideal for testing nation–region–globe frameworks.

The Indian Ocean: where regional meets global

  • A coherent macro-region with its own historical logic.
  • Also part of wider Afro-Eurasian and global networks.
  • Connected East Africa, Arabia, India and Southeast Asia.
  • Trade, migration, religion and empire moved through it.
  • The best example of regional and global history meeting (developed in S7 and S11).

Case study: Vasco da Gama at three scales Must know

ScaleHow the 1498 arrival at Calicut looks
National frameA moment in Indian history: the beginning of European presence, later colonial rule.
Regional frameEntry of a new player into the Indian Ocean world, which already had Gujarati, Arab, Mapilla and Chetti merchants (he was guided to Calicut by a Gujarati pilot, S11).
Global frameA shift in wider Eurasian and maritime systems: the Cape route as an alternative to the Red Sea, Gulf and Venetian routes.
The lessonThe Portuguese arrival changed existing networks, but it did not create connectivity from nothing. Europe entered an already connected world. This is the central claim of S7 and S11.
Summary of Session 3 History unfolds at many scales, and the scale chosen changes the questions and the answers. Global history studies connections, mobility and entanglement and challenges nation-centred narratives, but risks abstraction. Regional history studies how a place works internally, recovering hidden voices and testing big claims, but risks isolation; together they are stronger. Wallerstein's world-system is "a system that is a world", not necessarily the whole globe. Hall argues for multiple globalizations with deep histories, many world-systems (Chase-Dunn & Hall vs Frank's single 5,000-year system), and comparison defined by purpose. Ch. 8 frames place as nation, region, globe; nation-state borders mislead for earlier periods, which matters especially for India. The Indian Ocean is where regional and global meet; Vasco da Gama read at three scales shows that Europe changed existing networks but did not create connectivity.
