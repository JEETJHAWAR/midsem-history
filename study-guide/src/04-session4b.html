4.4 Indian nationalist historiography: history as cultural vindication Must know

Context After a phase of imitating Western ways, educated Indians wanted to make India "more Indian and less English", to revitalise rather than replace their culture (as Ram Mohun Roy wanted). This "renaissance" needed a historical consciousness. Bankim Chandra Chatterjee: "There is no Hindu history. Who will praise our noble qualities if we do not praise them ourselves?… The Hindus have no such glorious qualities simply because there is no written evidence." In the first half of the 20th century, nationalism supplied the ideology for historical investigation.

What nationalists were reacting against Supporting

  • Mill's condemnation; Elphinstone on Hindu "want of veracity" and his surprise that the Arabs did not overrun India; Smith on European military superiority and India's "endemic chaos".
  • R.C. Majumdar lists the belittling: Elphinstone assumed that India's foreign trade was carried by Greeks and Arabs despite the Periplus; the lowest possible dates for the Vedas and epics; claims that Indians borrowed everything (the epics from Homer, drama, mathematics and astronomy from the Greeks, the Krishna cult from Christ).
  • Joseph Needham: the trigonometric sine was used in India from the Gupta period, not by the Greeks, yet Tannery assumed it must be Greek simply because Indians could not have invented it.
  • Selective focus on weaknesses: Hindu sati condemned while European witch-hunting was forgotten; caste condemned while slavery and serfdom were passed over.

Definition (R.C. Majumdar): nationalist historians are Indians who examine or re-examine "points of national interest… which have been misunderstood or misconceived or wrongly represented". This is "not necessarily in conflict with a scientific and critical study"; a nationalist historian is not necessarily "a propagandist or a charlatan".

Weaknesses
  • Methodological lapses: making history serve present issues. Basham on Jayaswal: he used sources "in the manner of a barrister trying to win a favourable judgement", stressing supporting passages and ignoring contrary evidence.
  • Chauvinism: "from patriotism to chauvinism is but a step". Romila Thapar: "unashamed glorification of the ancient Indian past… a compensation for the humiliating present." Examples: the Indian origin of Aryans, Vedic culture older than Harappa, spiritual India vs materialist West, political unity from the earliest times.
  • Self-contradiction: military power vs non-violence; democracy vs imperial glory; spiritual vs worldly; high status of women vs their seclusion.
  • Communalism: Hindu accounts of Rajput, Maratha and Sikh struggles against Muslims provoked Muslim counter-histories. "Communalism… had its roots in the writing of history, too."
Strengths
  • The greatest stimulant of historical studies in India: national feeling drove scholars into the sources (monuments, epigraphs, coins, literature).
  • Thapar: by writing against imperialism they took a fresh look at sources; history moved from the antiquarian's collection of facts to narration and interpretation; debates led into social and economic history.
  • The growth of regional and local history (Bengal, Maharashtra; Nilakanta Sastri's south India) corrected generalisations based on the Ganges heartland.
  • The birth of economic history: William Digby, Prosperous British India; Naoroji's "drain theory"; R.C. Dutt's Economic History of India (1904) located India's malady in the agrarian problem, which became the economic platform of the national movement.
  • Cultural history (Tagore's idea; Panikkar: "we had each to 'discover India for ourselves'… a spiritual adventure").

Background: modern Muslim historiography on medieval India (Peter Hardy) Background

No Muslim historian wrote on medieval Muslim India until the 1920s because: Syed Ahmad Khan's priority was religious reform; after 1857 Muslims needed to conciliate the British; the Aligarh school was non-communal and did not want to stir memories; and Aligarh's syllabus favoured European history.

Muhammad Habib (Mahmud of Ghaznin): Mahmud plundered for glory and gold, not Islam; a Marxist-flavoured "urban revolution" and "rural revolution" under the Turks (too good to be true). K.M. Ashraf (Life and Conditions of the People of Hindustan, 1935): no fundamental revolution, classes as haves and have-nots, Hindu–Muslim cultural fusion. Nazim, I.H. Qureshi, Faruki (an apology for Aurangzeb against Sarkar) and Jaffar show "an unresolved tension between Islam and Indian nationalism" and an "Islamic apologetic". Hardy: in Habib, "Islam has absorbed Marxism".

4.5 The Marxist phase: the "new history" Must know

What made it new Influential after independence. Not all its practitioners were Marxists, but they adopted a materialist interpretation (dialectical materialism) and treated history as a social science, studying the relation between social and economic organisation and historical events. Crucially, it called "not so much for new evidence as for a re-reading of the sources with a different set of questions in mind." Focus: class relations, modes of production, agrarian structures, surplus extraction, caste and economy, state formation. Key figures: D.D. Kosambi, R.S. Sharma, Irfan Habib, Romila Thapar, Bipan Chandra ("Marxist-nationalist") and Sumit Sarkar.

D.D. Kosambi (1907–1966): the patriarch

  • Works: An Introduction to the Study of Indian History (1956) and The Culture and Civilization of Ancient India in Historical Outline (1965) "revolutionised Indian historiography"; also Myth and Reality, Exasperating Essays. No writer since Mill and Smith had so deeply influenced Indian history.
  • Definition of history: "the presentation, in chronological order, of successive developments in the means and relations of production." Reliable records and chronology for ancient India are lacking, but we can know how people lived. "Man makes himself" (Gordon Childe) by making tools; social organisation cannot be more advanced than the means of production. This is the only definition that allows treatment of prehistory.
  • Comparative and interdisciplinary method: India is full of "living survivals of the dead past". He used archaeology, field work on microliths and routes, ethnography (tribal clusters, red-painted village deities as relics of blood sacrifice, joint burials), philology (Hittite khatti = Sanskrit kshatriya), and statistics (weighing punch-marked coins).
  • From tribe to caste: plough agriculture, introduced through Brahmanical settlement, broke tribes and clans, and caste became the new form of organisation; clan totems became caste names; local cults were Sanskritised.
  • Myths reflect social change (Pururavas and Urvasi: sacred marriage, the transition from matriliny to patriliny).
  • Indus: thought the Harappans had no plough and that Aryans destroyed the dams (Indra freeing the waters from Vrtra). Thapar corrects him: the plough was known even pre-Harappan, and the Vedic word for the plough is non-Aryan.
  • Aryans: rejected an Aryan race but accepted Aryan-speaking conquerors (iron, horse); Indian tradition was an amalgam of Aryan and non-Aryan from the start.
  • Buddhism and Jainism: economic causes (technology, detribalisation, urbanism, new classes of grihapatis (landowning peasants) and shreshtis (rich traders) who wanted peace and a universal ethic). Ahimsa had an economic basis: Vedic sacrifice consumed cattle needed for plough agriculture. Mercantile patronage rooted these faiths more deeply than royal patronage did.
  • Thapar's assessment: not a mechanical Marxist. He rejected the Asiatic Mode of Production wholesale and qualified feudalism.

Assessment: why Marxist history was an advance
  • Broadened the scope from state to society, from soldiers and statesmen to the people; dynastic history went out of fashion and politics became a backdrop.
  • Interdisciplinary and primary sources: linguistics, sociology, anthropology, statistics; archaeology (PGW, NBP) seen as bias-free physical evidence (Sharma's Urban Decay is almost wholly archaeological).
  • Strong on origins: state formation in the Ganga valley (Kosambi, Sharma, Thapar) was "a triumph of materialist historiography".
  • Explanation and interpretation instead of narrative; processes over events.
  • Critique of Western clichés: the Asiatic Mode of Production, Oriental despotism, the Aryan race, static society; evidence of urbanism and social mobility.
Sreedharan's criticisms
  • Different conclusions from the same theory: Kosambi's no-plough Harappa is unlikely; Sharma says Buddhism accepted slave-keeping and money-lending while Thapar says Buddhism disapproved of slavery.
  • The economics of ahimsa lacks quantitative evidence: how many cattle were really sacrificed? The Suttanipata itself says "brahmins of old did not kill cattle", so why a taboo, and why would Brahmanists obey it?
  • Jargon: Bipan Chandra's and Habib's "contradictions" are "exercises in obscurity" (Thapar is exempted).
  • Interpretation must not leave the factual basis.
  • (Lecture model answer: class can become a rigid "master explanation", undervaluing culture, religion, region and ideas.)

4.6 The Cambridge School: politics, patronage and local interests Must know

  • Associated with Anil Seal, John Gallagher, Gordon Johnson and Christopher Bayly.
  • Challenged nationalist interpretations of the freedom movement.
  • Emphasised local politics, patron–client networks, elite competition, provincial interests, factionalism, and collaboration and negotiation with colonial power.
  • Saw Indian nationalism less as a unified idealist movement and more as a product of political mobilisation and elite bargaining (Sreedharan's chapter 15 calls this the view that nationalism was the "response of the Indian bourgeois elite to the stimulus provided by British ideas, institutions, opportunities and resources").
Evaluation It usefully highlighted local politics and networks, but critics argue it underestimated ideology, mass politics, anti-colonial commitment and popular nationalism. Bipan Chandra and the Marxists attacked it, and Subaltern Studies (Guha) attacked it as elitist.

4.7 Subaltern Studies: a historiography of protest ("history from below") Must know

Ranajit Guha's argument (Subaltern Studies vol. 1, 1982) The historiography of Indian nationalism suffered from two kinds of elitism, both treating nationalism as an elite achievement: (1) colonial/imperialist (and Cambridge) history, in which nationalism is the response of Indian elites to British stimuli; and (2) Indian nationalist history, in which Indian elites led the people from subjection to freedom. Neither recognises the contribution made by the people on their own, so neither can explain popular upsurges like the anti-Rowlatt agitation of 1919 or Quit India (1942). Parallel to elite politics there was an autonomous domain of people's politics, whose principal actors were the subaltern classes.
  • "Subaltern" is a term from Antonio Gramsci meaning "of inferior rank", whether of class, caste, age, gender or office. Associated especially with Ranajit Guha and Gayatri Spivak; a collective of the 1980s.
  • Focus: peasants, workers, tribals/adivasis, lower castes, women, marginalised communities; popular initiative, insurgency, consciousness, autonomous politics; subaltern consciousness (religion, superstition, views of the elite). Guha: the peasant never stumbled into rebellion; he rebelled consciously when petitions failed.
  • Marxian in tone, but it upset conventional Marxist historiography as well. It radically widened the subject of history, giving agency to people earlier seen as "crowds, mobs, or followers".

Assessment (Sreedharan) Weaknesses: a loose, negative elite/subaltern dichotomy; no theme beyond popular militancy; fragmented topics with no sustained narrative ("sub-history"); risk of essentialism (lecture). Strengths: it recovers neglected records, reopens questions thought settled, and restores individuals and groups considered too low for history. These "sagas of struggle" can be woven into mainstream history to enrich it.
Summary of Session 4 Ancient India seemed to lack "genuine" records, so it was recovered by Orientalists from texts, language, inscriptions, coins and sites: Jones and the Asiatic Society (1784), the Indo-European languages, Sandrocottas = Chandragupta (325 BC, the "sheet anchor"), Prinsep's Brahmi (1834), Cunningham's archaeology. Its achievements were real but it had a seamy side (the Aryan race theory, exaggeration), and through Said it was knowledge produced within colonial power: double-edged. Colonial historiography (Mill 1818, Elliot & Dowson, Maine, Lyall, Hunter, Smith, Moreland) built clichés of a static society and Oriental despotism to justify rule. Nationalists (Dutt, Bhandarkar, Jayaswal, Mookerji, Raychaudhuri, Sarkar, Majumdar) vindicated Indian civilisation, sometimes with chauvinism, but founded economic, regional and cultural history. Marxists (Kosambi, Sharma, Thapar, Habib, Bipan Chandra) re-read the sources for production, class and state formation. The Cambridge School stressed elite bargaining; Subaltern Studies (Guha) recovered history from below.
