SESSION10

Religions in India: Encounters, Adaptations and Transregional Influences

Readings: T. N. Madan (ed.), India's Religions: Perspectives from Sociology and History (OUP), "Introduction" · John Guy, "Tamil Merchants and the Hindu-Buddhist Diaspora in Early Southeast Asia" (ch. 11, in Early Interactions between South and Southeast Asia, ISEAS) · Himanshu Prabha Ray, "A 'Chinese' Pagoda at Nagapattinam on the Tamil Coast: Revisiting India's Early Maritime Networks" (IIC Occasional Publication 66, 2014/15) · Lecture 10 PPT
Where this fits / why it matters This session has two halves. Half 1 (Madan) asks what "religion" means in India: it is plural, internally diverse, lived and socially embedded. Half 2 (Guy, Ray) asks what happens to religion when people move across the Indian Ocean: it is carried, translated, appropriated and reinterpreted. The bridge, in the lecture's words: "If religion is plural, lived, adaptive and socially embedded… how does it change when people move?" The professor's model short answer (front matter) on doctrine vs lived religion comes from this session, so it is very likely in the exam.

10.1 The lecture's conceptual toolkit Must know

India is not simply a land of "many religions"

  • Religions originating in India (Indic): Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism.
  • Religions that arrived from outside: Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Zoroastrianism, Bahá'í.
  • Study their interaction and their relationship with society and politics.
  • The study of religion has shifted from studying religions only "objectively" (as doctrines) to religion as a social and historical phenomenon.

Plurality ≠ Pluralism

  • Plurality is a fact: many religious traditions exist.
  • Pluralism is a value or practice: communities negotiate their coexistence through accommodation, interaction, borrowing, negotiation, competition and conflict.
  • Key question: How do people live with religious difference?
IdeaWhat the lecture says
Religion is not one homogeneous entityReligions are internally diverse. Hinduism includes Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta, bhakti and regional and sectarian traditions; Islam, Buddhism, Christianity, Jainism and Sikhism are also diverse in schools, sects, philosophies, rituals, regional variations and competing authorities. Boundaries exist but are neither perfectly fixed nor internally uniform.
Religion as lived experienceReligion is more than belief: belief → practice → experience → social life. Food, worship, pilgrimage, death rituals, festivals, sacred spaces, family practices and community institutions. Distinguish religion as doctrine from religion as lived practice; stay mindful of the gap between textual references and lived experience.
Religion and society are intertwinedReligion is embedded in caste, community, family, gender, economy, political authority, hierarchy and institutions. A temple can also be an economic institution, a centre of redistribution, a site of political authority, a source of employment and a marker of community identity. To understand religion historically, study the society in which it is practised.
Tradition is not staticTradition → adaptation → innovation. Traditions reinterpret, generate new movements, respond to social change and encounter others; tradition itself contains mechanisms of reinterpretation. Examples: bhakti, the Kabir Panth, the Ramakrishna Mission, Ambedkar's engagement with Buddhism.
Why "religion" was misunderstood in India: the Judeo-Christian and colonial lens (lecture's closing slides)
  • Non-Western religions were first studied by Christian missionaries through a comparison model that was "Western" and "Christian". The Judeo-Christian model (one God, one book, one church, clear membership) did not suit South Asia, which had competing models of divinity, diverse practice, regional distinctiveness and inclusive religious identities. Only in the 20th century did the pluralist viewpoint dominate the academic study of religion.
  • From 1871 the British census classified the population by caste and community. Despite the varying status of castes and the fluidity of religious identity from region to region, it used uniform categories. Despite a policy of "non-interference", colonial administration enlivened perceptions of caste and community. The census's philosophical assumptions were problematic for South Asia and led to the hardening of religious identities (Muslim or Hindu or Sikh).
  • Connect: this is the same colonial knowledge problem as in Session 4 (Orientalism, Said, Mill) and the "census" step in Session 1's invention of tradition.

10.2 Madan: India's religions (the Introduction) Must know

Madan's main arguments in brief
  1. India is a land of religious plurality at two levels: between religions (the global level) and within each religion (the intra-religious level of sects and movements).
  2. The distinction between "pluralist" Indic religions and "homogeneous" (fundamentalist) religions of foreign origin is "wholly misleading". Any religion with many regional groups and millions of followers develops internal plurality; Islam and Christianity in India are as diverse as Hinduism.
  3. In India religion encompasses the secular; dharma is not the same as the Latin religio.
  4. Plurality is a fact; whether it produced pluralism as an ideology is a separate question. Traditional pluralism was often hierarchical ("tolerate and rank"). Popular folk pluralism (lived) differs from intellectual or secular pluralism (ideological).

(a) Religion, dharma and the numbers

  • Dharma vs religio: dharma is a moral and cosmic order, duty and the right way of life (including law, caste duties and life stages). The Western idea of "religion" as a separate private sphere of belief does not fit; in India religion has traditionally encompassed the secular (politics, economy, family).
  • Hinduism as a "federation of faiths": no single founder, book, creed or church; a loose union of many sects, gods, philosophies and practices.
  • Dalits (formerly "untouchables") occupy an ambiguous place: some reject Hindu identity (e.g. Kancha Ilaiah's Why I am not a Hindu), and many converted (to Buddhism under Ambedkar, or to Christianity).

(b) Hinduism: from the Vedas to bhakti Must know

c.2500–1800 BCEHarappan civilisation: possible precursors (a "proto-Shiva" seal, mother goddess figurines, ritual bathing)
c.1500 BCEAryans (Indo-European speakers) arrive; the Veda (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva), with Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads. Shruti ("that which is heard", revealed) vs smriti ("that which is remembered", tradition: Dharma Shastras, epics, Puranas)
c.200 BCE–300 CEDharma Shastras, especially Manusmriti (the Laws of Manu): varna-ashrama-dharma (duties by caste and life-stage: student, householder, forest-dweller, renouncer); the four goals (dharma, artha, kama, moksha)
ClassicalSix darshanas (philosophical "viewpoints"): Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vedanta
Epics & PuranasRamayana, Mahabharata (with the Bhagavad Gita); Puranas bring theism: Vishnu's avatars (Rama, Krishna and others), Shiva and the Goddess. The smarta worship of five deities (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Surya, Ganesha)
6th–9th c. onwardBhakti (loving devotion) arises in the Tamil south (Alvars for Vishnu, Nayanars for Shiva), then spreads north; the sant tradition (Kabir and others)

Three ritual orientations

Shrautas: follow Vedic (shrauta) sacrifice. Smartas: follow smriti, domestic rites and the five-deity worship. Pauranikas: Puranic, temple- and image-based theistic worship. Plus Tantra (esoteric ritual, also influencing temple worship; e.g. at Puri).

Sects (sampradayas)

Vaishnava: Pancharatra and Vaikhanasa temple traditions. Shaiva: Pashupata, Kapalika, Kalamukha (ascetic orders), Shaiva Siddhanta, Kashmir Shaivism, Virashaiva. Shakta: worship of the Goddess (Devi/Shakti), strong in Bengal, Assam and Kashmir.

Vedanta teachers

Shankara (8th c.): Advaita (non-dualism; Brahman alone is real). Ramanuja (11th–12th c.): Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism; devotion to Vishnu). Madhva (13th c.): Dvaita (dualism).

(c) Buddhism and Jainism Supporting

Buddhism

  • The Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama, 6th–5th c. BCE). The four noble truths: suffering is universal; its cause is craving and attachment; it can end (nirvana/nibbana); the way is the eightfold path. The three refuges: Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha (monastic order).
  • The second council at Vaishali led to a split into Sthaviras (elders) and Mahasanghikas. The Sthavira line became Theravada, which the Mahayanists called "Hinayana" and which survived in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.
  • Mahayana (the bodhisattva ideal; Nagarjuna's philosophy of emptiness); Vajrayana (Tantric), strong in eastern India and Tibet.
  • Decline in India (by about the 12th–13th c.): absorbed by Hinduism (the Buddha as an avatar of Vishnu), loss of patronage, and destruction of monasteries. Revival: B. R. Ambedkar's mass conversion of Dalits in 1956 (neo-Buddhism).
  • Pluralism in Buddhism (Madan): "even the Buddhas are many" (Mahayana), but doctrinal pluralism did not mean sectarian tolerance; Mahayanists "virtually drove" Theravada out to Sri Lanka.

Jainism

  • Mahavira (6th c. BCE), the 24th tirthankara ("ford-maker"); extreme ahimsa (non-violence) and asceticism.
  • Because farming harms living beings, Jains turned to trade and banking: a merchant community (Gujarat, Rajasthan).
  • Split into Shvetambara ("white-clad") and Digambara ("sky-clad", whose monks go naked).
  • Anekantavada ("many-sidedness"): reality has infinite aspects, so no single view is complete. Hence syadvada (conditional assertion, "in some respect it is…"), qualified by four specifications: substance (sva-dravya), location (sva-kshetra), time (sva-kala) and state of being (sva-bhava).

(d) Sikhism Supporting

  • Guru Nanak (1469–1539), a representative of the north Indian sant tradition; nam simran (remembrance of the divine Name); rejected external formalism (rituals, customs, caste distinctions) and offered a "transcendent third path" beyond Hinduism and Islam.
  • Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth and last personal guru, created the Khalsa ("the pure") in 1699 with initiation by pahul (baptism); Khalsa Sikhs keep their hair unshorn and are distinct from both Hindus and Muslims. Authority passed to the Guru Granth Sahib (gurbani).
  • Types: Amritdhari (initiated), Keshdhari (keep hair, uninitiated), Sahajdhari ("slow adopters" who cut their hair). Codes of conduct (rahitnamas) tightened identity. A lax Sikh is a patit (lapsed).
  • History: the kingdom of Ranjit Singh (early 19th c.); reform sects (Nirankari, Namdhari); the Singh Sabhas (late 19th c.) defined Sikhs as separate from Hindus; the Bhindranwale phenomenon (1980s) showed a fundamentalist turn. Madan: the Khalsa version is exclusivist, not pluralist, and the Sikh vision, like the Hindu and Muslim ones, is hierarchical.

(f) Islam in India Must know

  • Begins with the Prophet's hijra (622); the five pillars (shahada, prayer, fasting, zakat, hajj). Madan notes that jihad is not an obligatory "pillar" for Indian Muslims.
  • Arrival by trade before conquest: Arab traders on the Malabar coast, giving rise to the Mapillas (Moplahs), Muslims of mixed Arab–local descent who adopted local customs. Sind was conquered in 712 (Muhammad bin Qasim); later the Turkish sultanates and the Mughals.
  • Local incorporation: converts kept caste-like groups, local customs and saint veneration.
  • Ulama vs Sufis: the ulama (jurists, e.g. the chronicler Barani, who urged rulers to enforce the sharia) vs the Sufis, mystics whose shrines attracted Hindus and Muslims alike. Islam spread largely peacefully (Sufis and trade), not mainly by the sword; at Partition, less than a quarter of the subcontinent's population was Muslim.

Internal diversity of Indian Islam

  • Sunni majority (mostly the Hanafi school of law) and Shia minority.
  • Sufi orders: Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi. In Kashmir, the Rishi order (Nuruddin, 1379–1442), which drew on local ascetic traditions, alongside Iranian influence (Hamadani).
  • 19th-century movements: Deoband (1867, orthodox seminary); Aligarh (Sir Syed, Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, 1875: modern education); Ahl-i-Hadis (scripturalist); Barelwis (defend saint veneration and shrine practice).
  • Ahmadiyah (Mirza Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian, Punjab; claimed to be the mahdi). Recognised as Muslim in India, unlike in Pakistan; Madan reads this as a "general attitude of tolerance".

(g) Religious pluralism as ideology: has plurality produced pluralism? Must know

Tradition / personPluralist idea (and its limit, per Madan)
Rig Veda (I.164.46)"The Absolute is one, although the sages have given it different names." But this pluralism is internal to the Vedic tradition and silent on inter-religious pluralism.
Manusmriti (ii.14)Contradictory revelations are all valid and must be respected.
Hindu "fusion"Brahmanical carriers spread east and south through give-and-take (Chakrabarti). McKim Marriott: parochialization (the downward spread of Great Sanskritic Tradition elements) and universalization (the upward rise of Little/folk tradition elements). Diversity remained resilient.
Brahmanical toleranceEach group's right to its own way of life (svadharma; adhikara bheda), but not equal merit. Hinduism tolerates difference by incorporating and hierarchizing it (Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism seen as inferior varieties of Hinduism). Conflict was not absent (persecution of Buddhists and Jains; Shaiva–Vaishnava conflict).
Ramakrishna (1836–86)Practised Islam for a time and disregarded sectarian differences.
Vivekananda (1863–1902)Tolerance, not equal acceptance: Vedanta above all creeds, "the mother of all religions"; critical of Buddhism and Christianity. "Neo-Hinduism" was a reversal of traditional internal pluralism, with political implications (nationalism; Hinduism as a universal religion).
Arya Samaj (Dayananda, 1824–83)Founded in Bombay (1870s; Madan gives 1874). Rejected post-Vedic Hinduism (idol worship, caste and gender discrimination) and denied that Christianity and Islam were divinely inspired. An exclusivist strand that anticipates Hindutva.
Mahatma Gandhi (1869–1948)The most explicit pluralism: on 30 May 1913, "the world as a whole will never have, and need not have a single religion". All religions are equally true and equally imperfect; he refused to rank them. In Young India (1920) he credited the Jain anekantavada: "It is this doctrine that taught me to judge a Mussalman from his own standpoint and a Christian from his."
IslamSince the Quran is held to be the most perfect revelation, pluralism must honour that, but it has a basis: "To you your religion and to me mine" (109.3). Dara Shikoh (17th c.) translated Upanishads into Persian and called Vedanta and Islam "twin brothers", and was executed by Aurangzeb. Abul Kalam Azad (1888–1958): Allah is God of all creation, so all humanity is one; the ulama objected and the work stayed unfinished.
SikhismNanak's sant path transcends Hindu and Muslim identities, but the Khalsa (Gobind Singh) tightened identity: exclusivist, not pluralist.
ChristianityEcumenism; Raimundo Panikkar (exclusivist → inclusivist); a theo-centric rather than Christo-centric idea of salvation.

State pluralism: sarva dharma samabhava

"Equal respect for all religions", presented as Indian secularism (vs Western separation). Madan: it "does not go very far"; real pluralism must be built by people of faith, not as passive tolerance. Gandhi: the secular state should leave religion to the people.

Two pluralisms

Folk (people's) pluralism is spontaneous, lived social reality: ordinary Indians acknowledge difference as experienced fact, and "social harmony is built on the basis of difference". Intellectual or secular pluralism is ideological, self-conscious and sceptical of religion. 19th-century elites dismissed folk pluralism as ignorance. There is a "hiatus" between the two.
Connect to the model answer (doctrine vs lived religion): Madan's folk pluralism, Marriott's little traditions, Sufi shrines shared by Hindus and Muslims, Mapilla customs, and the gap between Brahmanical texts and village practice are all lived religion. The census of 1871 and the scripture-based Judeo-Christian model are doctrine-centred lenses that hardened identities.
