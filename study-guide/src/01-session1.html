SESSION1

Why Study History? Relevance, Impact and Significance

Readings: E.J. Hobsbawm, "The Social Function of the Past: Some Questions", Past & Present 55 (1972), pp. 3–17 (OT1) · Hobsbawm, On History (1997), ch. 3 "What Can History Tell Us About Contemporary Society?" (R1, covered in the PPT)
Where this fits / why it matters This is the foundation of the course. Before studying what happened in India and Asia, you need to understand why people care about the past and how they use it. Hobsbawm shows that every society uses its past: to stay the same, to justify change, to claim legitimacy, to build identity. Later sessions apply this directly: nationalist historians "using" ancient India (S4), modern states using the "Silk Road" to justify BRI and Act East (S8–S9), and selective memories of religion (S10). Exam tip: Hobsbawm is the easiest scholar to cite in an essay about the uses of history, nationalism or the present-day politics of the past.

1.1 Who is Hobsbawm and why does he matter? Supporting

  • Eric J. Hobsbawm (Birkbeck College, London) was one of the most influential historians of the twentieth century.
  • He is associated with Marxist historiography, and is one of the sharpest thinkers about the broader uses of history in modern societies.
  • He wrote about class, nationalism, capitalism, revolution, empire and modernity.
  • His key insight for us: interest in the past is organised into meaning. He helps us see how facts, memories, traditions and narratives are socially mobilised.
  • The 1972 essay began as a paper at the 1970 Past and Present conference on "The Sense of the Past and History". It deliberately raises questions rather than giving final answers ("It is easier to formulate questions than answers").

1.2 The core argument (1972) Must know

Core argument

"The past is a permanent dimension of the human consciousness, an inevitable component of the institutions, values and other patterns of human society."

  • Everyone is conscious of the past (defined as the period before what an individual directly remembers) simply because we live with people older than ourselves.
  • Every society has a past. Even the most "innovatory colonies" are populated by people from societies with long histories.
  • To belong to any community is to position yourself in relation to its past, even if only by rejecting it.
  • So the real question is not whether societies have a past but how they use it: as a source of authority, a moral guide, a political weapon, a marker of identity, or a framework for understanding change.
  • The historian's job is to analyse the nature of this "sense of the past" in society and to trace its changes and transformations. The past is not just inherited; it is socially organised and functionally used.
"We swim in the past as fish do in water, and cannot escape from it. But our modes of living and moving in this medium require analysis and discussion."Hobsbawm, concluding lines (1972)

1.3 The three ways a society relates to its past Must know

This is the heart of the essay and a favourite exam topic: distinguish the past as pattern, as model, and as process.

1 · Past as PATTERNTraditional societies.
Each generation copies the previous one, almost automatically.
"This is how things have always been done."
→
2 · Past as MODELWhen change accelerates beyond a point, the past can no longer be reproduced automatically, so it is consciously invoked, selected and adapted: "we ought to return to the ways of our forefathers".
→
3 · Past as PROCESSModern societies accept constant change ("progress"). The past legitimises the present as a process of becoming: history as development or evolution.

(a) The past as PATTERN: traditional societies

  • For most of history, societies treated the past as the pattern for the present. Ideally each generation copies its predecessor and sees itself as falling short when it fails to.
  • Inherited ways (customs, ancestral authority, sacred texts, old law) are treated as norms. History here is not a story of development but a reservoir of precedent and legitimacy.
  • Examples of the past as a stabilising norm: family structures, village customs, religious law, inherited land rights. In illiterate societies, "law equals custom"; old documents gain spiritual authority. A community may base its claim to common lands on old possession, or even on colonial-era court decisions, which are valued as records of a past that is a charter for the present.
In plain words: "traditional societies are not static" (a key correction) Hobsbawm says the idea that traditional society is static and unchanging is "a myth of vulgar social science". Change does happen, but it is absorbed in ways that do not openly challenge tradition. Change can be: gradual; concealed; renamed (new wine poured into old bottles, e.g. nomads "dealing in" lorries as an extension of dealing in camels); or absorbed into inherited forms by "stretching" the framework, through myth or ritualised history.
How innovation happens without breaking tradition: (1) the official "past" is always a selection from everything remembered, so it has interstices (gaps) where innovation can occur unnoticed; (2) technology is often the most flexible area: tradition-bound peasants accepted horses or new weapons without any sense of breaking with the past; (3) radical innovation can be legitimised only by disguising it as a return to a forgotten past, or by an anti-historical higher principle such as a religious revelation or prophecy.
Tradition is strong not because it blocks all change, but because it can absorb change without seeming to surrender its authority.
  • This kind of traditionalism implies a slow rate of change. Where change is too fast it survives only through isolation (e.g. the Amish in the USA), or absorbs single drastic steps at great psycho-social cost (e.g. the forced conversion of Mexican Indians to Catholicism after the Spanish conquest).
  • "What has always been done" for Bulgarian peasants in 1850 was very different from earlier centuries, yet they still felt traditional.
  • Dominance of the past is compatible with cyclical views and with regression/catastrophe. What it is incompatible with is the idea of continuous progress.

(b) When the past stops being a pattern: the past as MODEL

  • When social change accelerates beyond a certain point, the past "must cease to be the pattern of the present, and can at best become the model for it".
  • Crucial difference: a pattern is reproduced almost automatically; a model is consciously invoked, selected, interpreted and adapted.
  • The past now becomes a "mask for innovation": calls to restore it actually produce something new.
  • Key insight: the moment a society starts loudly calling for restoration is often the moment when continuity has already broken down. The past moves from lived tradition to a conscious reference point.

(c) Restoration and invention Must know

Hobsbawm is sceptical of the language of "restoration" because it is rarely literal. It always involves selection, reconstruction or reinvention.

Example: Zapata and Morelos (Mexico) The peasant movement of Morelos under Zapata tried to restore society to what it had been forty years earlier, before the era of Porfirio Díaz. It failed to restore the past literally: (1) it involved reconstructing what could not be accurately remembered (e.g. precise boundaries of disputed common lands) and imagining "what ought to have been"; (2) the hated innovation was not "a bullet lodged in the flesh" that could be surgically removed but part of a wider social change; (3) the effort mobilised forces with far greater effects. The peasants became a revolutionary power and the attempt to turn the clock back became a social revolution that helped produce "a historically unprecedented new Mexico". (Hobsbawm draws on John Womack's biography of Zapata, 1969.)
TypeMeaningExamples from Hobsbawm
Effective restorationTries to re-establish actual practices, institutions or structures.Calls to restore old morality or "old-time religion" (ideally everyone attends church); 19th-c. British radical hopes of returning to small peasant proprietorship.
Symbolic restorationRecovers emotional, political or cultural meaning rather than the actual social world. Often what people want back, such as "greatness" or "freedom", is too vast and vague for literal restoration.Rebuilding the bombed fabric of Warsaw after WWII; pulling down the Stalin monument in Prague; Churchill's insistence on literally restoring the Parliament building (partly practical, partly "magic": restoring a part to restore the whole).

Interpretation is therefore needed to assess innovations that claim the authority of antiquity.

(d) The past as artefact or fabrication Must know

  • If the past invoked is remote enough, it becomes little more than a language for expressing present aspirations. It may turn into an artefact or, "in less flattering terms, a fabrication".
  • Such a past is weakly connected to historical reality but politically useful, because it can be selectively organised into a story that legitimises the present.
  • Traditions may be recent, even constructed, yet powerful because they claim continuity with something older. Invented pasts can have very real effects.
Examples of remote or fabricated pasts
  • The "Free Anglo-Saxons before the Norman Yoke", and "Merrie England" before the Reformation (used by English radicals, 17th–19th c., mainly as explanatory devices).
  • The "Charlemagne" metaphor, used since Napoleon I for schemes of European unity, though nobody wants to recreate 8th–9th-century Europe.
  • The name "Ghana" transfers the history of one part of Africa to another, geographically remote and historically different.
  • The Zionist claim to return to the pre-diaspora past in Israel was in practice the negation of 2,000 years of actual Jewish history.
  • Nationalist movements are the clearest case: in Renan's words, nations "get their history wrong". Their objectives are unprecedented, yet they define them historically, especially territorial claims and deliberate archaism (Welsh neo-druids, Hebrew revived as a spoken secular language, the Nazi Ordensburgen). These are not revivals but "innovations using or purporting to use elements of a historic past, real or imaginary". "History is the most easily worked raw material" for manufacturing nations.
  • Contrast: attempts in genuinely traditional societies to restore remote regimes are meant literally, e.g. Peruvian peasant risings (to the 1920s) to restore the Inca Empire, or Chinese movements to restore the Ming. For Peruvian peasants the Incas were "yesterday"; to apply chronology to them "is to introduce anachronism".

(e) Innovation, progress and modernity: the past as PROCESS Must know

  • Systematic rejection of the past happens only when innovation is seen as inescapable and desirable, i.e. as "progress".
  • In modern culture, words like "new" and "revolutionary" (as in advertising) have become synonyms for "better".
  • People accept technological innovation (control over nature: science, technology) far more easily than social or moral innovation (e.g. changes in sexual and family relations). "Has there ever been a serious example of Luddism directed against bicycles or transistor radios?" Even popular religion accepted cheap printed holy images, yet violently resisted changes to holy texts. Where useful technology is rejected, it is because of fear of the social disruption it entails.
  • So modernity is not one simple embrace of the new; it produces enthusiasm in some areas and anxiety in others.
  • Once innovation becomes normal, the past cannot function simply as a stable precedent. Paradoxically, the past remains the most useful tool for coping with constant change, in a new form: as process. History becomes a story of development, transformation, evolution and becoming. Examples: Bagehot's Physics and Politics (1872); modern ideas of "modernization".
  • What legitimises the present is no longer the past as reference points (e.g. Magna Carta) or as duration (e.g. the age of parliamentary institutions), but the past as a process of becoming the present. "Faced with the overriding reality of change, even conservative thought becomes historicist."
  • The paradox: "the more change dominates social life, the more important history becomes as an analytical tool." Even innovation-proud societies remain deeply historical.
In plain words: the problem of the future Innovators who want to design a future unlike the past still look for examples, such as socialists citing "primitive communism" as an analytical prop: "a concrete precedent even for the unprecedented". Historicism (extrapolating past trends) is the most popular way to predict, but a contradiction arises. Marx was convinced capitalism would be replaced by socialism yet said almost nothing about what socialist society would be like, because history can show tendencies but not their precise outcome. Utopias need stability, while history is change. So "History ceases to be of use at the very moment when we need it most." We fall back on the past as a repository of precedent chosen for present problems. Examples: what happened to the poor displaced by 19th-century railway building can illuminate the effects of 20th-century urban motorways; "student power" in medieval universities bears on reforming modern universities. This dipping into the past does not replace proper social models.

(f) Genealogy, identity and legitimacy Must know

  • Genealogy means not just family trees but groups' efforts to locate themselves in a lineage.
  • It provides continuity, emotional depth, legitimacy, prestige, and often compensation for insecurity.
  • New nations, new elites, revolutionary movements and rising social groups search for heroic predecessors. They want ancestors, even symbolic ones.
  • Examples: modern revolutionaries claiming Spartacus, Thomas More and Winstanley as ancestors, though Marxist theory says these are irrelevant (Roman slave revolts could not produce communism); school history syllabuses teaching children about Boadicea, King Alfred or Joan of Arc; bourgeois parvenus seeking aristocratic pedigrees; social movements annexing past greatness in proportion to how much they feel they lack it; 20th-c. businessmen hunting elk like aristocrats; the Stalin-era claims that Russians invented everything.
  • Discussion point: Why do modern groups that claim to be creating something new still need to link themselves to older examples? Think of how political parties, social movements and nation-states constantly build lineages.

(g) Chronology and historical thinking Must know

  • For modern historians chronology is essential: sequence, dating, duration, and the avoidance of anachronism. Modern historical consciousness is bound to chronology because it assumes history is about directional change. Anachronism is an "alarm signal" for historians, which is why Macbeth in modern dress shocks today but did not in Jacobean times.
  • But not all uses of the past work like this. In traditional uses (pattern, model, moral storehouse) the moral, symbolic or exemplary value of a story matters more than its date. Examples: Romans and Moors fighting together in Spanish Easter processions; whether Horatius came before Mucius Scaevola matters only to pedants; the value of the Maccabees, Masada and Bar Kokhba to modern Israelis has nothing to do with their dates. (Moses Finley's contrast: Homer's Odysseus vs Samuel Butler's middle-aged Odysseus returning to an ageing wife.)
  • When real time is introduced into such a past (e.g. analysing Homer and the Bible with modern scholarship), it becomes something else, which is socially disturbing and a symptom of transformation.
  • Yet chronology (genealogies, chronicles, precedents) matters for legal, bureaucratic, economic and ritual purposes. The lawyer's search for precedent is present-oriented; the historian cares about the difference of circumstances.
Three things to keep separate (from the PPT)

History

Disciplined, temporal reconstruction (dates, sequence, evidence).

Memory

Social remembrance: what a group chooses to remember.

Myth

A meaning-bearing narrative: exemplary, moral, symbolic.

1.4 On History (1997), ch. 3: what can history tell us about the present? Must know

The link between the two texts The 1972 essay studies the social function of historical consciousness (how societies live with the past). Chapter 3 of On History studies the analytical function of historical explanation (how historians think with the past). The movement is from the social uses of the past to the explanatory uses of history. The present itself cannot be understood without historical depth.
  • History helps us understand contemporary society by placing it in sequence, showing its formation, and identifying long-term processes that are invisible if we look only at the present.
  • So history can help us: contextualise present institutions; distinguish continuity from rupture; identify what is actually new; and expose false assumptions about novelty.
  • History is an instrument of demystification. It does not predict the future, but it helps us understand how the present was made.
What history CAN doWhat history CANNOT do
Show the roots of present institutions.
Uncover the historical construction of nations, identities and traditions.
Reveal that many supposed novelties are part of longer developments.
Distinguish genuine rupture from recycled rhetoric.		 Provide mechanical lessons.
Tell us exactly what will happen next.
Serve as a substitute for political judgement.
Deliver a ready-made programme for action.

Why this balance matters: Hobsbawm gives history a serious public role without turning it into prophecy or moral preaching.

Hobsbawm's contribution in one sentence (PPT) History is not only about the past; it is about the ongoing relationship between past and present. Societies use the past to justify themselves, and historians use the past to explain how those societies came to be what they are. That is why history remains indispensable to public life and intellectual inquiry.

Discussion questions from the lecture (practise these)

  1. Why do societies appeal to the past most loudly in moments of crisis or change? (Because continuity has already broken; the past becomes a model or mask for innovation.)
  2. Is nationalism possible without selective history? (Renan: nations get their history wrong; territorial claims; invented traditions.)
  3. Does modern society really break from the past, or does it simply reorganise the past in new ways? (Past as process; even conservative thought becomes historicist; genealogies persist.)
Connections: nationalist historians (S4) built a glorious past "as compensation for a humiliating present" (Thapar), which is Hobsbawm's genealogy and compensation. BRI and Act East invoking the Silk Road (S8–S9) are symbolic restorations, "politically using the past". Vivekananda's and the Arya Samaj's versions of Hinduism (S10) reorganised the past for nationalist purposes.
Summary of Session 1 Every society lives with its past (a "permanent dimension of human consciousness"); the question is how it uses it. Traditional societies use the past as a pattern but are not static: change is absorbed, concealed or renamed. When change accelerates, the past becomes a model, consciously selected; calls for restoration (effective or symbolic) signal that continuity has broken, and restoration always reinvents (Zapata). Remote pasts become artefacts or fabrications (Norman Yoke, Charlemagne, Ghana, nationalism), invented yet powerful. Modern societies, accepting progress, use the past as process, and even conservatives become historicist. Genealogy gives legitimacy; chronology separates history from memory and myth. On History adds that history explains how the present was made (demystification) but cannot predict or prescribe.
