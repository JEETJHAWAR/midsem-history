SESSION9

India and the Silk Route Connections II: Trade, Religion and Cultural Interactions (the Maritime Buddhist Networks)

Reading: Tansen Sen, "Buddhism and the Maritime Crossings", in D. C. Wong & G. Heldt (eds.), China and Beyond in the Mediaeval Period: Cultural Crossings and Inter-Regional Connections (Cambria/Manohar, 2014), ch. 2 · Lecture 9 PPT (Prof. Jayati Bhattacharya)
Where this fits / why it matters Session 8 showed that the "Silk Road" was many routes, mostly over land. Session 9 moves to the sea and to religion. Sen asks how Buddhism travelled from India to China by sea, and who carried it. The answer joins three course threads: connected histories (Session 3), travellers as sources (Faxian and Yijing from Session 6) and trade–religion links (Sessions 7, 10 and 11). It is also the bridge to Session 10's question of how religion moved across the Indian Ocean.

9.1 Sen's main argument (in one box) Must know

Six claims of the article (and of the lecture's "Main argument" slide)
  1. Critique of the "Silk Road" paradigm. Scholarship has focused on the overland routes; one recent volume on the spread of Buddhism had not a single chapter on the sea routes or Southeast Asia. The phrase "maritime Silk Road" is historically misleading.
  2. Buddhism and maritime commerce were tied together. Like the overland spread, the sea spread of Buddhism was closely connected to mercantile activities.
  3. "Long-distance", not "contact", transmission (borrowing Erik Zürcher's model): in the first centuries CE, maritime Southeast Asia was only a "transit zone" where monks changed ships, not a place where Buddhism was established and then passed on step by step.
  4. India–China maritime Buddhist networks were cosmopolitan: Indian, Sogdian and Central Asian merchants and monks, Sri Lankan nuns, Southeast Asian rulers.
  5. Sri Lanka was not just the home of Theravada; it was also a centre of relic cults and of Tantric (and Mahayana) Buddhism linked to India, Java and China.
  6. Long-term transformation: maritime Buddhist networks lasted longer than the overland ones (into the 19th–20th centuries).

9.2 Why "maritime Silk Road" is a misleading label Must know

Recall: Ferdinand von Richthofen coined Die Seidenstrassen ("Silk Roads") in 1877 for the overland China–Europe routes through Central Asia. Later, every route linking China to the outside world got called a "Silk Road". Sen's objections:

  1. Silk was not the main cargo at sea. Early maritime trade was in beads, precious stones and pearls; later, bulk goods such as incense, pepper, spices and porcelain (the lecture adds ceramics, metals and textiles). Silk was "neither the earliest nor the most commonly traded commodity".
  2. It over-emphasises China. China was a major market and source, but ports, traders and ships from Southeast Asia, India and the Middle East were equally crucial. The Chinese became active in Indian Ocean shipping only from the 10th century.
  3. It underestimates Southeast Asia and Indian Ocean societies. Southeast Asia's rise as an economic, political and religious centre was crucial for the Mediterranean–China sea link.

Sen's alternative: see maritime Asia as a multi-centred commercial world, not a China-centred extension of the Silk Roads.

Deep background: the sea was busy long before Buddhism

  • 2500–1500 BCE: Austronesian migrations created maritime networks linking southern China, mainland Southeast Asia and the Indonesian archipelago.
  • 1st millennium BCE: metal technology and wet-rice cultivation spread through these networks; by the second half of the millennium they reached India. Southeast Asian sailors were as important as South Asian ones; Southeast Asia exported tin and gold and imported semi-precious stones and glass; Indian craftsmen in Southeast Asia made Indian-style goods.
  • 1st c. CE: Funan (Mekong delta, southern Vietnam and Cambodia) rose as a "buffer" between India and China; its ports, especially Oc Eo, trans-shipped cargo between "these two giant markets" (until about the mid-5th century).
  • India: Mauryas (c.324 BCE), then the Kushans, Guptas, Satavahanas, Cheras and Cholas sustained trade. Indo-Roman trade (1st c. CE): Roman coins, beads and glass in Tamil Nadu and Andhra, in exchange for black pepper and textiles.
  • China: under the Qin and Han, rhinoceros horn, ivory, pearls and incense reached the ports of Jiaozhi (north Vietnam), Hepu (Guangxi) and Panyu (Guangzhou). Sima Qian and Ban Gu describe Guangzhou as a rich trading outpost; glass, amber and agate appear in Han elite tombs; by the Jin (265–420) even commoners wore foreign jewellery. With these traders, Buddhist preachers, texts and ritual items came to China.

9.3 Land or sea first? Sen's way out of an old debate Supporting

PositionWho / evidence
Buddhism first reached China by seaLiang Qichao, Paul Pelliot
Buddhism first came overland via Central Asia (traditional view)Tang Yongtong, Rong Xinjiang
Sen: the debate is "irrelevant"There is no archaeological evidence of Buddhist monasteries in either eastern Central Asia or maritime Southeast Asia before the 4th century, by which time Buddhism was already in China. The evidence points to both routes: late-2nd-century Buddhist carvings at Mount Kongwang (coastal Jiangsu) suggest the sea; Buddha figures in Han tombs in Sichuan suggest the land. So the transmission came by multiple routes, not one conduit.
In simple words: "long-distance" vs "contact" transmission (Zürcher) Contact transmission works like a relay race: Buddhism spreads to the next region, settles there and builds monasteries, and that region passes it on to the next. Long-distance transmission works like a long-haul flight: monks and texts travel straight from India to China, and the places in between are just stop-overs. Zürcher argued that Central Asian oasis towns (Kucha, Khotan) became real Buddhist centres only from the mid-3rd century, once farming, population and trade grew. Sen applies the same model to the sea: early Southeast Asia was a stop-over ("transit zone").

Better question (Sen): instead of "which route came first", study the complementary, interconnected and sometimes competitive roles of land and sea. Many monks used both; Mahayana-dominated networks grew on the Central Asian land routes, while maritime networks later featured Theravada. The lecture calls this balanced view (land and sea complemented each other; sea did not "replace" land) one of the article's greatest strengths.

9.4 Monks and merchants: partners, but not always friends Must know

Reciprocity (the dominant model)

  • James Heitzman: within South Asia, Buddhist monasteries stood near commercial towns and trade routes, in mutual support with merchants.
  • Xinru Liu extended this to India–China: an interdependent, reciprocal relationship between monks and merchants.
  • Merchants: carried monks; met the demand for ritual items; financed monasteries and missions; circulated relics and carried manuscripts.
  • Monks and monasteries: met merchants' spiritual needs; protected merchants and gave lodging; created trusted commercial environments; introduced new items into trade.
  • Saptaratna ("seven jewels", Ch. qibao): offerings of precious objects to monasteries created demand for pearls, lapis lazuli and coral exported from India.

Lecture's point: religion did not merely accompany trade; it became embedded within commercial exchange itself. Buddhism expanded because commercial networks already existed.

Antagonism (Sen's addition)

Traders specialised in particular goods and route segments, owed loyalty to particular patrons, and believed in particular deities. Some were Brahmanical or later Muslim, and even Buddhist merchants may have competed over the lucrative trade in texts and ritual items. So the relationship was "more complex than is usually recognized".

Example: Faxian, 411/412. About a month out from Yepoti (Yavadvipa, Java/Sumatra?), a storm struck. The terrified merchants, whom Faxian calls "Brahmins", blamed the Chinese monk for bad luck and wanted to abandon him on an island. His patron (tanyue, Skt. danapati) threatened to report them to the Chinese ruler, who respected monks, and the merchants "faltered".

Sen calls for more study of Buddhist monks' relations with the Brahmanical and Muslim trader networks that dominated intra-Asian routes after the first millennium.

9.5 The India–China maritime network (3rd–6th centuries) Must know

Mount Kongwang (northern Jiangsu, late 2nd c.): Buddhist carvings mixed with Daoist motifs, and donors in Central Asian dress (Indo-Scythians or Parthians, key players in India–China sea trade via Southeast Asia). Why were Central Asians at sea? The Kushan empire (1st c. CE), spanning Central Asia and north and east India, created interlocking overland and maritime trade networks; Sogdians were active in trade, diplomacy and religion. Trade, diplomacy and religion were intertwined.

Faxian's return voyage (411–412): the first detailed sea account

  • Tamralipti → Sri Lanka → a "large ship" of 200+ people with a rescue boat; a typhoon on day 3 and a leak; cargo thrown or unloaded; about 90 days to Yepoti.
  • After 5 months, another 200-person ship with 50 days' provisions, bound for Guangzhou; blown off course; after about 3 months it reached north-east China (Shandong).
  • "On the seas there are many pirates… The great ocean spreads out, a boundless expanse. There is no knowing east or west; only by observing the sun, moon, and stars…"
  • He found no Buddhism at Yepoti, which supports the transit-zone argument.

A shift in shipping lanes (5th c.)

Earlier the Funan–Jiaozhi region (port of Oc Eo) was the key link; Sun Quan sent envoys there. From the 5th century, Java, Sumatra and Kedah (Malay peninsula) became the main centres, so Faxian's ship stopped at an Indonesian island, not at Oc Eo. Later monks also passed through the Indonesian islands.

Evidence for the "transit zone" phase (lecture): early Buddhist monasteries are absent before the 4th–5th centuries; Buddhist inscriptions appear late; Buddhist institutions grow alongside expanding maritime commerce. In fact maritime India–China traffic helped establish Buddhism in parts of Southeast Asia. The Buddhagupta inscription (5th c., from Raktamrttika, now in the Indian Museum, Kolkata) records a sea captain's prayers to the Buddha and confirms the Buddhism–seafaring trader link and Kedah's rise.

Srivijaya (7th–13th centuries) Must know

  • Ruled from Palembang (Sumatra); controlled Sumatra, Java and the Malay peninsula, and so the routes between the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea.
  • Used Buddhism in diplomacy: inscriptions at Nalanda, Nagapattinam (east coast of India) and Nakhon Si Thammarat (south Thailand) record Srivijayan donations to monasteries. (These come back in Session 10.)
  • Yijing (635–713) studied there (Ch. Foshi) on his way to Nalanda: "In the fortified city of Fo-che, there are more than one thousand Buddhist monks… Rules and ceremonies are identical [to India]. If a Chinese monk wants to travel to India… he must stay in Fo-che for one or two years." He returned to Sumatra to write his works. His Biographies of eminent monks who travelled to the Western Regions covers 60 Chinese monks, of whom 36 from China and 2 from Korea went to India by sea.
  • Lecture's point: intellectual authority was no longer monopolised by India; knowledge became distributed across maritime Asia.

Tantric Buddhism in Srivijaya (not mentioned by Yijing)

  • An Old Malay inscription of 684 (Palembang) suggests knowledge of the Gandavyuha sutra (Hiram Woodward); the Kota Kapur and Telaga Batu inscriptions also suggest Tantra.
  • Punyodaya (Nati), 7th c.: travelled between China and Cambodia.
  • Vajrabodhi (670–741): to China via Java in the early 8th century. Amoghavajra (d. 774): through the region in the mid-8th century.
  • Bianhong, a Javanese monk, went to China in 780 and studied under Amoghavajra's student Huiguo.
  • Atisa (982–1054), a prince from eastern India, went to Srivijaya in 1012 to study under Dharmakirti for about 13 years; then Vikramashila; invited to Tibet in 1040; carried Dharmakirti's Durbodha aloka, the oldest surviving Southeast Asian Buddhist commentary. An Indian going to Southeast Asia to learn: the flow reversed.

End of the 12th century: Chinese Buddhism took "its own ideological path", away from Indian Tantra, while Bay of Bengal trade (Myanmar, south India, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia) grew stronger, so Theravada from Sri Lanka shaped Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia and Laos (the "Theravada/Pali" networks).

9.7 Sri Lanka reinterpreted: relics, Tantra and the Bay of Bengal Must know

Lecture: "perhaps the most significant contribution of this article" Most histories describe Sri Lanka simply as the centre of Theravada. Sen shows it was also a transit point from the 4th century, a repository of relics, a staging ground for pilgrims, a centre of Mahayana and Tantric traditions, and a diplomatic actor across the Bay of Bengal. Faxian, Vajrabodhi, Amoghavajra (and, in the lecture, Atisa) all passed through; its monasteries were intellectual crossroads, not isolated Theravada centres.

The relic cult and political power

  • Faxian saw the Bodhi-tree sapling (planted in Ashoka's time, r. c.270–232 BCE) and the annual tooth relic ritual at Abhayagiri led by the king. Also famous: the Buddha's alms bowl and footprint.
  • Candrabhanu, a Southeast Asian king (probably from Tambralinga, Malay peninsula), attacked Sri Lanka in 1247 and 1262 to get relics (Culavamsa).
  • Kublai Khan sent an envoy in 1284, perhaps for the alms bowl (after missions to south India in 1272 and 1275).
  • Zheng He captured a Sri Lankan "ruler" in 1410, probably wanting the tooth relic.
  • Rulers believed relics had magical powers to project political power.

Tantric masters and Abhayagiri

  • Vajrabodhi, told in a vision by Amitabha Buddha, went to Sri Lanka, venerated the tooth relic for 6 months at Abhayagiri, saw the footprint at Lankaparvata, then sailed on a "Persian" ship to Srivijaya (where he may have met Amoghavajra) and reached China 719–20.
  • Amoghavajra (some biographies say Sri Lankan by birth) went in 741 after Vajrabodhi's death, on a Kunlun (Southeast Asian) ship, perhaps because of Xuanzong's 740 expulsion of foreign monks, or (Sundberg) to get the Tantric text Sarvatathagata-tattvasamgraha. The king bathed him; he learned from the Sri Lankan monk Samantabhadra; he returned in 746 with a diplomatic mission and the Prajnaparamita sutra.
  • Abhayagiri had Mahayana leanings from the 3rd century; it had ties with Pala Bihar–Bengal (750–1120), Tamralipti, Mahabodhi and Nalanda. Around 800, the abbot of Vikramashila was the Sri Lankan Jayabhadra. Gunawardana: Tantra was influential at Abhayagiri by the 9th century.
Ratu Boko, Java (792): Sri Lanka's influence in Southeast Asian architecture A Sanskrit inscription from Ratu Boko (central Java), dated 792, reads: "This Abhayagiri Vihara here of the Ceylonese [Sinhalese] ascetics… was established." J. G. de Casparis: this is a second Abhayagiri Vihara, either a replica or a building sharing its form or spirit. Véronique Degroot: it was dedicated to Avalokitesvara, and the Sinhalese influence came only in later stages (e.g. the northern platform has four access points instead of one). John Miksic: similar double platforms joined by a "bridge", a wall, rocky sites on the edge of a capital, pools and simple carving, but differences (an east–west axis in Sri Lanka vs north–south in Java) showing Javanese adaptation. Lesson: local adaptation, not copying.

11th–15th centuries: close links between Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Cambodia and Thailand. When the Cholas invaded Sri Lanka (11th c.), monks took refuge in Myanmar; after Vijayabahu I (r. 1055–1110) liberated the island, Sri Lankan and Burmese monks together restored Buddhism there. In the 12th–13th centuries Sri Lankan rulers sent monks, relics and texts to Myanmar; Sri Lankan monks visited Sukhothai, Chiang Mai and Ava and performed purification ceremonies. Modern echo: Anagarika Dharmapala (1864–1933) travelled by sea to India, Japan, China and the USA to make Buddhism a world religion.

9.8 Long-term transformation and conclusion Must know

Ashoka (3rd c. BCE)Buddhism enters Sri Lanka; maritime spread may begin
Early period (1st–5th c.)India → China; Southeast Asia is a transit zone; Sogdian and Indian merchants; Guangzhou and Nanjing
Middle period (7th–11th c.)Srivijaya and Sri Lanka as centres; Mahayana and Tantric networks; Vajrabodhi, Amoghavajra, Atisa
Later period (11th c. on)Sri Lanka → Southeast Asia: Theravada/Pali networks linking Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand and Cambodia
13th c.Overland routes dominated by Islamic exchanges, but maritime Buddhist exchange continues
19th–20th c.Chinese diaspora; Chinese in Calcutta; monk Taixu (1890–1947); Malaya, Singapore, Thailand and Myanmar as intermediaries, reviving the 1st-millennium networks
Sen's conclusion in brief Buddhism moved through trade routes, carried by monks, traders and other travellers, over both short and long distances. Maritime routes complemented land routes and created unique networks. Southeast Asia, like eastern Central Asia on land, was first a transit zone. Maritime Buddhist links endured longer than overland ones. Southeast Asia's later contribution to Chinese Buddhism needs more study.

9.9 Evaluating Sen (lecture's critical evaluation) Must know

Strengths and contributions

  1. Connected histories: not separate histories of India, China and Southeast Asia, but interconnected networks.
  2. Maritime history: the sea is not empty space but an active historical arena.
  3. Network theory: ideas spread through networks, not political boundaries.
  4. Economic history: trade and religion are mutually reinforcing.
  5. Buddhist studies: the long-term evolution of networks.
  • Interdisciplinary evidence: archaeology, inscriptions, travel accounts, Chinese chronicles, Buddhist biographies, material culture.
  • Strong comparative framework: land and sea complemented each other.
  • Excellent use of mobility: people, objects, texts, rituals and ideas all travelled together.

Limitations

  1. Heavy dependence on Chinese textual sources (Faxian, Yijing, Chinese Buddhist biographies). Recall Session 2 on source criticism: whose voice survives?
  2. Limited local agency: early Southeast Asians appear as intermediaries, not active producers of Buddhist thought. Recent scholarship stresses local adaptation, not passive reception (cf. Ratu Boko).
  3. Merchants generalised into one category; their different motivations need more attention: Tamil merchant guilds, Arab merchants, Malay traders, Gujarati merchants.
  4. Gender almost absent: apart from the Sri Lankan nuns, women get little attention.

9.10 "Silk Roads": connecting the old with the new Supporting

  • The lens has changed in the new millennium. OBOR → BRI is Xi's most important foreign-policy initiative. It focuses on Central Asia (Chinese capital flowing into Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan and Tajikistan), integrates markets, and feeds the needs of Chinese steel and coal production, aimed at industrialising Central Asia. The lecture asks: why did India refrain from joining? (e.g. objections to the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor through disputed Kashmir, and debt concerns; see the Forbes 2017 article the lecture cites.)
  • Re-emergence of the Southern Silk Route: its rhetoric is now part of political, policy and academic dialogue, rethinking Yunnan–Myanmar–India links "that had never ceased to exist".
  • Three take-aways (repeated from Bhattacharya): (1) the Southern Silk Road was a network (overland, riverine and maritime), not one line; (2) colonialism restructured rather than simply expanded mobility, disrupting older circulations and imposing borders; (3) modern states selectively use the past (BRI, Act East, BCIM): "the past is being mobilized, not neutrally remembered."
  • Multilateral groupings in the Bay of Bengal: BIMSTEC (Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation); the Asian Trilateral Highway (India–Myanmar–Thailand); BCIM (Bangladesh–China–Myanmar–India); BBIN (Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal).

Summary of Session 9 Sen rejects "maritime Silk Road" as a label (silk was not the main cargo; it is China-centric; Chinese shipping in the Indian Ocean dates only from the 10th c.) and sees maritime Asia as multi-centred, with roots in Austronesian voyaging, Funan/Oc Eo, Indo-Roman trade and Han Guangzhou. The land-vs-sea debate (Liang Qichao/Pelliot vs Tang Yongtong/Rong Xinjiang) is irrelevant: Mount Kongwang and the Sichuan tombs show both routes. Buddhism spread by long-distance transmission (Zürcher), with Southeast Asia as a transit zone until the 5th c. (Buddhagupta, Kedah). Monks and merchants were reciprocal partners (Heitzman, Liu, saptaratna; Nandin the ship owner) but sometimes antagonists (Faxian and the "Brahmin" merchants). The networks included Kang Senghui, Gunavarman, Paramartha and Bodhidharma via Guangzhou and Nanjing. Srivijaya became a learning centre (Yijing: 1,000 monks; Atisa studied under Dharmakirti). Sri Lanka was a hub of relics (Candrabhanu, Kublai, Zheng He) and Tantra (Vajrabodhi, Amoghavajra, Abhayagiri, Jayabhadra, Ratu Boko 792). Later Theravada networks reached Myanmar, Thailand and Cambodia, and maritime links outlasted overland ones (Dharmapala, Taixu).
