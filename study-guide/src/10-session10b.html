10.3 From Madan to the Indian Ocean: religion on the move Must know

From "diffusion" to "circulation"

  • Traditional model: India → Southeast Asia (one-way "Indianization").
  • Dynamic model: India ↔ Sri Lanka ↔ Southeast Asia ↔ China.
  • Agents: merchants, monks and teachers, pilgrims, rulers. Media: ships, texts, images, temples and monasteries, merchant guilds.
  • "Religion travelled because people travelled; what travelled was transformed in the process."
  • The movement also ran back toward India; Islam spread to Southeast Asia along the same routes.

Who carried religion? Not one agent but several

  • Merchants created mobility.
  • Brahmans brought ritual and kingship.
  • Buddhist monks and teachers brought monasteries, teachings and pilgrimage.
  • Rulers gave patronage and appropriated religion politically.
  • Artisans made images and material culture.

"Commercial mobility created conditions within which religious mobility became possible." The sequence: merchant → settlement → community → religious presence; resident communities allowed practices to become institutionalised.

Religious encounters: porous boundaries Hindu ↔ Buddhist ↔ Jain ↔ Islamic ↔ Christian interaction can produce borrowing, adaptation, shared vocabulary, shared sacred spaces, new institutions and new identities. Lecture images: a temple in Bali; the Ramakien (the Thai Ramayana); Thai religious practice; Mendut temple in Java; Buddhist Tantra from Odisha to Tibet. Encounter does not necessarily mean conversion: traditions may coexist, compete, borrow, adapt and influence art and politics. The Indian Ocean was a cultural space: the sea connected South India, Sri Lanka, the Bay of Bengal, Southeast Asia and South China, carrying goods, people, ideas, institutions and rituals. Ports were where merchants settled and religious specialists met.

10.4 John Guy: Tamil merchants and the Hindu-Buddhist diaspora Must know

Guy's argument It is generally accepted that Hindu-Buddhist beliefs spread in first-millennium Southeast Asia largely through local rulers' initiatives to recruit Brahman priests and Buddhist monks to secure their political and personal welfare. Yet the medium of transmission was the merchant vessels of the Indian Ocean diaspora. So early traces of Indian (mostly Tamil) merchant activity (inscriptions and religious imagery) reveal the early entry of Indic religion into Southeast Asian states. Tamils were dominant among the earliest Indian traders in insular Southeast Asia, supported by successive south Indian kingdoms. The distribution of inscriptions mirrors the trade routes and commercial nodes: coastal sites on rivers, giving access to hinterland riches.

Earliest Indic inscriptions and kingship (4th–7th centuries)

Site / evidenceWhat it shows
Kutei (east Borneo), late 4th c.Seven yupa steles of King Mulavarman, in Sanskrit, box-head Grantha script: the earliest Indic inscriptions in Southeast Asia. They record Vedic animal sacrifice and gift-giving by Brahmans for the king, so Indian Brahmans were present in a "court context" from the very start. (Yupa = sacrificial post, normally wooden; only about four stone ones are known in north India, two from Kushan Mathura.) Kutei grew from a fiefdom into an "Indic" kingdom through Indian merchants seeking Borneo's riches; it may also have been a production centre for bronze images for Java (McKinnon's scrap finds; the Intan and Cirebon shipwreck cargoes).
Purnavarman, West Java, c.450Rock inscription at Ciaruteun (near Bogor): king of Tarumanagara, devotee of Vishnu, Sanskrit in cursive Tamil Grantha; the name ending "-varman" is a Pallava convention. It shows a Vishnupada (sacred footprint), the earliest pada in Southeast Asia. The footprints are both the king's and Vishnu's: the first Southeast Asian ruler personally identifying with an Indic god, "the language of the Puranas, invoked by a new convert to the concept of Indian kingship". The threatening tone ("capable of destroying his enemies") is like the "curse inscriptions", e.g. the Telaga Batu naga-stele (Palembang, late 7th c.: subjects "drink the curse"), with Tantric overtones.
Vo Canh stele (near Nha Trang, Vietnam)The earliest mainland Sanskrit inscription (cruder Sanskrit), in Cham territory, signalling trading states on the India–China route
My Son (Champa), c.400Three inscriptions of Bhadravarman I ("Dharmamaharaja"): sacrifices to Shiva as Bhadresvara
Champasak (Laos), mid-5th c.Devanika inscription: the earliest Chenla/Khmer Sanskrit record, on a tripartite pillar, i.e. the earliest inscribed linga in Southeast Asia
The key interpretive point (lecture) India was not simply "copied". Indic symbols were appropriated and reworked locally: Indic vocabulary + local political context = a new cultural formation. Purnavarman used Vishnu to boost his own kingship. Brahmanical ritual came to be associated with kingship and statecraft, while Buddhism was more linked to commerce (Guy).

"Lands of gold": Tamil merchants, gold and religion

  • Indian merchants were drawn by spices, resins, forest products and especially gold, and because Southeast Asia was the gateway to China. The Silappadikaram mentions Chinese silk at Pompuhar. Buddhist Jatakas call the region Suvarnadvipa ("Island of Gold") and Suvarnabhumi ("Land of Gold").
  • Wat Klong Thom (Krabi, peninsular Thailand): a goldsmith's touchstone with the earliest Tamil inscription in Southeast Asia (Tamil Brahmi, 3rd–4th c.), belonging to a Tamil calling himself "Perumpatan" ("the great goldsmith"). Excavations at Khao Sam Kheo show settlements that processed gold and glass, going beyond mere trading.
  • Takuapa (west coast of the Thai peninsula), early 9th c.: three large Pallava-style Hindu sculptures (the main figure over 2 m tall, made under Brahmanical direction) and a Tamil inscription on a new tank ("Sri Avani-naranam"), linked by Nilakanta Sastri to the Pallava king Nandivarman III (r. 826–50). A tank implies an existing temple. The work was done by three merchant guilds, including the manigramattar (most active 9th–14th c.) and the sena mugattar (perhaps guild militia, known from Javanese inscriptions). Traces of processed gold are found in the soil.
  • "Takola": a place of riches in Suvarnadvipa named in the Buddhist Mahaniddesa (3rd c. CE), perhaps Takuapa, confirming continuous Tamil activity. Interestingly, the earliest reference is Buddhist but the later archaeology is mostly Hindu, as also in the Bujang valley (Kedah).
  • Lobu Tua, Barus (west Sumatra, rich in camphor, benzoin and gold), dated 1088: a Tamil inscription of the ainnurruvar ("the Five Hundred") guild, whose members set up a charitable fund, enforced by withdrawing trading rights ("to step on the spread cloth") from those who failed to pay the fee in gold. Other Tamil inscriptions come from Aceh and an inland Minangkabau site, showing Tamils settled even in the hinterland.
  • Nakhon Si Thammarat: a 7th-c. Sanskrit Buddhist inscription (Wat Mahaeyong); the Chong Koi rock inscription on a community of Shaivite Brahmans, urged to perform rituals correctly and live harmoniously with their host community.
  • Bujang valley (Kedah): two Buddhist steles (stupa engravings with the Buddhist creed, Grantha script, c.450–500). One names "the great sea-captain Buddhagupta, a resident of Raktamrittika" ("Red Earth", Malay Tanah Merah), praying that he and his fellow travellers be successful in their voyage. It was found by Col. James Low in the 1840s. Hindu shrines, late Tang Chinese ceramics and Islamic glass were also found there.
  • Belitung shipwreck (early 9th c., Java Sea): an Arab dhow built of African hardwoods, active in the China–Persian Gulf trade; the first Arab dhow found in Southeast Asian waters. (Links to Session 7.)
  • Early stupas at Sri Ksetra, Nakhon Pathom and Beikthano (Pyu sites).

Quanzhou's lost Hindu temples

  • Remains were found in 1940 when the Ming city wall was demolished (temple stones had been used as filler).
  • Two late-Chola-style pillars and basement reliefs (lotus and lion mouldings) were reused in the Ming renovation of the Kaiyuan (Buddhist) temple: "a remarkable instance of cultural syncretism".
  • The reliefs show Vishnu (with Sri Devi and Bhu Devi; as Narasimha; young Krishna subduing the naga Kaliya) and Shiva as a mendicant; one scene shows an elephant worshipping a linga. These are localised Tamil myths from the Kaveri region.
  • The carving was done by Chinese-trained stonemasons directed by a Tamil sthapati (architect) recruited by the merchant community.
  • Quanzhou is "a final and spectacular manifestation of a long process" of Tamil presence from Coromandel and Malabar to Southeast Asia and China.

Purnavarman, Balaputradeva, Atisa (lecture case studies)

  • Trade routes become religious routes: Tamil coast → Thai peninsula → Malay peninsula → Sumatra and Java → China (evidence: Klong Thom, the Vishnupada, Indic royal titles).
  • Balaputradeva, lord of Suvarnadvipa (Sumatra), founded a monastery at Nalanda; the Pala king Devapala (c.810–847) granted five villages to support it (the Nalanda copper plate, found 1921). Southeast Asian rulers patronised institutions in India; India provided learning.
  • Atisa Dipankara (982–1054): Bengal (born in Vikrampur near Dhaka) → Suvarnadvipa (studied with Dharmakirti, 1012–24, travelling on a merchant ship with students) → Vikramashila → Tibet (1040/1042), "the father of Tibetan Buddhism". The same ship carried commodities and religious knowledge.

10.5 H. P. Ray: Nagapattinam, a multi-religious maritime landscape Must know

Ray's argument Indian maritime history has usually been treated as trade driven by foreign (Western) demand for "oriental" luxuries, while historians marginalised maritime communities, studying ancient India through agrarian expansion and caste (assumed to forbid sea travel). Ray focuses instead on maritime cultural landscapes: the coast, its communities and their religious environment. She places the Buddhist complex at Nagapattinam on the east coast (127 contemporary Buddhist sites on the Tamil coast, plus the Bengal, Orissa and Andhra coasts), challenging Alexander Cunningham's theory (Cunningham was the first Director-General of the ASI) that Buddhism declined in India around the 7th–8th century. She also rejects explaining the 8th–13th centuries only through "Indian feudalism" (R. S. Sharma) or trade and politics. The sacred landscape was multi-religious, not a sequence of "Buddhism, then Hinduism".

Nagapattinam: the port and the vihara

  • Settled since the Neolithic. It has a 16th-c. dargah (Nagore, the saint Shahul Hamid) and, 10 km away, the 17th-c. basilica of Our Lady of Good Health at Velankanni. The 7th-c. Tamil saints Appar and Sambandar (31 couplets) describe a fortified, prosperous city where large ships called vangam anchored for Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.
  • Chinese records of the 1060s–70s mention missions from the Cholas and from Srivijaya; Jambi sent missions to China in 852 and 871.
  • Cudamanivihara (Chulamanivarma-vihara): founded at the initiative of the kings of Srivijaya (around 1005); construction began under Rajaraja I (985–1016) and was completed under Rajendra I (1012–44). The Larger Leyden Plates (Sanskrit, 111 lines, plus Tamil, 332 lines): in his 21st regnal year Rajaraja gave the village of Annaimangalam to the shrine that Mara Vijayottungavarman of the Sailendra family, ruler of Srivijaya and Kataha (Kedah), had built in his father's name, "towering above Mount Meru". The Smaller Leyden Plates (Tamil) list nine units of land.
  • The 1467 Kalyani inscription of the Burmese king Dhammaceti: shipwrecked Burmese monks worshipped at Nagapattinam.

The "Chinese Pagoda" and its destruction

  • Wouter Schouten (Dutch, 1660s) called a brick tower the "Chinese Pagoda" (repeated by Valentyn, 1724).
  • Sir Walter Elliot (1846, on the steamer Hugh Lindsay): "a four-sided tower of three stories constructed of bricks closely fitted together without cement".
  • Despite local objections, the Governor-in-Council approved its demolition (28 Aug 1867) by French Jesuits expelled from Pondicherry, who wanted to build a college there.
  • Finds: five bronze Buddhas in a concealed chamber; 350 Buddhist bronzes (1856–1930s); the Sellur hoard (2004, 42–45 bronzes, 11th–13th c., including a votive stupa with scenes of the Buddha's life and a tiny Buddha inside).

Hybrid Buddhism: what the bronzes and inscriptions show

  • The bronzes include Buddha images and votive stupas, and also Avalokitesvara, Maitreya, Lokesvara, Jambhala, Vasudhara and Tara. Tamil pedestal inscriptions (11th–13th c.) record Buddha epithets and gifts by monks, nuns and lay donors.
  • Rockefeller Buddha (69.2 cm): "for festival procession(s)" at the image-house of Rajendracolapperumpalli; installed by a monk; donor "the prefect of artisan manufactories for the merchants of the eighteen countries". So the Buddha image was treated like a Hindu deity (processions, epithets of divinity like those of Shiva and Vishnu), showing inter-religious communication.
  • Two monasteries: the Cudamanivihara and the Rajendracolapperumpalli. Pauline Scheurleer: some 9th-c. Javanese bronzes used Nagapattinam models.
  • Peter Schalk's three Buddhisms at Nagapattinam: (1) bronze-pedestal Buddhism so close to Shaivism that the two are hard to tell apart; (2) devotional Buddhism mediated by the sage Agastya (in the 12th-c. Viracoliyam and its commentary); (3) the Buddhism of acaryas (teachers), which barely survives.
  • Woodward vs Davidson: Hiram Woodward argues that a shared world of Mantrayana and Yogini Tantras made Indonesia and India "an integral unit well into the ninth century" (e.g. parallel Lalitavistara and Gandavyuha programmes at Borobudur and Tabo). This counters Ronald Davidson, who, following R. S. Sharma's feudalism, sees esoteric Buddhism as a response to the feudalization of Indian society (the yogin as overlord of a mandala of vassals).
  • Travel as Buddhist self-identity (Robert Buswell): the Gandavyuha (part of the Avatamsaka sutra) tells of Prince Sudhana, sent by Manjusri to visit 53 "spiritual friends" of all walks of life, so enlightenment comes through pilgrimage. It was popular in Indonesia, China and Japan, in an environment of trading guilds and merchant travel.

A multi-religious sacred geography

Hindu

The Kayarohanaswami (Shiva) temple (6th-c. origins, 11th-c. structure). The king of Srivijaya gave ornaments to its silver image Nakaiyalakar; his agent donated lamps; Chinese gold coins were given for worship of an Ardhanarisvara image installed by the king of Kidar (Kedah).

Buddhist

The Cudamanivihara and Rajendracolapperumpalli; bronzes; monks and nuns; links to Srivijaya, Burma, China and Java.

Islamic, Christian, Jain

The Nagore dargah (Shahul Hamid), patronised by maraikkayar ship-owners, Nayak rulers and even the Dutch East India Company; the Velankanni basilica; inland at Tiruchirappalli, Jain ascetics' inscriptions (3rd–11th c.), rock-cut shrines and the 8th-c. mosque of Abdullah ibn Muhammad.

Textual complexity (Anne Monius on the Viracoliyam): Chola-era Tamil Shaiva and Vaishnava literature shows saints defeating ignorant Buddhist monks in debate (Cekkilar imagines Buddhism's end), yet Pali sources of the same period show south India as a bastion of Buddhist orthopraxy, with monks called "Coliya" or "Damila". Recognise cultural complexity.

Merchant guilds across the Bay of Bengal (9th–14th c.) Must know

  • Guilds: the Ainurruvar, Manigramam, Nanadesi and Anjuvannam (the last associated with West Asian merchants), with associated craftsmen (weavers, potters and others). Inscriptions cluster in Karnataka (Dharwad–Bijapur, Mysore) and Tamil Nadu (Thanjavur, Tiruchirappalli, Madurai).
  • They kept private armies, donated to temples (some named after them) and built tanks.
  • Two kinds of assembly record: pattana-dharmam (Kannada) / pagudi (Tamil), "shared contribution" to temples (12th–14th c.), and erivira-pattinam, towns where merchants and soldiers lived (11th–12th c. in Tamil Nadu), showing the rising importance of armed guards.

Tamil inscriptions abroad (Jan Wisseman Christie)

Eight mid-9th to late-13th-century Tamil inscriptions in Southeast and East Asia: 1 near Pagan (Burma); 2 south of the Isthmus of Kra; 4 in north and west Sumatra; 1 on the China coast (Quanzhou, bilingual). Earliest: Takuapa (9th c.: a tank and a military camp). Then a gap of about 2 centuries to Lobo Tuwa/Barus, 1088 (a tax on ships' captains and crews); a 12th-c. north Sumatra inscription on trading rules. In the 10th c., local guilds called banigrama appear in Java and Bali (seven Javanese inscriptions, 902–1053), largely indigenous tax-farming organisations.
Questioning the Chola "naval expedition" The volume Nagapattinam to Suvarnadwipa (Kulke, Kesavapany, Sakhuja, 2009) argues that Rajendra I's famous expedition against Srivijaya (1025) rests on a single source, the eulogy in Rajendra's own inscription, uncorroborated by Chinese sources. Contemporary sources instead show an extensive Indian Ocean trading system from the Tamil coast to China, the real context. (Session 2's source criticism: a royal eulogy is a biased source.) Ray's conclusion: stories of travel and pilgrimage lost centrality with "scientific" archaeology and national histories; socio-economic history stressed trade, towns and Brahmans legitimising kings. Maritime history should now study multi-religious cultural landscapes across the oceans, not the "sequential development" of Buddhism and Hinduism.

10.6 Synthesis: what does this tell us about religion? Must know

Nagapattinam: a religious crossroads (lecture)

  • Buddhist images used Shaiva + Vaishnava + Buddhist visual and ritual vocabularies: religion was adapted, not simply transplanted.
  • One port, multiple religious worlds: a Shiva temple, Vaishnava traditions, the Cudamanivihara, bronzes, monks and nuns; links to Srivijaya, China and Southeast Asia.
  • Hybridity does not mean traditions simply merged; they borrowed, adapted and reinterpreted shared forms. A multi-religious geography preceded and followed the vihara.

The direction also reversed

  • Srivijaya → Nagapattinam; Sumatra → Nalanda; Chinese gifts to Tamil temples; Southeast Asian rulers → Indian monasteries.
  • India was not simply the source: its religious centres became nodes in transregional networks.
  • Quanzhou's Tamil–Chinese inscription and Shiva temple. The readings do not show a simple China → India transmission. Better formulation: Chinese and Indian communities participated in a shared maritime religious environment.

Overarching argument (memorise) Premodern religion was mobile, networked, adaptive, plural, reciprocal and open-ended. Religion travelled with people; people travelled through commercial networks; networks created institutions; institutions created new cultural forms. Indian religions became historically significant beyond the subcontinent not because they remained unchanged as they travelled, but because they could be carried, translated, appropriated and reinterpreted in different cultural environments, producing new combinations of local and transregional elements.
