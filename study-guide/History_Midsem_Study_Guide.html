Model short answer given in class Must know

Q. Why is it important to distinguish between religious doctrines and lived religious practices in understanding religion in India? (The professor's model answer, condensed. Notice the structure: definition → Islam example → Kashmir/Rishi example → Hinduism → Christianity → conclusion.)

Model answer (condensed)

Formal teachings do not by themselves determine how religion is experienced. Traditions provide beliefs, texts, rituals and principles, but these are interpreted and adapted within particular social and cultural contexts.

Islam in India: it has universal principles (one God, the Qur'an, prayer, fasting, charity, pilgrimage), yet Indian Muslims lived in a society with established traditions, so Islamic practice acquired distinctive regional and cultural characteristics. They shared Islam's universal foundations while being shaped by geography, language and social circumstances.

Kashmir: Islam spread there not only by transmitting doctrine but by adapting to existing practice. The Rishi order of Nuruddin incorporated familiar elements of Kashmiri religious life, such as vegetarianism and concern for animals. A tradition can take local forms without losing its broader identity.

Hinduism: the Brahmanical textual tradition differs from local and popular ritual. Brahmanical ideas were incorporated into local traditions and popular religion entered wider Hindu practice, producing great variation and diversity.

Christianity: converts did not abandon their earlier identities. Communities retained pre-conversion beliefs, social attitudes and ritual practices.

Conclusion: studying religion requires attention to how people interpret, negotiate and practise teachings in everyday life. Lived religion shows how caste, region, culture and historical experience shape religious expression.

Model essay plan given in class Must know

Q. Evaluate the claim that the shift from colonial and nationalist history to Marxist "new history" represented a major advance in historical understanding.

Professor's essay guideline (condensed)
  1. Introduce the historiographical schools of Indian history briefly and connect them to the question.
  2. Colonial and nationalist writing focused on civilisational judgements, political narratives and identity arguments. Marxist history redirected attention to economy, society, class and material life.
  3. Advance 1, a shift away from elite-centred narrative: from rulers, dynasties, decline or national greatness to peasants, labour, land systems, production relations and social hierarchy.
  4. Advance 2, explanation: events are not isolated episodes or moral lessons; causes are sought in material structures and social relations.
  5. Advance 3, correcting nationalist weaknesses: where nationalists used the past to foster pride, Marxists exposed inequality, conflict and contradiction within Indian society.
  6. Critique: Marxist history could become rigid when class was the "master explanation". Culture, religion, region and ideas could be undervalued as mere reflections of the economy.
  7. Layered revision: earlier phases should not be dismissed wholesale. Marxists built on them, and understanding advances by layered revision, not total replacement.
  8. Conclusion: a major advance (broader evidence, sharper explanation, deeper processes), but one stage in an evolving historiography, not the final form.

Five threads that run through every session (use them in essays)

1 · Sources are never neutral

Every source has an author, purpose and audience. Hobsbawm (selective pasts), Backerra (source criticism), foreign travellers (bias), Mill (misused sources), Chinese pilgrims (Buddhist lens), Shahrastani (secondary sources).

2 · Scale changes the story

City, region, nation, ocean, globe. Hall (multiple globalizations); Vasco da Gama at three scales; Hansen (city) vs Bhattacharya (region); Chaudhuri (unity in the large, disunity in the small).

3 · Connection, not isolation

India as producer, intermediary and cultural conduit: Gujarati and Tamil merchants, monks on merchant ships, Nalanda–Srivijaya links, Malacca. Europe entered an already connected world.

4 · Centres and peripheries are made

Chang'an's cosmopolitanism depended on "outsiders"; Bengal and Yunnan were labelled peripheral but were central; Southeast Asia was not just a transit zone forever; Sri Lanka was more than "Theravada".

5 · The past is used in the present

Hobsbawm's invented traditions → nationalist historiography → BRI, Act East, BCIM and BIMSTEC invoking the "Silk Road". Are we reviving or inventing connectivity?

6 · Trade and religion move together

Merchants carried monks, relics and texts; monasteries hosted traders; Islam as commercial law; Hindu temples built by Tamil guilds in Takuapa and Quanzhou; conversion followed commerce.

SESSION1

Why Study History? Relevance, Impact and Significance

Readings: E.J. Hobsbawm, "The Social Function of the Past: Some Questions", Past & Present 55 (1972), pp. 3–17 (OT1) · Hobsbawm, On History (1997), ch. 3 "What Can History Tell Us About Contemporary Society?" (R1, covered in the PPT)
Where this fits / why it matters This is the foundation of the course. Before studying what happened in India and Asia, you need to understand why people care about the past and how they use it. Hobsbawm shows that every society uses its past: to stay the same, to justify change, to claim legitimacy, to build identity. Later sessions apply this directly: nationalist historians "using" ancient India (S4), modern states using the "Silk Road" to justify BRI and Act East (S8–S9), and selective memories of religion (S10). Exam tip: Hobsbawm is the easiest scholar to cite in an essay about the uses of history, nationalism or the present-day politics of the past.

1.1 Who is Hobsbawm and why does he matter? Supporting

  • Eric J. Hobsbawm (Birkbeck College, London) was one of the most influential historians of the twentieth century.
  • He is associated with Marxist historiography, and is one of the sharpest thinkers about the broader uses of history in modern societies.
  • He wrote about class, nationalism, capitalism, revolution, empire and modernity.
  • His key insight for us: interest in the past is organised into meaning. He helps us see how facts, memories, traditions and narratives are socially mobilised.
  • The 1972 essay began as a paper at the 1970 Past and Present conference on "The Sense of the Past and History". It deliberately raises questions rather than giving final answers ("It is easier to formulate questions than answers").

1.2 The core argument (1972) Must know

Core argument

"The past is a permanent dimension of the human consciousness, an inevitable component of the institutions, values and other patterns of human society."

  • Everyone is conscious of the past (defined as the period before what an individual directly remembers) simply because we live with people older than ourselves.
  • Every society has a past. Even the most "innovatory colonies" are populated by people from societies with long histories.
  • To belong to any community is to position yourself in relation to its past, even if only by rejecting it.
  • So the real question is not whether societies have a past but how they use it: as a source of authority, a moral guide, a political weapon, a marker of identity, or a framework for understanding change.
  • The historian's job is to analyse the nature of this "sense of the past" in society and to trace its changes and transformations. The past is not just inherited; it is socially organised and functionally used.
"We swim in the past as fish do in water, and cannot escape from it. But our modes of living and moving in this medium require analysis and discussion."Hobsbawm, concluding lines (1972)

1.3 The three ways a society relates to its past Must know

This is the heart of the essay and a favourite exam topic: distinguish the past as pattern, as model, and as process.

1 · Past as PATTERNTraditional societies.
Each generation copies the previous one, almost automatically.
"This is how things have always been done."
→
2 · Past as MODELWhen change accelerates beyond a point, the past can no longer be reproduced automatically, so it is consciously invoked, selected and adapted: "we ought to return to the ways of our forefathers".
→
3 · Past as PROCESSModern societies accept constant change ("progress"). The past legitimises the present as a process of becoming: history as development or evolution.

(a) The past as PATTERN: traditional societies

  • For most of history, societies treated the past as the pattern for the present. Ideally each generation copies its predecessor and sees itself as falling short when it fails to.
  • Inherited ways (customs, ancestral authority, sacred texts, old law) are treated as norms. History here is not a story of development but a reservoir of precedent and legitimacy.
  • Examples of the past as a stabilising norm: family structures, village customs, religious law, inherited land rights. In illiterate societies, "law equals custom"; old documents gain spiritual authority. A community may base its claim to common lands on old possession, or even on colonial-era court decisions, which are valued as records of a past that is a charter for the present.
In plain words: "traditional societies are not static" (a key correction) Hobsbawm says the idea that traditional society is static and unchanging is "a myth of vulgar social science". Change does happen, but it is absorbed in ways that do not openly challenge tradition. Change can be: gradual; concealed; renamed (new wine poured into old bottles, e.g. nomads "dealing in" lorries as an extension of dealing in camels); or absorbed into inherited forms by "stretching" the framework, through myth or ritualised history.
How innovation happens without breaking tradition: (1) the official "past" is always a selection from everything remembered, so it has interstices (gaps) where innovation can occur unnoticed; (2) technology is often the most flexible area: tradition-bound peasants accepted horses or new weapons without any sense of breaking with the past; (3) radical innovation can be legitimised only by disguising it as a return to a forgotten past, or by an anti-historical higher principle such as a religious revelation or prophecy.
Tradition is strong not because it blocks all change, but because it can absorb change without seeming to surrender its authority.
  • This kind of traditionalism implies a slow rate of change. Where change is too fast it survives only through isolation (e.g. the Amish in the USA), or absorbs single drastic steps at great psycho-social cost (e.g. the forced conversion of Mexican Indians to Catholicism after the Spanish conquest).
  • "What has always been done" for Bulgarian peasants in 1850 was very different from earlier centuries, yet they still felt traditional.
  • Dominance of the past is compatible with cyclical views and with regression/catastrophe. What it is incompatible with is the idea of continuous progress.

(b) When the past stops being a pattern: the past as MODEL

  • When social change accelerates beyond a certain point, the past "must cease to be the pattern of the present, and can at best become the model for it".
  • Crucial difference: a pattern is reproduced almost automatically; a model is consciously invoked, selected, interpreted and adapted.
  • The past now becomes a "mask for innovation": calls to restore it actually produce something new.
  • Key insight: the moment a society starts loudly calling for restoration is often the moment when continuity has already broken down. The past moves from lived tradition to a conscious reference point.

(c) Restoration and invention Must know

Hobsbawm is sceptical of the language of "restoration" because it is rarely literal. It always involves selection, reconstruction or reinvention.

Example: Zapata and Morelos (Mexico) The peasant movement of Morelos under Zapata tried to restore society to what it had been forty years earlier, before the era of Porfirio Díaz. It failed to restore the past literally: (1) it involved reconstructing what could not be accurately remembered (e.g. precise boundaries of disputed common lands) and imagining "what ought to have been"; (2) the hated innovation was not "a bullet lodged in the flesh" that could be surgically removed but part of a wider social change; (3) the effort mobilised forces with far greater effects. The peasants became a revolutionary power and the attempt to turn the clock back became a social revolution that helped produce "a historically unprecedented new Mexico". (Hobsbawm draws on John Womack's biography of Zapata, 1969.)

(d) The past as artefact or fabrication Must know

  • If the past invoked is remote enough, it becomes little more than a language for expressing present aspirations. It may turn into an artefact or, "in less flattering terms, a fabrication".
  • Such a past is weakly connected to historical reality but politically useful, because it can be selectively organised into a story that legitimises the present.
  • Traditions may be recent, even constructed, yet powerful because they claim continuity with something older. Invented pasts can have very real effects.
Examples of remote or fabricated pasts
  • The "Free Anglo-Saxons before the Norman Yoke", and "Merrie England" before the Reformation (used by English radicals, 17th–19th c., mainly as explanatory devices).
  • The "Charlemagne" metaphor, used since Napoleon I for schemes of European unity, though nobody wants to recreate 8th–9th-century Europe.
  • The name "Ghana" transfers the history of one part of Africa to another, geographically remote and historically different.
  • The Zionist claim to return to the pre-diaspora past in Israel was in practice the negation of 2,000 years of actual Jewish history.
  • Nationalist movements are the clearest case: in Renan's words, nations "get their history wrong". Their objectives are unprecedented, yet they define them historically, especially territorial claims and deliberate archaism (Welsh neo-druids, Hebrew revived as a spoken secular language, the Nazi Ordensburgen). These are not revivals but "innovations using or purporting to use elements of a historic past, real or imaginary". "History is the most easily worked raw material" for manufacturing nations.
  • Contrast: attempts in genuinely traditional societies to restore remote regimes are meant literally, e.g. Peruvian peasant risings (to the 1920s) to restore the Inca Empire, or Chinese movements to restore the Ming. For Peruvian peasants the Incas were "yesterday"; to apply chronology to them "is to introduce anachronism".

(e) Innovation, progress and modernity: the past as PROCESS Must know

  • Systematic rejection of the past happens only when innovation is seen as inescapable and desirable, i.e. as "progress".
  • In modern culture, words like "new" and "revolutionary" (as in advertising) have become synonyms for "better".
  • People accept technological innovation (control over nature: science, technology) far more easily than social or moral innovation (e.g. changes in sexual and family relations). "Has there ever been a serious example of Luddism directed against bicycles or transistor radios?" Even popular religion accepted cheap printed holy images, yet violently resisted changes to holy texts. Where useful technology is rejected, it is because of fear of the social disruption it entails.
  • So modernity is not one simple embrace of the new; it produces enthusiasm in some areas and anxiety in others.
  • Once innovation becomes normal, the past cannot function simply as a stable precedent. Paradoxically, the past remains the most useful tool for coping with constant change, in a new form: as process. History becomes a story of development, transformation, evolution and becoming. Examples: Bagehot's Physics and Politics (1872); modern ideas of "modernization".
  • What legitimises the present is no longer the past as reference points (e.g. Magna Carta) or as duration (e.g. the age of parliamentary institutions), but the past as a process of becoming the present. "Faced with the overriding reality of change, even conservative thought becomes historicist."
  • The paradox: "the more change dominates social life, the more important history becomes as an analytical tool." Even innovation-proud societies remain deeply historical.
In plain words: the problem of the future Innovators who want to design a future unlike the past still look for examples, such as socialists citing "primitive communism" as an analytical prop: "a concrete precedent even for the unprecedented". Historicism (extrapolating past trends) is the most popular way to predict, but a contradiction arises. Marx was convinced capitalism would be replaced by socialism yet said almost nothing about what socialist society would be like, because history can show tendencies but not their precise outcome. Utopias need stability, while history is change. So "History ceases to be of use at the very moment when we need it most." We fall back on the past as a repository of precedent chosen for present problems. Examples: what happened to the poor displaced by 19th-century railway building can illuminate the effects of 20th-century urban motorways; "student power" in medieval universities bears on reforming modern universities. This dipping into the past does not replace proper social models.

(f) Genealogy, identity and legitimacy Must know

  • Genealogy means not just family trees but groups' efforts to locate themselves in a lineage.
  • It provides continuity, emotional depth, legitimacy, prestige, and often compensation for insecurity.
  • New nations, new elites, revolutionary movements and rising social groups search for heroic predecessors. They want ancestors, even symbolic ones.
  • Examples: modern revolutionaries claiming Spartacus, Thomas More and Winstanley as ancestors, though Marxist theory says these are irrelevant (Roman slave revolts could not produce communism); school history syllabuses teaching children about Boadicea, King Alfred or Joan of Arc; bourgeois parvenus seeking aristocratic pedigrees; social movements annexing past greatness in proportion to how much they feel they lack it; 20th-c. businessmen hunting elk like aristocrats; the Stalin-era claims that Russians invented everything.
  • Discussion point: Why do modern groups that claim to be creating something new still need to link themselves to older examples? Think of how political parties, social movements and nation-states constantly build lineages.

(g) Chronology and historical thinking Must know

  • For modern historians chronology is essential: sequence, dating, duration, and the avoidance of anachronism. Modern historical consciousness is bound to chronology because it assumes history is about directional change. Anachronism is an "alarm signal" for historians, which is why Macbeth in modern dress shocks today but did not in Jacobean times.
  • But not all uses of the past work like this. In traditional uses (pattern, model, moral storehouse) the moral, symbolic or exemplary value of a story matters more than its date. Examples: Romans and Moors fighting together in Spanish Easter processions; whether Horatius came before Mucius Scaevola matters only to pedants; the value of the Maccabees, Masada and Bar Kokhba to modern Israelis has nothing to do with their dates. (Moses Finley's contrast: Homer's Odysseus vs Samuel Butler's middle-aged Odysseus returning to an ageing wife.)
  • When real time is introduced into such a past (e.g. analysing Homer and the Bible with modern scholarship), it becomes something else, which is socially disturbing and a symptom of transformation.
  • Yet chronology (genealogies, chronicles, precedents) matters for legal, bureaucratic, economic and ritual purposes. The lawyer's search for precedent is present-oriented; the historian cares about the difference of circumstances.

1.4 On History (1997), ch. 3: what can history tell us about the present? Must know

The link between the two texts The 1972 essay studies the social function of historical consciousness (how societies live with the past). Chapter 3 of On History studies the analytical function of historical explanation (how historians think with the past). The movement is from the social uses of the past to the explanatory uses of history. The present itself cannot be understood without historical depth.
  • History helps us understand contemporary society by placing it in sequence, showing its formation, and identifying long-term processes that are invisible if we look only at the present.
  • So history can help us: contextualise present institutions; distinguish continuity from rupture; identify what is actually new; and expose false assumptions about novelty.
  • History is an instrument of demystification. It does not predict the future, but it helps us understand how the present was made.

Why this balance matters: Hobsbawm gives history a serious public role without turning it into prophecy or moral preaching.

Hobsbawm's contribution in one sentence (PPT) History is not only about the past; it is about the ongoing relationship between past and present. Societies use the past to justify themselves, and historians use the past to explain how those societies came to be what they are. That is why history remains indispensable to public life and intellectual inquiry.

2.1 What are historical methods? Must know

  • Historical methods are systematic ways of investigating the past. Research is analytical, not merely descriptive: historians explain why events happened (this connects with Lange's point that historical methods seek causal explanations rather than simple narratives).
  • They include: asking research questions → finding evidence → evaluating sources → comparing societies → explaining change → presenting historical arguments.
  • Source vs evidence: a source is any surviving trace of the past (a letter, coin, tomb, photo). It becomes evidence only when the historian uses it, critically evaluated, to answer a specific question. Backerra: almost anything surviving can become a source if it helps answer a research question.

2.2 Lange: comparative-historical methods Must know

Lange's core argument Comparative-historical analysis (CHA) has produced some of the most influential social science (Adam Smith, Tocqueville, Marx, Max Weber, Barrington Moore, Charles Tilly, Theda Skocpol). Between 1986 and 2010, CHA books won one-quarter of the American Sociological Association's best-book awards, although few sociologists use it. Yet there was no textbook on what the method actually is. Lange fills this gap and argues that CHA is distinctive because it combines diverse methods (a template for methodological pluralism) and balances the particular and the general (ideographic and nomothetic explanation).

CHA's big topic is social change, the pivotal social issue of the past half-millennium: state building, nationalism, capitalist development and industrialisation, technological development, warfare and revolutions, social movements, democratisation, imperialism, secularisation and globalisation. It is prominent in sociology and political science, and more marginal in history, economics and anthropology. (The older name was "comparative-historical sociology"; Mahoney & Rueschemeyer (2003) renamed it comparative-historical analysis.)

The four defining elements of comparative-historical analysis (Lange's Venn diagram)

1 · Within-case methods (the "historical")

Analyse processes over time inside one case. Main types:
  • Causal narrative: a "detective-style" account of processes and their causes.
  • Process tracing: a focused narrative examining the mechanisms linking two phenomena.
  • Pattern matching: uses within-case evidence to test theories.
Works that use within-case methods without analysing temporal processes are not CHA.

2 · Comparative methods (the "comparative")

Compare multiple cases to explore similarities and differences and highlight causal determinants. Types: narrative, Millian, Boolean, statistical comparisons. CHA mostly uses small-N comparisons that attend to context and mechanisms.

3 · Epistemology: social-scientific

Accepts that real causal insight is possible. Postmodern views that deny social-scientific knowledge (because discourses bias all analysis) are "anti-methodological", so they fall outside CHA.

4 · Unit of analysis: meso/macro

"Big structures, large processes, huge comparisons" (Tilly 1984): states, classes, economies, religions, social movements. Individuals matter (Weber; Kalyvas and Petersen on violence) but always in relation to structures and institutions.
What is NOT comparative-historical analysis (and why)
  • Edward Said, Orientalism (1978) and other postmodern works: they compare and narrate, but they deny social-scientific insight.
  • Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2001, 2002) on colonial legacies: social-scientific and macro, but they omit within-case analysis (purely statistical).
  • Tilly, The Contentious French (1986): within-case analysis of France, but no explicit inter-case comparison.
These are excellent works; they just lack one defining element. The boundaries are "very blurred".

Methodology vocabulary Must know

Methodology
A body of practices, procedures and rules used to gain insight into the world. Michael Mann (1981): there is really one methodology in eight steps: formulate a problem → conceptualise variables → make hypotheses → establish a sample → operationalise concepts → gather data → analyse data to test hypotheses → conclude. Only the techniques of analysis differ.
Method
A technique for analysing data (and, by extension, the matching strategy for gathering it).
Insight
Evidence contributing to an understanding of a case or set of cases; CHA cares most about causal insight.
Nomothetic explanation
From Greek nomos, "law": general explanations that apply to many or all cases; associated with positivism and the natural sciences, and often (wrongly) seen as "more scientific".
Ideographic explanation
Explains the causes of a particular case, stressing its uniqueness. Tilly: an ideographic explanation is not "a bungled attempt at generalization".
Positivism
The scientific method is the best way to gain knowledge; at its extreme, it holds that social laws can be discovered.
Verstehen
German, "understanding" (Max Weber): social scientists should aim to understand meaning rather than find laws (interpretivism).
Epistemology
The branch of philosophy dealing with the possibility and scope of knowledge.

CHA rarely uses statistics (its logic of variables differs from that of causal processes, and historical data and case numbers are small) and never laboratory experiments (it studies concrete real-world events, such as "what caused the American civil rights movement?", that cannot be replicated). It borrows data from ethnography and history but differs in its goal: causal explanation rather than description.

Two famous examples Lange uses
  • Reinhard Bendix, Kings or People (1978), a difference-oriented (ideographic) strategy. He compares England, France, Germany, Japan, Russia and China on how popular sovereignty replaced hereditary monarchy. Conclusion: each path was distinctive, with no single route to modernity.
  • Theda Skocpol, States and Social Revolutions (1979), a balance of the nomothetic and ideographic. She shows that state breakdown + peasant mobilisation capacity cause social revolution, but limits the claim to agrarian bureaucracies that were not colonies, in a particular period.
In plain words: why combining methods matters Comparison (looking across cases) pushes you towards general explanations; within-case narrative (looking deep into one case) pushes you towards the particular. Using both stops you from over-generalising ("all revolutions are caused by X") and from treating every case as utterly unique. That is why CHA sits in the "middle" of the ideographic–nomothetic continuum, and why it is criticised from both sides.

2.3 Backerra: source criticism for cultural history Must know

Backerra's core argument Source criticism has been the historian's basic method since the 19th century, and textbooks suggest it has barely changed in 150 years. But cultural historians today ask different questions, use different materials, and so reach different interpretations, often without reflecting on how they are modifying the method. The formal analysis of a source stays largely the same; what changes is the heuristic (questions and sources) and the interpretation. The final step, presentation, remains "largely unreflected" and needs rethinking. Cultural history is avant-garde in theory but works with an established, partly adapted methodology: a tension between theory and method.

History of the method

Humanism"Ad fontes" ("back to the sources") becomes an imperative.
early 1800sThe Tübingen School of biblical criticism develops critical reading of the Bible. Monumenta Germaniae Historica (1819) edits German sources. Niebuhr is an early user of source criticism.
1830sLeopold von Ranke makes source-reading courses a standard part of studying history, using Tübingen methods.
1868Johann Gustav Droysen, Historik (an 84-page outline of his Jena lectures): four steps: heuristic, source analysis, interpretation, presentation. The steps are intertwined ("a continuous back and forth"). Translated into French (1882) and English (E.B. Andrews, 1893).
1889Ernst Bernheim's 500–800-page textbook spreads the method to generations of German students (Erslev in Denmark follows him).
1970s–80sDroysen is rediscovered (Gadamer, Jörn Rüsen, Hayden White). The method is still taught in Germany, Austria, Denmark, Estonia and Finland, but rarely in anglophone countries.
TodayDigital humanities: online databases, digitised editions, text-mining, automatic handwriting transcription (Transkribus).

Droysen's four steps, as taught today Must know

1 · HeuristicDevelop a guiding question; check that it is relevant; search for suitable sources (textual and material). Question and sources shape each other.

Droysen's four kinds of interpretation

  1. Pragmatic: cause and effect.
  2. Of conditions: space, time, resources (geography, chronology, materials).
  3. Biographical / psychological: action as the result of character.
  4. Of ideas: events express the concepts or ideologies of an age.
White compares these with psychological, causal, teleological and ethical interpretation. The Annales school and economic history need analytical (statistical) rather than hermeneutic interpretation.

Forms of presentation

  • Investigative: follows the guiding question and shows the historian's decisions.
  • Narrative: "tell a story" (the 19th-c. norm; White's Metahistory).
  • Didactic: "learn from history"; history as the teacher of humanity.
  • Discursive: the most common academic form; engages in controversies.
Droysen (via White): historians determine what is plausible, not absolute truth, so representation matters.

Common student misconceptions (Backerra): that source analysis is a universal "recipe" (perhaps true); that heuristic–analysis–interpretation is the only way to assess sources (perhaps not); and that this is all there is to Droysen. In practice historians work "instinctively", shaped by criticism and by reading others.

Backerra's case study: early modern diplomatic correspondence Must know

Traditional (political) historians used diplomatic letters mainly to establish "who said what to whom, when and where". For cultural historians that is only the starting point. They ask about actors and their roles, patronage and family networks (Hillard von Thiessen's "diplomacy of the type ancien"), symbols, ceremony, space, emotions, language and knowledge. These interests come from the linguistic, performative, spatial and knowledge "turns".

Examples that show how cultural history "reads" a source differently
  • Layout reveals hierarchy: blank margins grew larger the higher the rank of the addressee; letters were folded and sealed ("letterlocking"); style, language and calligraphy reflected rank. (The lecture's example: a diplomatic letter reveals negotiations, but also social hierarchy through handwriting, language and layout.)
  • Labels: "open" reports could be shown to others; "secret" or "very secret" ones held critical information, sometimes encrypted; "private" meant limited readership (king and ministers), not personal. A letter could be both secret and private.
  • Ceremony was not trivial: ceremonial questions made up about half the instructions for diplomats (Emperor Charles VI, 1728). The imperial envoy to London was told to insist that the Spanish and French ambassadors pay the first formal visit, to show imperial precedence.
  • Newspapers, tokens and society letters reveal networks: Count Philipp Kinsky (imperial envoy, then ambassador in London) never mentions his social network in official letters, but newspapers show him fencing at Figg's club and hosting a ball at Somerset House; a Vauxhall Gardens token marked "His Excellency" dates to 1733 or later (after he became ambassador).
  • Language asserts status: Charles VI wrote to George II in Italian (emperor to king), and the reply came in French, but in German when George wrote as Elector of Brunswick-Lüneburg, a vassal of the emperor. Credentials were in Latin, but because Vienna downgraded "maiestas" to "serenitas", the British envoy carried a French set to secure "majesté".
  • Space and religion: British envoys Waldegrave and Robinson lived in the Freihaus outside Vienna's walls, owned by the Starhemberg family. This shows help from senior officials, and it placed them outside Catholic confessional restrictions so they could provide Protestant worship.
  • Even weather means something: Waldegrave's 1728 complaint "I never knew what Cold was, till I felt it here" coincided with frozen negotiations, so it likely expresses his sense of diplomatic failure.
  • Emotions are performances: displays of emotion are read as constructed performances reflecting rank, origin and the observer's background (history of emotions). Thomas Otte calls personality the "missing dimension".

Cultural history expands sources: traditional history focused on government archives, official documents and political correspondence. Cultural history also studies rituals, symbols, emotions, material culture, literature, newspapers, personal letters and everyday life. It asks not only "What happened?" but also "What did this event mean?". Peter Burke: cultural history is less a separate field than a way of writing history from a particular point of view, and the frontier with social history is hard to locate.

On presentation: British historiography tends towards chronological chapters; German, French and Spanish towards analytical categories. French and German cultural history distrusted "master narratives", but fictional-yet-fact-based narration appears (Ginzburg, The Cheese and the Worms; Schama, Dead Certainties). Backerra asks historians to state their own point of view ("I"/"we") instead of hiding behind neutral language.

3.3 Wallerstein and world-systems analysis (WSA) Must know

In plain words: what is a "world-system"? Immanuel Wallerstein (The Modern World-System, vol. I, 1974) argued that the modern world economy since the 16th century is one system divided by an international division of labour into a core (capital-intensive, high-skill production), a semi-periphery and a periphery (labour-intensive, low-skill suppliers of raw materials). The key phrase: "A world-system is not the system of the world, but a system that is a world", and it can be (and usually has been) smaller than the whole globe. A world-system is the largest unit of social evolution: empires, states, non-state societies and families all exist inside it, with a recursive relationship in which the parts shape the whole and the whole constrains the parts.

(In S8, J. Bhattacharya notes that Wallerstein placed South Asia in the "semi-periphery". David Washbrook rejected this, pointing to strong states, finance and a dynamic bourgeoisie, and Bayly and Ludden called Wallerstein Eurocentric.)

3.4 Hall, ch. 1, "Comparing Globalizations": the argument Must know

Hall's core claims
  1. Reject the idea of one single globalization; there are MULTIPLE globalizations.
  2. Globalizing processes have long historical depth. They are ancient, becoming "truly global" only in the last century or so. Contemporary globalization has new aspects, but these did not appear de novo: "how can one tell what is new if one does not already know what is old?" If processes are cyclical, you need a long view to tell what is new from what is the same process intensified.
  3. Different world-systems emerged at different times (many world-systems since at least the Neolithic, c. 12,000 years ago).
  4. Comparison is more useful than one linear story. What counts as an appropriate comparison depends on the purpose of comparing, not on the objects themselves.

The great debate: one system or many? Must know

Andre Gunder Frank (with Barry Gills, 1993)Christopher Chase-Dunn & Thomas Hall (1997)

Hall jokes that this debate "may appear to be nit-picking about whether or not world-system has a hyphen" and has caused "no end of headaches for copy editors", but the hyphen marks an important theoretical distinction. Others extended WSA into the past: Janet Abu-Lughod, Before European Hegemony: The World System A.D. 1250–1350 (1989), traced Europe-to-China interconnections in the 13th–14th centuries.

Hall's lessons about comparison Supporting

  • Apples and oranges: it is fine to compare apples and oranges if you are discussing fruit; add baseballs if you are discussing spherical objects. The purpose decides what is comparable.
  • Context matters: narrow comparisons give detail and nuance but need larger contexts. The cartoon of the young stock-market whiz who asks "you mean they can go down?" shows how narrow experience restricts analysis. Progress in the social sciences comes from discovering the larger contexts, which can reveal that supposed constants are actually slow-changing variables.
  • Move between levels: some local processes cannot be understood from very broad contexts. Know when to treat something as constant and when as a variable. Very slow changes (e.g. climate change) can become invisible in short spans of noisy data.
  • Emergent change and inflection points: moments when a boundary is crossed and a whole set of phenomena changes, e.g. the "emergence of the state" (like a machine's purpose, which physics and chemistry cannot explain). Because "pristine" states arose several times, there may be systematic processes. If you study only recent decades (as Sklair does), you cannot even frame questions about the rise of states or the modern nation-state.
  • Julian Go compares the 19th-century British and 20th-century American empires using "world-system time" (equivalent phases of hegemonic cycles), showing that the "American Empire" is hardly exceptional.

  3. Turchin & Hall (2003), building on Teggart's Rome and China (1939): synchronised cycles show why low volumes of luxury trade (silk, gems) can have large effects at both ends of a network. Luxury goods can matter as much as the bulk goods Wallerstein emphasised, and this highlights the role of Central Asian pastoral nomads.

WSA is a hallmark of multi- and inter-disciplinary work (anthropology, archaeology, geography, philosophy, political science, sociology, world history). It often studies systems "from the bottom up", from the periphery to the centre (frontiers, indigenous peoples; Hall & Fenelon argue indigenous movements were the original social movements).

3.5 Hall, ch. 8: nation, region and globe Must know

  • Historical "place" can be understood at three levels: nation, region, globe. Each scale reveals different patterns.
  • Historians must move across scales.
  • Nation-state boundaries can be misleading for earlier periods. "India", "China" or "Bangladesh" did not exist in their modern form in the past.
  • This is especially useful for Indian history.

3.6 Why India is central to this discussion Must know

India as a test case

  • India has always contained multiple regions.
  • It has long-standing ties to Asia, Africa and the Indian Ocean.
  • Its history cannot be reduced to one national narrative.
  • It shows both internal diversity and global connectedness.
  • It is ideal for testing nation–region–globe frameworks.

The Indian Ocean: where regional meets global

  • A coherent macro-region with its own historical logic.
  • Also part of wider Afro-Eurasian and global networks.
  • Connected East Africa, Arabia, India and Southeast Asia.
  • Trade, migration, religion and empire moved through it.
  • The best example of regional and global history meeting (developed in S7 and S11).

4.1 The problem: ancient India "without history"? Must know

  • Medieval India had Indo-Muslim chronicles, so the British had fair knowledge of the 700–800 years before them. Ancient India seemed harder to reconstruct: British scholars claimed there were few "genuine" historical records, so ancient history had to be recovered from indirect sources (literature, inscriptions, coins, monuments, sculpture).
  • Sreedharan says the typical Hindu view kept mundane history outside "useful knowledge": there was a vague, exaggerated sense of antiquity but no "genuine historical consciousness as we understand the term". Example (cited by R.C. Majumdar): Mrtyunjay Sharma, chief pundit of the Supreme Court and teacher at Fort William College (founded 1800), published a Bengali history in 1808 with kings ruling for lakhs of years and 119 unknown kings on the throne of Delhi.
  • Robert Orme (historiographer to the Company, 1769–1801) began British historiography on India with Military Transactions of the British Nation in Indostan (1764) and Historical Fragments of the Mughal Empire…, dismissing Hindu gods as "a heap of the greatest absurdities". Francis Gladwin, History of Hindustan (1783), covered Jahangir to Aurangzeb. William Robertson (1792) collected Greek and Roman references (Herodotus, Megasthenes, Pliny, Arrian). Ancient India remained terra incognita, "a land of marvel and mystery".

Why this matters for the exam: the course stresses that history is "built from fragments". This is the methodological challenge that Indology tried to solve.

4.2 Orientalism and Indology: the recovery of ancient India Must know

Indology = the scientific or systematic study of Indian history, languages, literature, religions and culture (with little emphasis on politics). Missionaries, especially the Jesuits, began earlier, but their religious motive outweighed the historical.

Influences behind the Orientalists

  • European Enlightenment (David Kopf): differences between peoples come from custom and culture, not from unchanging nature, leading to cultural relativism, tolerance and appreciation (Voltaire thought India and China invented the arts before Europe).
  • Romanticism: fascination with ancient, distant and mysterious civilisations.
  • A new scientific, critical spirit in handling sources (after the Scientific Revolution).
  • Comparative scholarship.
  • Colonial administrative needs: officials had to know Indian languages, law and culture; linguistic skill was key to promotion.

William Jones (1746–1794) and the Asiatic Society of Bengal Must know

  • A Welsh philologist, linguist and Orientalist (Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Arabic, Persian); appointed judge of the Supreme Court at Calcutta in 1783.
  • January 1784: founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal, supported by Hastings and consciously modelled on the Royal Society of London. It institutionalised Indological research, encouraged translations, supported source collection, and modelled organised scholarly inquiry. Indology became systematic, collaborative and institutional. (Its journal and the Bibliotheca Indica series published critical editions.)
  • Translations: Shakuntala (1789, epoch-making: five editions in 20 years, a German translation in 1791 that moved Herder and Goethe and the whole Romantic movement), Gita Govinda, and the Manusmriti (published posthumously as The Institutes of Hindu Law). He studied Sanskrit under Pundit Ramlochan.
  • Of his eleven annual discourses to the Society, eight were on history and one on science.

After Jones: two lines of development Must know

(1) Critical study of ancient texts

  • H.T. Colebrooke: a mathematician who first mocked the "Sanskrit-mad" Orientalists' work as "a repository of nonsense", then became Sanskrit-mad himself. Jones's successor (1794); professor of Sanskrit at Fort William College (1800, under Wellesley); Essay on the Vedas (1803). He pictured the Vedic age as a golden age with no sati, caste, idolatry or polytheism, and the early Indians as robust, beef-eating, egalitarian, with tribal republics (yet he thought the Vedas would hardly reward a translator).
  • Anquetil Duperron (French) translated Dara Shukoh's Persian Upanishads (1801–02), which reached Schelling and Schopenhauer (who called it the profoundest philosophy he had read).
  • Eugène Burnouf linked Rig Vedic language with the Zend Avesta; his Essai sur le Pali (1826) first made Buddhism known in the West as a system of thought. Bopp, Grimm, Humboldt established Aryan language relations.
  • Max Müller (1823–1900, German at Oxford): edited the Rig Veda with Sayana's commentary (1845–79), History of Sanskrit Literature (1859), and Sacred Books of the East (1879–1904; 50 non-Christian scriptures in English); comparative religion.
  • Rhys Davids, the Pali Text Society, Fausbøll and Jacobi: Buddhist and Jain texts.

Lecture point: ancient history was rebuilt from religious, literary, linguistic and philosophical texts, which raised methodological challenges: texts had to be dated, interpreted, compared and used carefully.

(2) Epigraphy, numismatics and archaeology

  • Epigraphy = the study of inscriptions; numismatics = the study of coins.
  • Context: Champollion read the hieroglyphs (1822); Rawlinson, cuneiform (1851); Schliemann and Evans dug up Troy and Minoa.
  • Charles Wilkins used Pala inscriptions (1781) and read Gupta Brahmi at Gaya (1785), revealing the Maukharis.
  • James Prinsep (1799–1840), assay-master of the Calcutta Mint, studied facsimiles every morning for seven years and in 1834 deciphered the Old Brahmi of Ashoka's inscriptions. This turned the legendary Buddhist emperor into the historical Ashoka and opened the history of Buddhism to investigation. ("The decipherment of a script can open an entire historical world.") Indian Antiquities (1858).
  • Alexander Cunningham (1814–1893), a Bengal Army engineer inspired by Prinsep and the pioneer of Indian archaeology, associated with the Archaeological Survey of India (director 1870): Sarnath (1837), Sanchi (1850), Taxila (1870–85). Archaeology includes everything, even ploughs and irrigation; finds are "fossil fragments of the great skeleton of lost Indian history". He published the first Harappan seal, but was not interested in settlement sites.
  • Colin Mackenzie collected 8,000+ south Indian inscriptions. Later Harappa and Mohenjo-daro upset the belief that Indian civilisation began with the Vedic Aryans. In the 19th century, three-quarters of ancient and medieval Indian history was read from inscriptions.

Significance of the Orientalist recovery Must know

Achievements
  • Introduced modern historical methods into Indian history.
  • Developed source criticism, philology, epigraphy, archaeology and numismatics.
  • Established chronological anchors (325 BC).
  • Revealed Indian literature and philosophy to the world. A.A. MacDonell: "Since the Renaissance there has been no event of such world-wide significance… as the discovery of Sanskrit literature."
  • Restored India to a major place in world civilisation, a "Renaissance in the East". Irony: as India passed under British rule, British Orientalists showed it as a creative civilisation with 3,000 years of continuity: "no mean spiritual triumph for a people so physically crushed".
The "seamy side" (Sreedharan)
  • The language-race link was false, and the Aryan race theory is now discarded. Yet White Aryan superiority became a basic assumption of imperialism and a justification for British rule.
  • Aryan invasion theory: a superior conquering race subdued "inferior" Dravidians and Austrics, segregated them through caste, and founded Indian civilisation. This explained upper-caste superiority and an Aryan–Dravidian racial divide ("motivated fancy").
  • Everything of value in India was credited to European origins, "unhistorical".
  • Romantic exaggeration (Jones): Sanskrit "more perfect than the Greek"; Plato and Pythagoras borrowed from India; India invented the decimal scale, grammar and chess; even Newtonian theory was in the Vedas. These claims were balm to a conquered people but bred chauvinism, and extreme nationalist historians later "read into the sources things that were not there".

Reading Orientalism through Edward Said (lecture) Must know

Edward Said, Orientalism (1978): European scholarship on "the East" was not only academic inquiry but part of a wider relationship of power, representation and colonial authority. "The Orient" was not simply discovered by European scholars; it was constructed through European systems of knowledge.

James Mill, The History of British India (1818) Must know

  • Begun 1806, published 1818. Mill was a Benthamite utilitarian and East India Company official in London who never visited India. Motive: to apply utilitarian doctrine to the governance of India, with Indian decadence supplying the argument for reform.
  • Method failures: he reached his conclusions before writing; held the Orientalists and their primary-source work in contempt; relied on travellers' reports and hostile, partial witnesses (Orme; Buchanan; Tennant, "a most superficial observer"; Tytler, who knew India only through criminal courts; Abbé Dubois, a missionary); cited hostile testimony and ignored favourable testimony, including the 1813 parliamentary evidence. C.H. Philips: he "attached the greatest weight to the writers who are least entitled to confidence". (A perfect example of what Backerra's source criticism is meant to prevent.)
  • Judgements (Book Two, 500 pages): Hindu laws could only exist "under… one of the rudest and weakest states of the human mind"; Europe even in feudal times was superior; "the Hindu, like the Eunuch, excels in the qualities of a slave"; "human nature in India gained… by passing from a Hindu to a Muhammadan government". British rule was better than either.
  • Periodisation into Hindu, Muslim and British periods, still with us today.
  • Influence: editions in 1818, 1820, 1826, 1840 and 1848 (H.H. Wilson's annotated edition). Macaulay called it "the greatest historical work… since Gibbon"; it shaped his Minute on Indian Education. It was the textbook at Haileybury College (1805–1855), where civil servants were trained, so administrators came to India with "illusions of the permanence of British rule" and "imperial dogmatism".
  • Critics: H.H. Wilson, the leading Orientalist, said the comparisons ignored time and circumstance (were Britons in Caesar's day more civilised than Hindus then?), and that the book destroyed sympathy between ruler and ruled, created aversion to Indians, and encouraged British monopoly of all posts of power and honour, helping lead towards 1857. R.C. Majumdar: "so much prodigious labour has been misdirected to produce a historical work which by any canon of criticism does not deserve the name."

Mountstuart Elphinstone, History of Hindu and Mohammadan India (1841) Supporting

  • A scholar-administrator (governor of Bombay, retired 1827) and a critic of Mill. A Romanticist: human nature differs by region, period and people, so the historian should understand rather than judge (unlike Mill's Enlightenment-utilitarian universalism). His criticism of Mill was implicit and cautious.
  • Used native sources and personal observation (William Erskine's chronicles); rejected the four yugas; started with the Rig Veda (dated 14th c. BC); fixed Chandragupta and counted backwards and forwards. His chronological framework is still broadly accepted.
  • Weak on political history, but "laid the foundations of the cultural history of India": society, religion, arts, trade, the north–south distinction, and even Indian maritime activity and colonisation of Java and Bali. Used at Haileybury from 1841 and hailed as the "Tacitus of India". Yet even he wrote that "want of veracity" was the Hindus' most prominent vice.
  • His followers: Grant Duff, History of the Marathas (1825, from the Peshwa papers, printed at his own expense); Erskine, translation of the Babur-nama; James Tod, Annals and Antiquities of Rajasthan (1812–23), rich in legend and romance.

The Mill tradition persists; late-19th-century imperial ideas Supporting

  • Henry Elliot & John Dowson, The History of India as Told by its own Historians (8 vols, 1867–77): translated Persian chronicles. Elliot's preface poured scorn on Muslim rule, as Mill had on Hindu, so that "our native subjects" would appreciate "the mildness and equity of our rule". All later histories of Muslim rule relied on it.
  • Ideas of the age: race superiority (Lord Rosebery: "What is empire but the predominance of race"); Social Darwinism and "survival of the fittest"; Kipling's "White Man's Burden" (1899: "half-devil and half-child"); the "civilising mission"; Seeley's Expansion of England (1883); Curzon. J.S. Mill justified despotism in India: a despot responsible to a free people 12,000 miles away is better than one responsible to none.

Overview: the assumptions of imperialist historiography Must know
  1. A static, unchanging Indian society (Mill), which influenced Hegel (India outside world history). Christian Lassen recast Mill's three periods as Hegelian thesis (Hindu), antithesis (Muslim), synthesis (Christian).
  2. Oriental despotism: unchanging, politically indifferent villages with no private property in land, and despots on top. Marx turned this into the Asiatic Mode of Production (self-sufficient villages, no private landed property). Maine, Durkheim and Weber accepted it.
  3. Purpose: if India never changed, the British could present themselves as the catalyst of change, the raison d'être of their rule. Even Max Müller's "our Aryan brother" became a reason to explain why Indian Aryans failed (blamed on caste).
  4. Dominance of the political element: British prejudice, moralising, value-laden judgements, focus on the British period, and an English readership. Example: Morley told Gokhale that a fur coat needed in Canada was not needed in the Deccan (denying India the self-government given to Canada and Australia).

4.4 Indian nationalist historiography: history as cultural vindication Must know

Context After a phase of imitating Western ways, educated Indians wanted to make India "more Indian and less English", to revitalise rather than replace their culture (as Ram Mohun Roy wanted). This "renaissance" needed a historical consciousness. Bankim Chandra Chatterjee: "There is no Hindu history. Who will praise our noble qualities if we do not praise them ourselves?… The Hindus have no such glorious qualities simply because there is no written evidence." In the first half of the 20th century, nationalism supplied the ideology for historical investigation.

What nationalists were reacting against Supporting

  • Mill's condemnation; Elphinstone on Hindu "want of veracity" and his surprise that the Arabs did not overrun India; Smith on European military superiority and India's "endemic chaos".
  • R.C. Majumdar lists the belittling: Elphinstone assumed that India's foreign trade was carried by Greeks and Arabs despite the Periplus; the lowest possible dates for the Vedas and epics; claims that Indians borrowed everything (the epics from Homer, drama, mathematics and astronomy from the Greeks, the Krishna cult from Christ).
  • Joseph Needham: the trigonometric sine was used in India from the Gupta period, not by the Greeks, yet Tannery assumed it must be Greek simply because Indians could not have invented it.
  • Selective focus on weaknesses: Hindu sati condemned while European witch-hunting was forgotten; caste condemned while slavery and serfdom were passed over.

Definition (R.C. Majumdar): nationalist historians are Indians who examine or re-examine "points of national interest… which have been misunderstood or misconceived or wrongly represented". This is "not necessarily in conflict with a scientific and critical study"; a nationalist historian is not necessarily "a propagandist or a charlatan".

  • Communalism: Hindu accounts of Rajput, Maratha and Sikh struggles against Muslims provoked Muslim counter-histories. "Communalism… had its roots in the writing of history, too."
Strengths
  • The greatest stimulant of historical studies in India: national feeling drove scholars into the sources (monuments, epigraphs, coins, literature).
  • Thapar: by writing against imperialism they took a fresh look at sources; history moved from the antiquarian's collection of facts to narration and interpretation; debates led into social and economic history.
  • The growth of regional and local history (Bengal, Maharashtra; Nilakanta Sastri's south India) corrected generalisations based on the Ganges heartland.
  • The birth of economic history: William Digby, Prosperous British India; Naoroji's "drain theory"; R.C. Dutt's Economic History of India (1904) located India's malady in the agrarian problem, which became the economic platform of the national movement.
  • Cultural history (Tagore's idea; Panikkar: "we had each to 'discover India for ourselves'… a spiritual adventure").

4.5 The Marxist phase: the "new history" Must know

What made it new Influential after independence. Not all its practitioners were Marxists, but they adopted a materialist interpretation (dialectical materialism) and treated history as a social science, studying the relation between social and economic organisation and historical events. Crucially, it called "not so much for new evidence as for a re-reading of the sources with a different set of questions in mind." Focus: class relations, modes of production, agrarian structures, surplus extraction, caste and economy, state formation. Key figures: D.D. Kosambi, R.S. Sharma, Irfan Habib, Romila Thapar, Bipan Chandra ("Marxist-nationalist") and Sumit Sarkar.

D.D. Kosambi (1907–1966): the patriarch

  • Works: An Introduction to the Study of Indian History (1956) and The Culture and Civilization of Ancient India in Historical Outline (1965) "revolutionised Indian historiography"; also Myth and Reality, Exasperating Essays. No writer since Mill and Smith had so deeply influenced Indian history.
  • Definition of history: "the presentation, in chronological order, of successive developments in the means and relations of production." Reliable records and chronology for ancient India are lacking, but we can know how people lived. "Man makes himself" (Gordon Childe) by making tools; social organisation cannot be more advanced than the means of production. This is the only definition that allows treatment of prehistory.
  • Comparative and interdisciplinary method: India is full of "living survivals of the dead past". He used archaeology, field work on microliths and routes, ethnography (tribal clusters, red-painted village deities as relics of blood sacrifice, joint burials), philology (Hittite khatti = Sanskrit kshatriya), and statistics (weighing punch-marked coins).
  • From tribe to caste: plough agriculture, introduced through Brahmanical settlement, broke tribes and clans, and caste became the new form of organisation; clan totems became caste names; local cults were Sanskritised.
  • Myths reflect social change (Pururavas and Urvasi: sacred marriage, the transition from matriliny to patriliny).
  • Indus: thought the Harappans had no plough and that Aryans destroyed the dams (Indra freeing the waters from Vrtra). Thapar corrects him: the plough was known even pre-Harappan, and the Vedic word for the plough is non-Aryan.
  • Aryans: rejected an Aryan race but accepted Aryan-speaking conquerors (iron, horse); Indian tradition was an amalgam of Aryan and non-Aryan from the start.
  • Buddhism and Jainism: economic causes (technology, detribalisation, urbanism, new classes of grihapatis (landowning peasants) and shreshtis (rich traders) who wanted peace and a universal ethic). Ahimsa had an economic basis: Vedic sacrifice consumed cattle needed for plough agriculture. Mercantile patronage rooted these faiths more deeply than royal patronage did.
  • Thapar's assessment: not a mechanical Marxist. He rejected the Asiatic Mode of Production wholesale and qualified feudalism.

Assessment: why Marxist history was an advance
  • Broadened the scope from state to society, from soldiers and statesmen to the people; dynastic history went out of fashion and politics became a backdrop.
  • Interdisciplinary and primary sources: linguistics, sociology, anthropology, statistics; archaeology (PGW, NBP) seen as bias-free physical evidence (Sharma's Urban Decay is almost wholly archaeological).
  • Strong on origins: state formation in the Ganga valley (Kosambi, Sharma, Thapar) was "a triumph of materialist historiography".
  • Explanation and interpretation instead of narrative; processes over events.
  • Critique of Western clichés: the Asiatic Mode of Production, Oriental despotism, the Aryan race, static society; evidence of urbanism and social mobility.

  • Interpretation must not leave the factual basis.

4.6 The Cambridge School: politics, patronage and local interests Must know

  • Associated with Anil Seal, John Gallagher, Gordon Johnson and Christopher Bayly.
  • Challenged nationalist interpretations of the freedom movement.
  • Emphasised local politics, patron–client networks, elite competition, provincial interests, factionalism, and collaboration and negotiation with colonial power.
  • Saw Indian nationalism less as a unified idealist movement and more as a product of political mobilisation and elite bargaining (Sreedharan's chapter 15 calls this the view that nationalism was the "response of the Indian bourgeois elite to the stimulus provided by British ideas, institutions, opportunities and resources").
Evaluation It usefully highlighted local politics and networks, but critics argue it underestimated ideology, mass politics, anti-colonial commitment and popular nationalism. Bipan Chandra and the Marxists attacked it, and Subaltern Studies (Guha) attacked it as elitist.

4.7 Subaltern Studies: a historiography of protest ("history from below") Must know

Ranajit Guha's argument (Subaltern Studies vol. 1, 1982) The historiography of Indian nationalism suffered from two kinds of elitism, both treating nationalism as an elite achievement: (1) colonial/imperialist (and Cambridge) history, in which nationalism is the response of Indian elites to British stimuli; and (2) Indian nationalist history, in which Indian elites led the people from subjection to freedom. Neither recognises the contribution made by the people on their own, so neither can explain popular upsurges like the anti-Rowlatt agitation of 1919 or Quit India (1942). Parallel to elite politics there was an autonomous domain of people's politics, whose principal actors were the subaltern classes.
  • "Subaltern" is a term from Antonio Gramsci meaning "of inferior rank", whether of class, caste, age, gender or office. Associated especially with Ranajit Guha and Gayatri Spivak; a collective of the 1980s.
  • Focus: peasants, workers, tribals/adivasis, lower castes, women, marginalised communities; popular initiative, insurgency, consciousness, autonomous politics; subaltern consciousness (religion, superstition, views of the elite). Guha: the peasant never stumbled into rebellion; he rebelled consciously when petitions failed.
  • Marxian in tone, but it upset conventional Marxist historiography as well. It radically widened the subject of history, giving agency to people earlier seen as "crowds, mobs, or followers".

India through the Eyes of Foreign Travellers: Travel Writing as a Historical Source

Readings: Tansen Sen, "The Travel Records of Chinese Pilgrims Faxian, Xuanzang, and Yijing: Sources for Cross-Cultural Encounters between Ancient China and Ancient India", Education About Asia 11:3 (2006), pp. 24–33 (OT3) · M.S. Khan, "A Twelfth Century Arab Account of Indian Religions and Sects", Arabica 30:2 (1983), pp. 199–208 (OT4)
Where this fits / why it matters Session 4 showed that ancient India seemed to lack "genuine" historical records. So how can historians reconstruct periods with no Indian records? One answer is foreign travellers. This session applies Session 2's source criticism to two traditions: Chinese Buddhist pilgrims (eyewitnesses with a religious purpose) and an Arab scholar (al-Shahrastani, who never came to India). The core lesson: travel writing is both evidence and interpretation. It also opens Part C of the course: travellers reveal a history of connections, not isolation.

6.1 Why study foreign travellers? Must know

Possible sources for India's past: archaeology (physical remains), inscriptions (official records in stone or metal), coins (rulers, dates, trade), literary texts (religious and courtly works), and foreign travellers (outsider accounts of everyday life).

Foreigners often noticed everyday details (diet, dress, city life) that local writers took for granted and rarely recorded, because they were observing an unfamiliar society.

Learning objectives (lecture)

  1. Explain what travel writings are as historical sources.
  2. Identify different motivations behind foreign travel (pilgrimage, trade, faith, curiosity).
  3. Compare Chinese and Arab accounts.
  4. Evaluate their strengths and limitations (eyewitness insight vs bias, language gaps, selective focus).
  5. Appreciate how foreign accounts help reconstruct Indian history where indigenous records are scarce.

What is travel writing? Must know

A description of a society by someone who travelled outside their homeland, blending firsthand experience with the traveller's own outlook. It records geography, economy, cities, religion, customs, trade, political institutions, food and everyday life. It combines four layers:

1 · ObservationWhat the traveller directly witnessed
+
2 · MemoryRecalled later, often years afterward
+
3 · InterpretationFiltered through their own worldview
+
4 · Personal opinionJudgements about people and rulers

Method: how to read a travel account Must know

Travel narratives are not objective mirrors of reality. Historians ask:

QuestionWhy it matters
Why was the traveller visiting?Purpose shapes what they noticed and recorded.
Who was the intended audience?A report for a court differs from private notes (Xuanzang wrote for Emperor Taizong).
What languages did they know?This limits what they could read, ask or verify (al-Biruni knew Sanskrit; al-Shahrastani did not).
What sources did they use?Direct observation, hearsay, or earlier written accounts.
What did they ignore?Absences are as telling as inclusions (al-Shahrastani omits Jainism and the Vedas).
What cultural assumptions shaped them?Their beliefs coloured their judgements (al-Shahrastani's Islamic idea of "prophecy").
Advantages
  • Eyewitness observations: direct testimony often unavailable in Indian sources.
  • Information unavailable elsewhere: details locals had no reason to record.
  • Cross-cultural comparisons: India compared with places they knew.
  • Independent perspective: distance from local politics can mean less bias.
Limitations
  • Language barriers and dependence on interpreters: meaning lost or distorted.
  • Religious bias: faith shaped how customs were judged.
  • Limited geographical experience: most saw only a fraction of the subcontinent.
  • Exaggeration: marvels and dangers overstated.
  • Selective observation: they noticed what struck them, not what was typical.

6.2 The Chinese pilgrims (Tansen Sen) Must know

Sen's framing argument The spread of Buddhism from India to China from the 1st century CE triggered cross-cultural exchanges with a profound impact on Asian and world history, involving missionaries, traders, artisans and medical professionals across Central and Southeast Asia. Chinese pilgrims introduced new texts and doctrines, carried ritual paraphernalia, and wrote detailed accounts. These records created a unique Chinese perception of India, for some a sacred, even Utopian realm, for others a land of "civilized" and sophisticated people. Since Chinese discourse usually described foreigners as uncivilised barbarians, this elevated India's status. The pilgrims' journeys became channels for wider cultural exchange, far beyond the transmission of religious texts alone.

Why these records are valuable (Sen): (1) meticulous accounts of Buddhist doctrines, rituals and monastic institutions in South, Central and Southeast Asia; (2) vital information on social and political conditions in South Asia and the kingdoms on the routes; (3) insight into cross-cultural perceptions and interactions; and (4) evidence of the hardships of long-distance travel, commercial exchange, and the pilgrim–merchant relationship.

Why Chinese pilgrims came (lecture): primary motivation Buddhism, to collect scriptures, visit sacred places linked to the Buddha's life, study Sanskrit, learn from Indian monks, and carry texts back for translation projects in Chinese monasteries.

Faxian (337?–422?) Must know

  • One of the first and perhaps the oldest Chinese monks to travel to India: he left Chang'an in 399, aged over 60, and returned 14 years later, aged about 77.
  • Route: across the Taklamakan desert → Central Asia → India's pilgrimage sites → Sri Lanka → a perilous sea voyage back via Southeast Asia.
  • Purpose (stated in the opening of A Record of the Buddhist Kingdoms): to obtain Vinaya texts (monastic rules). Texts like the Lotus Sutra had been translated by the 3rd–4th centuries, but Vinaya texts, essential for running monasteries, were scarce. (Vinaya + Sutras + Abhidharma = the Tripitaka, the "three baskets".)
  • In Central Asia: in Loulan, people dressed like Chinese but followed Indian customs, and monks read Indian books; in Khotan, every family had a small stupa before its door and monasteries hosted travelling monks.
  • Highlights Buddhist practices and legends: at Taxila, the Buddha (in a previous life) gave his body to a starving tigress; conception at Kapilavastu, birth at Lumbini, nirvana at Kushinagara.
  • Relic veneration: the Buddha's alms-bowl at Peshawar, the tooth relic ceremony in Sri Lanka led by the king. These records spread similar ceremonies to China and created demand for relics.
  • Monks and merchants were mutually beneficial: monks hitchhiked on merchant caravans and ships; traders profited from new demand for ritual goods; monasteries gave traders lodging and health care, and traders gave donations.
  • In 408/409 he sailed on a merchant ship from Tamralipti (eastern India) to Sri Lanka, stayed about two years, then sailed home through Southeast Asia: proof of maritime channels linking India and China, and that sea travel was perilous and navigation rudimentary.
  • A Record of the Buddhist Kingdoms was the first eyewitness account in Chinese of Buddhist practice and pilgrimage sites in Central and South Asia. It was written in the third person, apparently by someone to whom he recounted it (translated by James Legge).
  • Borderland complex (Antonino Forte): Chinese clergy felt melancholy at living in the "borderland", far from the Buddha's land. His companion Daozheng stayed in India: "I wish that I not be reborn in the borderland."
  • Impact on Chinese views of India: Li Daoyuan (d. 527), commenting on the Shui jing, wrote, using Faxian, that south of Mathura is Middle India (Madhyadesha), whose people are rich and "dress and eat like people in China", which was high praise in Chinese terms. (Wordplay: both used Zhongguo, "Middle Kingdom", for Middle India and for China.) This view of India as civilised lasted until about the 10th century.

Lecture summary: Faxian's main interests were Buddhist monasteries, Vinaya texts, pilgrimage centres and maritime travel. His historical contributions: one of the earliest outside accounts of early Gupta India; Buddhist institutions; trade routes linking India, Central Asia and China; Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.

Xuanzang (600?–664): "the greatest Chinese traveller to India" Must know

  • A leading Indophile who promoted Buddhism and the image of India as a holy land, and lobbied his patrons, Tang emperors Taizong (r. 626–49) and Gaozong (r. 649–83), for diplomatic exchange.
  • His book, The Records of the Western Regions Visited During the Great Tang Dynasty, was written for his royal patrons as much as for the clergy, so he appears as both a pious pilgrim and a diplomat for Tang China. (His biography by Huili and Yancong was completed in 664.)
  • Context: by 627 Buddhism was rooted in China; almost all basic texts had been translated; Chinese schools (e.g. Tiantai) were emerging; China transmitted Buddhism to Korea and Japan.
  • Motives: visit sacred sites; dissatisfaction with Chinese translations: "When words are wrong, the meaning is lost, and when a phrase is mistaken, the doctrine becomes distorted." He wanted original texts and Indian teachers.
  • Success: brought back 657 texts and is one of the three best translators in ancient China. Emperor Taizong built the Big (Great) Wild Goose Pagoda in Chang'an to house them.
  • He left without official authorisation, which may explain why he sought audiences with foreign rulers (protection, or to impress Taizong). This gives rare insight into the politics of Central and South Asian courts.
  • Noted Indic influence in Central Asia: Agni, Kucha and Khotan used modified Indic scripts.
  • Fascicle two, a general survey of India: the correct Chinese name for India should be "Yindu" (still used); geography, climate, measurement, concepts of time, urban life and architecture, caste, Brahmin education, Buddhist teaching, law and economy, social norms, eating habits, natural and manufactured products.
  • Fascicle five: Kanauj, capital of Harshavardhana, reached in 637/638. Harsha's empire stretched from north-west Bengal to the river Beas and had restored peace and prosperity to north India for the first time since the Guptas. Harsha had heard of a "compassionate" ruler in China; Xuanzang praised Taizong (reduced taxes, mild punishments). This meeting led to diplomatic relations between Kanauj and the Tang court, acknowledged by Tang official scribes. His motive may have been that the pilgrimage sites and Nalanda lay in Harsha's empire.
  • Borderland dialogue at Nalanda: monks asked why he would return to China, "a borderland where… the Dharma [is] despised; the Buddhas are never born in that country". He replied that the Dharma must be spread, and that China was civilised, with laws and cultured people. This is rare evidence of Indian perceptions of China.
  • His meticulous notes on Bamiyan and Nalanda help modern archaeologists. His reports of urban decay in the Ganges basin (Kushinagara, Vaishali) were used by R.S. Sharma (Indian Feudalism) for early medieval decline.

Lecture summary: he observed cities, universities (Nalanda), politics, society, economy, and religion (Buddhist, Hindu and Jain side by side). Historians value him because he is among the richest sources for 7th-century India; his observation was detailed, systematic and wide-ranging; and his account is often cross-checked against and confirmed by archaeology and inscriptions.

Yijing (635–713) Must know

  • Travelled mainly by sea, 671–695; studied in Srivijaya (Sumatra) on the way. Less studied than Faxian and Xuanzang.
  • Two works, completed and sent home from Srivijaya: A Record of Buddhism as Practised in India Sent Home from the Southern Seas (how doctrine and monastic rules were practised in India), and Memoirs of Eminent Monks who Visited India… (biographies of 56 Chinese monks who went to India in the 7th century, some overland via Central Asia and Tibet, others by sea, some never returning).
  • He wanted to correct "errors" in Chinese practice. He described 40 practices in Indian monasteries (from cleansing after meals to ordination rules) and compared them with China. He allowed compromise: Indians eat with the right hand and never use chopsticks, but since the Vinaya neither allows nor forbids chopsticks, the Chinese may keep them, "for if we obstinately reject their use, people may laugh or complain".
  • Example biography: Xuanzhao went via Tibet and Nepal; Emperor Gaozong sent him back to India for longevity drugs and physicians; he died before returning.
  • His aim: "If you read this Record of mine, you may, without moving one step, travel in all the five countries of India", and to "build a second Rajagriha in the Divine Land of China". All the pilgrims tried to recreate an Indic world in China, so followers could make pilgrimage without the journey and dispel the borderland complex.

Lecture: Yijing focused on monastic discipline, Buddhist education, daily religious life and translation. His contribution: he shows India within a larger Asian Buddhist world, connecting it to Southeast Asian Buddhist centres.

What Chinese pilgrims teach historians (lecture) Their writings reveal Buddhist networks (links between monasteries across Asia), universities (Nalanda), urban life, trade routes (shared by monks and merchants), diplomacy (contacts between courts), and cultural interaction. Key message: religion became a vehicle connecting Asia, moving monks, texts and ideas along the same routes as merchants and goods.

6.3 Arab scholars and al-Shahrastani (M.S. Khan) Must know

Why were Arab scholars interested in India? Supporting

Trade (sea and land routes); the expansion of Islam into South Asia; scientific curiosity (Indian mathematics, astronomy and philosophy fed the translation movement in Baghdad); and comparative religion. Comparative religion has been "rightly acclaimed as one of the great contributions of Muslim civilization" (Franz Rosenthal), because of Muslims' attachment to their own faith, their early direct contact with other world religions, and their interest in philosophy. Religious debates were held at Baghdad.

What al-Shahrastani wrote Supporting

  • Indians are divided into Brahmins, Naturalists and Dualists; the majority are "Sabeans". All fall into five groups: Brahmins, followers of spiritual beings, proponents of heavenly bodies, idol-worshippers, and philosophers.
  • Brahmins are named after a man called "Brahma" (an error); they deny prophecy (four arguments, which Shahrastani rebuts with his own case from the Qur'an). Sub-sects: followers of the Buddha, proponents of meditation (experts in astrology; "meditation… kills [a man] instantly"), adherents of metempsychosis (transmigration).
  • Spiritual beings (divine messengers in human form without a written book): sects Basawiya (Vasudeviya), Bahuwadiya (Mahadeviya), Kabaliya (Kapalika), Bahaduniya. Planet worshippers: sun and moon worshippers.
  • Idol worshippers, five sects: Mahakala worshippers (the great idol at Ujjain), tree worshippers (Vrksa-bhaktas), Dakini followers, water worshippers, fire worshippers (Agnihotra).
  • Philosophers: supposedly followers of "Qalanus", a student of Pythagoras who settled in India (a garbled version of Kalanos, the Indian sage who accompanied Alexander); God is pure Light; ends with an Alexander story in which Indian sages are superior.

Khan's evaluation: how reliable is al-Shahrastani? Must know

Strengths / merits
  • Preserves medieval knowledge that might otherwise be lost.
  • Systematic classification of Indian religions into clear categories.
  • Comparative religion: places Indian belief alongside other traditions.
  • Faithful, objective reporting of what his sources said; he does not denigrate Indian religions or attack the Upanishads or Gita.
  • Unique information on Buddhism not found in other Arabic sources: reward and punishment, the eternity of the world, five virtues (comparable to the Four Noble Truths), lists of ten virtues and ten vices (an expanded Eightfold Path); "Shakamin" = Sakyamuni; Bodhisattvas.
  • Correctly understood why Brahmins "reject prophethood": avatara (God himself descends; no book needed) differs from the Semitic prophet (a human who brings God's book).
Weaknesses / demerits
  • Never visited India; no Sanskrit or Pali; his account is "mainly literary", based on secondary Arabic compilations (paraphrased from Ibn al-Nadim, al-Maqdisi and especially Gardizi, who used Jayhani). He did not use al-Biruni.
  • Inherited errors: Brahma as a man's name; Buddhism treated as a branch of Hinduism (as medieval Europe called Islam a Christian heresy); no knowledge of Mahayana/Hinayana.
  • Omissions: nothing on the Vedas or Jainism; describes sects rather than religions; his material reflects roughly the 8th century.
  • Factual errors: chronology (Buddha "5,000 years" before the Hijra, actually about 1,182); astronomy (Saturn rather than Jupiter as the largest planet); meditation mixed with black magic; the false Pythagoras link.
  • Islamic categories imposed: he defends prophecy with the Qur'an and criticises idol worship as treating idols as divine, unaware of symbolic worship (pratikopasana), which al-Biruni understood.

Khan's fair conclusion: it would be "futile to expect scientific accuracy from an author of the twelfth century". He should be judged by the Arabic information available in the mid-12th century. The chapter mixes fact and fiction, and except for al-Biruni, Arab authors had little or no access to original Indian sources.

Commercial and Cultural Exchanges in the Medieval Indian Ocean World: Baghdad to Canton

Reading: William Bernstein, A Splendid Exchange: How Trade Shaped the World (2008), ch. 4, "The Baghdad–Canton Express: Asia on Five Dirhem a Day", pp. 77–109 (R5). Key sources discussed: Marco Polo · Akhbar al-Sin wa'l-Hind · The Book of the Marvels of India · Ibn Battuta · Tomé Pires.
Where this fits / why it matters Session 6 showed travellers revealing connections. Session 7 shows the system behind those connections: the Indian Ocean as "one of the clearest examples of premodern globalization", with India at the centre. This is the first big application of Session 3's argument: Europe entered an already connected world; it did not create global interconnection. 7.1 The big argument Must know

Core argument (lecture, based on Bernstein)
  • The Indian Ocean was a major arena of premodern globalisation: a dense system of interregional exchange linking many societies over long distances. This is not modern capitalism, but a dense, interregional system.
  • Trade moved goods, people, beliefs and institutions: merchants, sailors, pilgrims, diplomats, technologies and stories travelled together.
  • Exchange depended on ports, monsoons, merchant networks and law.
  • India occupied a strategic middle position: geographically central, commercially productive and culturally influential, both a destination and a relay zone between western and eastern Asia.
  • It was a patterned, durable system, not occasional or accidental, linking East Africa, the Middle East, India, Southeast Asia and China (from the Red Sea to the South China coast), supporting high-value and bulk trade (spices and silk are remembered, but textiles, grain, horses, metals and timber mattered as much), and functioning before European naval control. This challenges Eurocentric narratives.

What moved across the system

  • Goods: silk, spices, porcelain, cotton textiles, horses, ivory, incense, gold; each region supplied what others lacked.
  • People: merchants, sailors, pilgrims, judges, envoys, carrying information and expertise.
  • Ideas: religion, law, stories, customs, technologies, especially Islam.
  • Institutions: taxation, port regulation, contracts and credit, which made trust at a distance possible.
Key idea: trade rested on both material and cultural foundations; goods alone cannot explain how the system worked.

Monsoons and maritime rhythm

  • Seasonal winds structured routes: ships could cross safely only at specific times.
  • Sailing calendars set when merchants left and returned.
  • Most merchants travelled in segments, not whole routes. Bernstein's example: a Gujarati merchant sails the summer (SW) monsoon to Malacca with cotton cloth and indigo, swaps for silk, spices and porcelain, and returns on the winter (NE) monsoon; or goes west to Aden (horses, incense) or Malindi (gold, slaves). Goods on the whole Baghdad–Canton route travelled in at least three different ships.
  • Nature actively shaped exchange: the Indian Ocean world was ecological as much as commercial.

Danger, distance and uncertainty Supporting

  • Before the Chinese invented the magnetic compass (c. 12th century), sailors used the stars; fog and overcast skies were as deadly as storms. Latitude could be measured, but longitude only from the 18th century.
  • Shipwreck, piracy, storms and delay were constant; a voyage that looked short could take over a year.
  • Long-distance trade was profitable precisely because it was risky: scarcity and danger raised the value of whatever survived.
  • Travel narratives reflect both fear and fascination. Exaggeration is not just a flaw; it is evidence of how travellers experienced distance and difference (a source-criticism point, linked to S2 and S6).
"The Great Ocean spreads out over a boundless expanse. There is no knowing east from west; only by observing the sun, moon, and stars was it possible to go forward… The merchants were full of terror, not knowing where they were going."Faxian (5th c.), quoted by Bernstein

7.3 The story in phases (Bernstein's narrative) Supporting

Phase 1: Tang China and the early caliphate, direct trade (7th–9th c.)

  • As the Pax Romana and Han enabled indirect Rome–China trade, the early Islamic and Tang empires stimulated more direct exchange. Chinese sources say Islam reached Canton c.620, before the Prophet's death.
  • The Chinese distinguished "Po-ssi" (Persians), the more numerous seafarers, from "Ta-shih" (Arabs), and from "Fu-lin" (Byzantium). By 758 Muslims in Canton were numerous enough to sack the city. After the Arabs defeated the Sasanians at Ctesiphon (636), Arab and Persian ships sailed directly to China.
  • Trade diasporas: Po-ssi communities along the Chinese coast, followed by Jews; Nestorian Christians (tolerated by Islam as "people of the book") came overland.
  • 750: the Abbasids moved the centre of Islam from landlocked Damascus to riverine Baghdad: "This is the Tigris; there is no obstacle between us and China."
  • The Akhbar route: load at Basra and Siraf → one month from Oman to the Malabar coast (tax of 10–30 dinars per ship) → a month across the Bay of Bengal, provisioning at the Andamans → Kedah (Malaysia) → through the Strait of Malacca or a portage across the peninsula → Indochina (20 days) → Canton (a month). About four months' sailing in theory, over a year in practice.
  • Exchange at Canton: China wanted copper, ivory, incense, turtle shells; Muslims bought gold, pearls, silk and brocade. A government monopoly held goods for six months, took a 30% import duty, and bought what it wanted at the highest price. The Akhbar admired China's literacy, poll tax and old-age pensions, disliked pork and toilet paper, and described tea ("al-sakh"), the West's first encounter with it.
  • Marvels of India: a captain who made seven China voyages safely was astonishing; the Jewish merchant Ishaq's ship carried a million dinars of musk. In the longest story, traders shipwrecked in East Africa are treated kindly by a king, then enslave him and sell him in Oman. Years later he has escaped, converted to Islam in Baghdad and converted his kingdom, and he receives the same merchant kindly: "Musulmans shall know that they may come to us like brothers, since we are Musulmans too." This shows the slave trade and Islam's power to bind a commercial system.
  • Collapse: as the Tang declined, foreign traders became scapegoats; in 878 Huang Chao sacked Canton and killed about 120,000 Muslims, Jews and Christians, and even destroyed mulberry groves. Trade shifted north to Quanzhou (Zaitun). China appointed inspectors of maritime trade (Chau Ju-Kua: "none surpasses the realm of the Arabs").
  • "Sinbad's Way" (Mediterranean → Syrian desert → Tigris/Euphrates → Persian Gulf → Indian Ocean) was the preferred route over the Red Sea (pirates, shoals, adverse winds) and the overland Silk Road (inefficiency, danger, politics). Sinbad was really the scion of a Baghdad trading family, not a sailor; few crew earned salaries, and most traded on their own account. China's silk, sandalwood, spices and porcelain went west for horses, ivory, incense, cottons, gold and copper; rice served as ballast.

Phase 2: the Mongol window and Ibn Battuta (13th–14th c.)

  • Hulagu sacked Baghdad in 1258; the Mamluks stopped the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260). For about a century (c.1260–1360) a chain of Mongol states kept the Silk Road open, which the Polos and Ibn Battuta exploited.
  • Ibn Battuta (b. Tangier 1304), a qadi who made the hajj in 1325 and travelled about 74,000 miles over three decades, selling his expertise in sharia.
  • Delhi under Muhammad bin Tughluq (1325–51): famous for hare-brained schemes (moving the capital to the Deccan, agricultural regimentation, an army against the Mongols); his passion was Islamic law, so he recruited foreign scholars with lavish rewards. Credit and patronage: newcomers had to give expensive gifts to win favour, financed by loans from merchants of China and India, repaid from the sultan's larger counter-gift. Battuta became qadi of Delhi but ran up a 55,000-dinar debt; association with a dissident Sufi brought nine days under guard. He was then made ambassador to China.
  • Sailed from Cambay to Calicut (pepper country, huge Chinese junks); his ships were wrecked or lost. In Samudera (Sumatra, 1345), the first Islamic-ruled place in Southeast Asia, he witnessed the beginning of the conversion that made Indonesia the largest Muslim nation. In China he disliked "paganism" and paper money but admired Zaitun's six boroughs (one for Muslims) and the safety of travel.
  • Polo vs Battuta: Polo was Christian, curious and dependent on the Mongol khans; Battuta was Muslim, uncurious about non-Muslims, and successful in Delhi. Battuta's lack of interest shows Muslim dominance of Asian trade: he could travel 74,000 miles entirely within Dar al-Islam.
  • A shared commercial code: a Muslim importer in Cairo or Tangier and his supplier in Cambay or Malacca obeyed the same religious, ethical and commercial rules. Customs rates were typically 2.5% for Muslims, 5% for protected dhimmi (Christians, Jews), 10% for others (Hindus, animists). The hajj united the trading world, and pilgrims paid their way with spices and textiles, making Jeddah a great market. The Indian Ocean was "no Muslim lake" (Calicut had a Hindu zamorin), yet 14th-century Indian Ocean trade was "essentially one and the same with Dar-al-Islam".

Phase 3: Chinese ships and Zheng He's treasure fleets (1405–1433)

  • Song China created a permanent navy (1132) and built huge ships with iron nails, watertight compartments, stern rudders and the compass. Indian Ocean dhows were sewn with coconut fibre (Marco Polo preferred the Silk Road to sailing on one from Hormuz). Why dhows persisted: tradition, little iron on India's west coast, and being more "beachworthy" (flexible on reefs and shallows).
  • Zheng He (born Ma, a Muslim boy captured in 1382 and made a eunuch) commanded seven voyages, 1405–1433, of about 300 ships and 30,000 men to Malacca, Sumatra, Java, India, Hormuz, the Red Sea and East Africa, following the monsoon. Their purposes were diplomatic, military and symbolic, not opening markets. He stabilised the Strait of Malacca (killing 5,000 pirates, making Malacca a "country" protected from Siam). His interpreter Ma Huan recorded the voyages. A prized cargo was giraffes, taken for the mythical qilin.
  • The end: after Zhu Di's death (1424), the Confucian bureaucracy defeated the eunuchs; voyages stopped after 1433. By 1500, building ships with more than two masts was a capital offence; in 1525 oceangoing ships were banned, so Japanese wako pirates flourished. Bernstein: Confucian contempt for merchants and centralised politics could switch off contact, whereas the decentralised Indian Ocean world produced "Darwinian" competition among trading states.
  • Modern China uses Zheng He to illustrate its "peaceful" foreign policy, but the voyages kidnapped and killed those who refused homage, an example of the political use of the past (S1). Gavin Menzies' claim (1421) that Zheng He reached America is not taken seriously. Da Gama missed the fleets by about 65 years.

Phase 4: Tomé Pires and Malacca on the eve of the Portuguese Must know

  • Tomé Pires, an apothecary, left Lisbon in 1511, worked in India, was sent to Malacca, then became the first European ambassador to China (died in captivity). His Suma Oriental was rediscovered in the 1930s in the "Paris Codex". It gives "a last look at the indigenous world of Asian commerce".
  • Main axis: Cambay (Gujarat) ↔ Malacca. Cambay "with her right arm reaches out towards Aden, and with the other towards Malacca". Cambay's long-distance trade was dominated by Hindu trading castes. Pires praised the Gujaratis: "these people have the cream of the trade… They are men who understand merchandise… Those of our people who want to be clerks and factors ought to go there and learn." That a chauvinistic Portuguese said this "spoke volumes" about Asian sophistication.
  • Malacca was founded c.1400 by Parameswara, a prince of Palembang from the line of Srivijaya, who fled the Javanese Majapahit (malaqa = "hidden fugitive"). It was medieval Asia's Singapore. Pires counted 84 languages (Cairo, Aden, Gujaratis, Malabars, Klings, Bengal, Pegu, Siam, Chinese, Moluccas…). Four harbourmasters handled different regions. Cloth (30 types) came from India; spices, sandalwood, tin, silk and porcelain went west.
  • Why Malacca succeeded (institutions, not just location): low duties (at most 6% for westerners, 3% if settled with a wife, none for "easterners", plus 1–2% "presents", no export duty); an honest legal structure (the bendara as mayor and chief justice; the tumungam as customs judge); a transparent auction with well-known rules, a "medieval eBay". "The law and the prices of merchandise in Malacca are well known."
  • Islam and trade: Parameswara converted to Islam to marry the daughter of the king of Pase (protection against Majapahit); by 1400 most merchants in the strait were Muslim. Bernstein: Islam's backbone is law covering commerce, so embracing it "did wonders for one's credit rating". Southeast Asia was converted not by conquerors but by cloth and spice merchants from Cambay and Calicut who married local women; their children were raised Muslim. Malacca fell to the Portuguese in 1511 under the "dissolute" Mahmud Shah.

7.4 Why India becomes central (lecture, Part Two) Must know

Geography alone is not enough to explain India's role. India was geographically between the Gulf and China, economically vital as producer and intermediary, home to major ports and merchant communities, and a key transmitter of religion and commercial culture.

1 · Intermediary

Bridged the western and eastern circuits; ships from the Gulf or Red Sea reached India before going east; sub-networks converged at Indian ports; cargo was sorted, taxed, repackaged and redirected. India was market and transit zone. Centrality can come from connecting regions, not just producing goods.

2 · Gujarat & Cambay

One of the most sophisticated commercial regions; Cambay a hub to the Gulf, Red Sea, East Africa and Southeast Asia; Gujarati merchants admired even by Pires; cloth, indigo and western Indian trade fuelled larger circuits. A local commercial culture competing on a global stage.

3 · Textiles

"If spices were glamorous, cloth was indispensable." Cotton was among India's most important exports; it linked producers to Asian consumers (not just elites) and was exchanged for spices, silk and porcelain. India was central structurally, not just symbolically.

4 · Malabar & pepper

Malabar was a crucial spice zone; Calicut was built around pepper; Chinese demand was huge (Polo: pepper at Zaitun was 100 times what went to Alexandria). Asian markets were powerful in their own right: decentring Europe.

5 · Ibn Battuta's India

The Delhi Sultanate drew scholars and officials from across Islam: India as a hub for talent and ambition; Tughluq's volatility; credit, patronage and office intertwined; India integrated into a wider Muslim ecumene. Exchange took many forms: Battuta came as a jurist, not a merchant.

6 · From Delhi to the coast

Inland power and maritime trade were connected but distinct; Cambay and Calicut linked hinterland and ocean; coastal prosperity rested on security, predictable taxation and port efficiency; both Muslim and Hindu rulers sustained trade. Look at institutions and incentives, not fixed civilisational labels.
India as a cultural conduit Muslim traders from India carried Islam eastward; commercial networks preceded political conversion; intermarriage created lasting communities; change moved through everyday commerce, not conquest. "Religious and cultural change often happens through households and trade, not just armies."
Malacca: important largely because of India Malacca linked India to China and the Spice Islands; Indians (especially Gujarati and Malabari traders) were among its most important merchants; cloth moved east, spices, silk and porcelain west; low duties and good governance encouraged return. This is "one of the clearest demonstrations of India's centrality to the entire system".
Why this matters for global history (conclusion)
  • Premodern globalisation was real and complex, long before European dominance.
  • Europe entered an already connected world: a later participant, not the creator.
  • India was central geographically, economically, institutionally and culturally all at once.
  • Oceanic exchange shaped later imperial systems, which built upon, disrupted and partly appropriated existing structures.
  • The Indian Ocean was a major arena of "peaceful" global exchange (note the quotation marks: violence, slavery and piracy existed). Its operation depended on winds, ports, merchants, institutions and shared norms. Commercial exchange was also cultural exchange: "to study the history of trade is to study the making of connected worlds."
Summary of Session 7 The medieval Indian Ocean was a durable, patterned system of premodern globalisation from East Africa to China, organised by monsoons (segmented voyages), ports, trade diasporas (Persian, Arab, Jewish, Indian, Chinese), and institutions (taxes, contracts, credit). Islam worked as commercial infrastructure (shared law, hajj, customs rates), easing trade for everyone. The sources (Akhbar, Marvels, Polo, Battuta, Pires) each reflect their authors' concerns. The story runs from Tang–Abbasid direct trade (Canton, 878 massacre), through the Mongol window (Battuta in Tughluq's Delhi), Zheng He's voyages (1405–33) and their end, to Malacca's institutional success and fall (1511). India was central as intermediary, producer (textiles, pepper), market and cultural conduit, with Gujarat/Cambay and Malabar/Calicut as hubs. Europe entered an already connected world.

8.1 What is the Silk Road? Must know

  • The popular view: a single overland route linking China to the West, mainly for silk and luxury goods.
  • The Britannica definition (lecture): starting at Xi'an, a 4,000-mile (6,400 km) caravan track followed the Great Wall north-west, bypassed the Taklamakan desert, climbed the Pamirs, crossed Afghanistan to the Levant, and then crossed the Mediterranean by ship. Few persons travelled the entire route; goods were handled in a staggered progression by middlemen.
  • The term "Silk Road" (Seidenstrasse) was coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877, who drew it as a single line on a map while looking for a railway route from Germany to China (Hansen, plate 2).
  • What the readings show: multiple routes, not one road, carrying people, religions, ideas, commodities, states and ecologies. The lens a historian chooses shapes which Silk Road becomes visible.

Dynamics of the Silk Road (lecture)

  • Goods moved not only through trade but through gifts and dowries: Han rulers kept peace by regularly giving silk and lacquerware to the Xiongnu beyond the Great Wall.
  • Cosmopolitan centres (Chang'an, Samarkand, Dunhuang).
  • Transmission of religions: Christianity, Buddhism, Manichaeism, Zoroastrianism.
  • Cultural expressions: Dunhuang and Bezeklik caves; a Khotan king's portrait at Mogao; Sogdian frescoes at Samarkand; Buddhist art from Tokharistan.

8.3 Hansen: Chang'an as cosmopolitan terminus Must know

Hansen's argument Chang'an was not merely a Chinese capital but a genuinely international hub, home to monks, merchants, craftsmen, officials, refugees, military dependents and religious communities. Overland trade brought few bulk goods; long-distance cargo volumes were smaller than popular memory suggests. What travelled most significantly was not cargo but lives and ideas. Her evidence is above all archaeological: tombs, hoards and paper reused in burials.

The city

  • Modern Xi'an (over 10 million people) was capital of ten dynasties, three of them major unifying ones: Former Han (206 BCE–9 CE), Sui (589–617), Tang (618–907). It was the starting point for travellers west (Xuanzang sought advice in the Western Market from Sogdians) and also for sea travellers, who went overland to the coast.
  • The Sui founder built a new planned city (582). Tang Chang'an: walls about 9.5 × 8.4 km (about 80 sq km); a main avenue 155 m wide; 109 walled quarters (fang) with a night curfew; the palace and government to the north; officials in the less-crowded east, ordinary people in the west.
  • Two markets: the Eastern Market (domestic goods: 220 groups of shops, brushes, iron, cloth, meat, wine, printing) and the Western Market (foreign goods by camel: leather, jewellery, precious stones), with restaurants, inns, warehouses and bank-like institutions. Officials above the fifth rank were barred because the Tang Code viewed commerce as polluting; market officials checked weights and set prices every ten days.
  • Law for foreigners: if foreigners of the same country wronged each other, their home law applied; if of different countries, Chinese law applied.
  • Population about 960,000 (300,000 households), mostly Chinese, with a sizable foreign community around the Western Market. Foreigners came through treaties (after the Eastern Turks surrendered in 631, about 10,000 households, many Sogdian, were moved to Chang'an), as hostage princes, and as refugees (the Sasanian royal heirs Peroz and Narseh after the Muslim conquest of Iran in 651).
  • Religions: 5–6 Zoroastrian temples (four around the Western Market); one Church of the East church. The "Nestorian stele" (781) in the Forest of Steles museum records Aluohan arriving in 635 with 70 Syriac names (it was found in 1625 and delighted the Jesuits). Today only the Big and Small Goose Pagodas survive from Tang Chang'an (the Big Goose Pagoda housed Xuanzang's texts).

Lens 3: archaeology and material culture (Hansen's evidence) Must know

Sogdian tombs: burial hybridity

  • An Jia (d. 579; found 2001; the only undisturbed Sogdian tomb; "top ten discovery"): from a Bukhara family settled at Liangzhou (Wuwei); a sabao (a Chinese-appointed headman of the foreign community). The tomb is Chinese in form (sloping walkway, underground chamber, Chinese epitaph) with Sogdian elements: a stone funeral bed instead of a coffin, a Zoroastrian fire altar, the "Sogdian swirl" dance. Puzzle: his bones lay outside the door. The panels show little commerce and more diplomacy (meeting a Turkish leader; camels with gifts).
  • Shi Wirkak (d. 579; "wolf"; also a sabao at Wuwei): a stone house (sarcophagus) with a bilingual Sogdian–Chinese epitaph (not translations of each other) and the soul crossing the Chinwad Bridge to paradise, a more detailed Zoroastrian scene than any from Iran.
  • 2005: the first Indian ("Brahman") tomb in Xi'an, of Li Cheng.
  • Normal Sogdian practice was exposure, then ossuary; in China they adapted, so the stone bed is like an ossuary "turned inside out". Immigrants adjusted to and modified Chinese practices.

Pawn tickets and the Hejiacun hoard

  • Pawnshop tickets (dated 662–689) from Chang'an, reused to make a figurine buried at Astana (Turfan): 54 transactions, mostly clothing or cloth pawned for about 100 coins at 5% a month (within Tang law). Another set of 608 transactions shows a quarter made by women, so women did leave home despite Confucian ideals. This is everyday economic life, not grand trade.
  • Hejiacun (Hejia Village) hoard, found 1970: over 1,000 items in three pots (gold and silver vessels, gems, medicines, 478 coins). Tax-silver "biscuits" dated 722 and 731 suggest a government storehouse; perhaps buried in a crisis (the An Lushan rebellion of 755?).
  • What it reveals: only a few imported items (sapphires, rubies, topaz, Indian agates, a glass bowl, a Sasanian coin, Japanese coins, an imitation Byzantine coin) against many locally made, hybrid Iranian–Chinese vessels made by migrant Sogdian metalsmiths in Chang'an. This fits the overall pattern: relatively few goods travelled long distances overland, mainly small, light, precious ones; people and skills moved.
Other evidence correcting textbook myths (Hansen's plates) Fewer than 50 Roman gold coins have been found in all of China, many of them imitations used as talismans, not money, and the earliest date only to the 500s CE (so no Han–Rome coin trade). By contrast, over 1,000 Sasanian silver coins circulated in north-west China. Silk often functioned as currency (e.g. paying soldiers at Loulan), not as a luxury good. "Objects and tombs correct the popular myth of endless caravans loaded with luxury goods."

Disruption and decline: Hansen's "change over time"

  • An Lushan (An Rokhshan) rebellion, 755–763, led by a Turco-Sogdian general. The emperor fled; Uighur troops helped suppress it and then sacked Chang'an. Afterwards the Tang blamed Sogdians: the character "An" was removed from street names, and in one account people with "big noses like the Sogdians" were massacred. This was a new and ugly chapter, the first time a ruler targeted a minority group within China. Many foreigners nevertheless stayed.
  • Tibetans pillaged Chang'an in 763. In 787 minister Li Mi cut subsidies for 4,000 foreign envoys, mostly Sogdians who had lived there 40+ years, married, and bought land.
  • Stereotype vs reality: 9th-century "wonder tales" and model legal cases portray Sogdians as fabulously rich jewel merchants, but merchants were only a minority of Sogdians in China (also envoys, soldiers, refugees, farmers, metalsmiths). A stereotype is not proof of a prosperous trade.
  • 843: Manichaeism banned; 845: Buddhism, Zoroastrianism and Christianity banned (to melt statues for coin). Buddhism's ban was lifted in 847; the others never recovered.
  • Shift to the sea: after Chinese troops withdrew from the Taklamakan oases in the 750s, land routes declined and sea travel gained primacy. Faxian's voyage (c.412) was perilous; Yijing (671–695) sailed Guangzhou–Palembang three times ("express boats… stopped for no one"). An Arabic account of 851 describes Guangzhou's 30% tax. Huang Chao (877/878) killed foreigners at Guangzhou, then burned Chang'an's Western Market (881). In 904 the palace was dismantled and floated to Luoyang; the Tang ended in 907, and the Silk Road entered "a quieter era".

8.4 Bhattacharya: Bengal and circulatory centrality Must know

Bhattacharya's argument New policies (India's Look East / Act East since the 1990s and China's Belt and Road Initiative from 2013) have revived memories of old routes that had been marginalised as "frontier" and "periphery". The article argues for the centrality of "Bengal" (Bengal delta, Ganges–Brahmaputra valleys, Tibetan plateau, Himalayas, Arakan hills, Yunnan, and the Indian Ocean networks) in well-connected networks reaching China, Southeast Asia and the globe. Bengal synergised overland and maritime worlds through rivers, mountain agriculture and valley states. It was called peripheral only from the viewpoint of rulers far from the sea (Delhi; and for Yunnan, Beijing). The "Silk Route" was not one road but a web of pathways across land, river and sea: a Southern Silk Route, a Southwest Silk Route (China's term), or a "Cotton Road".

Concepts used (useful vocabulary):

  • Periphery as construct: counter-arguments by Yang Bin (Yunnan's global centrality) and Willem van Schendel (The Bengal Borderland).
  • "Zomia" (James C. Scott): the vast uplands from Vietnam to north-east India, extended by van Schendel to the Himalayas and Tibet.
  • Tirthankar Roy: Bengal as a "series of parallel globalizations"; Bhattacharya stresses linkages within diversity.
  • Braudel's longue durée vs "episodic" (short-term political) history; the article "makes long leaps" across periods.
  • Lefebvre: space as a means of production, control and domination. Richard Eaton: Bengal as a frontier (political, Islamic, agrarian, 13th–18th c.). Nile Green: sacred geographies of memory. Massey: "time-space compression". Appadurai: disjunctive global flows.
  • Wallerstein placed South Asia in the "semi-periphery"; Washbrook rejected this (strong states, finance, bourgeoisie), and Bayly and Ludden called it Eurocentric. But even these critics used a Cold War "area studies" map that made the sub-Himalayan regions peripheral.

Bengal's history in "episodes"

  • Buddhism spread under the Mauryas and flourished under the Palas; sites include Somapura, Salban Vihara, Mahasthangarh, Tamralipti (visited by Xuanzang in 639), Chandraketugarh and Mogalmari.
  • The Senas reinstated a reformed Brahmanism (Shaivism, Vaishnavism, goddess cults).
  • Ilyas Shahi sultanate (1342–57) broke from Delhi; capital at Pandua; two centuries of autonomy.
  • Outsiders' "periphery" images: Marco Polo's "Bangala", with its "peculiar language"; Abul Fazl's Bulghakkhana ("house of turbulence"); Ibn Battuta, guest of the Sufi Shah Jalal in Sylhet (1345), found it magical.
  • Autonomy enabled global ties: two of Zheng He's Ming expeditions reached Pandua (1415) and Gaur (1432); close links with Arakan.
  • Colonial: Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764); Calcutta, the "London of the East"; partitions in 1905 and 1947, and Bangladesh in 1971.

Pre-colonial phase: commodities, routes and networks

Four major economic corridors: (a) Tibet–Bhutan–Nepal–Bengal; (b) Yunnan–Arakan–Bengal; (c) Gangetic valley riverine networks; (d) Kamarupa (Assam)–Bengal.

  • The Periplus (1st c. CE) mentions trade across the Himalayas via Sikkim and the Chumbi valley, with Chinese raw silk and cloth re-exported down the Ganges. 35 mountain passes between Kamarupa and Tibet brought horses to Lakhnauti. Tibet traded gold, rock salt, musk and wool; Nepal exchanged herbs, hides, musk, yak tails and timber for Indian spices, textiles, sandalwood, indigo and jewellery.
  • The Silk Roads took shape from the late 1st century BCE as the Han occupied parts of Central Asia; Rome in the east Mediterranean and the Kushans (1st–4th c.) linked continents. The Southern Silk routes ran from the Tibetan mountains to the Bay of Bengal, Southeast Asia and Guangzhou and Hepu, overland through Burma and Yunnan to Sichuan. They were facilitated by royal patronage and diplomacy, and by monks like Xuanzang, Yijing and Faxian.
  • China is "Cina" in the Mahabharata; "Cinasi"/"Cinapatta" (Chinese silk) in Kautilya's Arthashastra. The Chinese envoy Zhang Qian (sent in 136 BC) noted India–China trade before the 2nd century BC. Most knowledge comes from Chinese records.
  • Popular products and currencies: silver (imported until about the 16th c.), horses (trans-Himalayan), cotton textiles (exported to China, Southeast Asia and the world) and cowries.

Case study 1: cotton textiles, the "Cotton Road"

Chinese sources (e.g. Wang Ta-yuan, c.1349–50, who visited Bengal) praise Bengal's wealth and fine cotton (tu-lo mien, i.e. muslin/malmal). Varieties named in Chinese include pi-pu (bafta), sha-na-pa-fu (shanbaft) and sha-ta-eul (chadar). India had its own cotton technology, and China kept buying Indian cotton even after it grew its own (Stephen Dale). Chinese names for the routes include the South Silk Road, the Southwest Silk Road, the Ancient Yunnan–Burma–India Road and the "Road of the Shu Cloth". Why "Silk Road" and not "Cotton Road"? Richthofen's name stuck in the West (and silk was used as currency), while the Cotton Road was neglected "perhaps due to the imagination of Bengal as a merely peripheral zone".

Case study 2: cowries

Small shells used as low-value money (Bengal cowries were larger with yellow stripes; white ones came from Pegu, Arakan, Siam and Malacca), also hoarded as wealth. Mentioned by Faxian, Xuanzang and Yijing; the Chinese word kaoli comes from Bengali kauri (Yuan–Ming records). Ibn Battuta describes the Maldives–Bengal cowrie trade; Tomé Pires lists links from Malabar to Bengal, Pegu and Arakan; a good chicken cost about 448 cowries in Bengal. Cowries circulated in Chiang Mai, Borneo, Malacca and Yunnan. An unstable currency (farmable, brittle), but its spread maps the outward stretch of the "Southern Silk Road". They were most prevalent under the Palas and Senas.

Colonial phase: restructuring routes Must know

  • Colonialism restructured, rather than simply expanded, mobility (lecture S9): new institutions, technologies and infrastructure reshaped places; it was a rupture in the continuity of trade.
  • Europeans in Bengal: the Portuguese (Bandel factory; Chittagong; slave trade with Arakan), Dutch (Serampore), French (Chandernagore), all overshadowed by the English East India Company.
  • Warren Hastings sent missions to Bhutan and Tibet in the 1770s (Bogle 1774, Hamilton, Turner 1783) to tap existing trade; fairs at Rangpur (1780) and other towns. The Deb Raja of Cooch Behar allowed only non-Europeans free passage to Tibet.
  • After the British annexed the Duars (1841–65), Bhutanese trade suffered (the Sinchula treaty of 1865 was partly violated). Assam's tea industry grew after the Company lost its monopoly (1833). The Permanent Settlement, commercial crops (jute, indigo, sugar), plantations and mining reshaped land and people. Bengal textiles succumbed to the colonial order; profits flowed to the Company, reversing the earlier bullion inflow.
  • Railways, telegraphs and steamships served British advantage. India–China trade via Burma rose about 30% (1896–99). James Rennell (first Surveyor-General, 1765) noted Yunnan was only 220 miles from the Brahmaputra; plans emerged to link Chittagong to the Mandalay–Rangoon railway (1899). Strategic fears (Japan after 1905, France near Yunnan) shaped the 1905 Partition and its parallel railways. The Younghusband mission (1903–04) opened Tibet trade.
  • The Yunnan firm Hengshenggong (early 20th c.) shipped Yunnan tea via Burma, Rangoon, Calcutta, Siliguri and Kalimpong to Lhasa, and Tibetan wool back to India.
  • Ledo (Stilwell) Road (1942), Assam to Kunming through Myanmar, built after Japan cut the Burma Road; closed after independence and now being revived. Bengal's centrality turned into a "peripheral identity" after the capital moved to Delhi in 1912, and buffer states and borders followed.

Postcolonial rupture and 21st-century revival Must know

  • After 1947 (Partition), the 1962 Sino-Indian war, Communist China and the Cold War, political boundaries erected huge walls; states turned inward and away from the sea; ASEAN (1967) and SAARC (1985) separated Southeast from South Asia; area studies reinforced the divide. "The 'cowrie' connections were soon chapters of a forgotten history."
  • New millennium: the Eastern Indian Ocean became an "arc of advantage" within the "Indo-Pacific".
    • India: Look East Policy (1990s, PM Narasimha Rao, announced in Singapore) → Vajpayee's car rally (2004) through the north-east → Act East Policy (PM Modi, East Asia Summit, Nay Pyi Taw, 2014). Regional, evolving across governments, focused on the north-east "periphery"; partly hedging against China.
    • China: Belt and Road Initiative (Silk Road Economic Belt + 21st-century Maritime Silk Road), announced by Xi Jinping in 2013 in Kazakhstan and Indonesia. A global, "one-man-driven" strategy that addresses industrial overcapacity at home and invests in Myanmar, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. Bangladesh is the second-largest recipient of BRI loans; India has not joined BRI.
  • Multilateral groupings in the Bay: BIMSTEC (born 1997 as BIST-EC; now Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand), with the Kaladan multimodal project (Kolkata–Sittwe–Mizoram) and the Asian Trilateral Highway (India–Myanmar–Thailand, Moreh to Mae Sot); BBIN (Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal) for goods and vehicles; BCIM (Bangladesh–China–Myanmar–India), born in Yunnan's "Kunming Initiative" (late 1990s), backed by Li Keqiang and Manmohan Singh (2013), with the K2K (Kolkata–Kunming) car rally (2013) and a 2,800 km corridor; also ReCAAP (anti-piracy), Milan naval exercises and the Shangri-La Dialogue. BCIM's success was arguably impeded by India staying out of BRI.
  • Contestation: Myanmar and Bangladesh are courted by both giants; India boosts naval assets; Beijing's "containment" (90% of China's arms sales go to India's neighbours).
Bhattacharya's critical conclusion: the political use of Silk Road history States have selectively used history (facts, legends, mythologies) for soft diplomacy and grand strategy. BRI appropriated the "Silk Route", which "at no point in history was a single route, nor a static one", as one dominating path from the Mediterranean to the South China Sea, pushing South and Southeast Asian channels into the periphery. Act East likewise reasserts the peripherality of the north-east through a centrally driven national agenda. There is a disconnect between policy and nuanced historical understanding, visible in displacements and walls between overlapping communities. She suggests cosmopolitan heritage sites with many stakeholders as an alternative. (This is Hobsbawm's symbolic restoration and invented past in action.)

The question of scale

City, region, empire, ocean, borderland: each gives a different Silk Road. The most useful historical question: at what scale are we studying connectivity? (Urban archaeology vs longue durée regional history.)

Centre and periphery

Centres are not natural; they are historically produced, and so are peripheries. Chang'an's cosmopolitanism depended on so-called outsiders; Bengal and Yunnan were labelled peripheral by later political narratives yet were central to exchange.

Connectivity has a history

It expands, contracts, reroutes and gets reimagined. Modern projects (BRI, Act East, BCIM, BIMSTEC) invoke old routes. Discussion: are they reviving historical connectivity, or inventing politically useful pasts?

9.1 Sen's main argument (in one box) Must know

Six claims of the article (and of the lecture's "Main argument" slide)
  1. Critique of the "Silk Road" paradigm. Scholarship has focused on the overland routes; one recent volume on the spread of Buddhism had not a single chapter on the sea routes or Southeast Asia. The phrase "maritime Silk Road" is historically misleading.
  2. Buddhism and maritime commerce were tied together. Like the overland spread, the sea spread of Buddhism was closely connected to mercantile activities.
  3. "Long-distance", not "contact", transmission (borrowing Erik Zürcher's model): in the first centuries CE, maritime Southeast Asia was only a "transit zone" where monks changed ships, not a place where Buddhism was established and then passed on step by step.
  4. India–China maritime Buddhist networks were cosmopolitan: Indian, Sogdian and Central Asian merchants and monks, Sri Lankan nuns, Southeast Asian rulers.
  5. Sri Lanka was not just the home of Theravada; it was also a centre of relic cults and of Tantric (and Mahayana) Buddhism linked to India, Java and China.
  6. Long-term transformation: maritime Buddhist networks lasted longer than the overland ones (into the 19th–20th centuries).

9.2 Why "maritime Silk Road" is a misleading label Must know

Recall: Ferdinand von Richthofen coined Die Seidenstrassen ("Silk Roads") in 1877 for the overland China–Europe routes through Central Asia. Later, every route linking China to the outside world got called a "Silk Road". Sen's objections:

  1. Silk was not the main cargo at sea. Early maritime trade was in beads, precious stones and pearls; later, bulk goods such as incense, pepper, spices and porcelain (the lecture adds ceramics, metals and textiles). Silk was "neither the earliest nor the most commonly traded commodity".
  2. It over-emphasises China. China was a major market and source, but ports, traders and ships from Southeast Asia, India and the Middle East were equally crucial. The Chinese became active in Indian Ocean shipping only from the 10th century.
  3. It underestimates Southeast Asia and Indian Ocean societies. Southeast Asia's rise as an economic, political and religious centre was crucial for the Mediterranean–China sea link.

Sen's alternative: see maritime Asia as a multi-centred commercial world, not a China-centred extension of the Silk Roads.

Deep background: the sea was busy long before Buddhism

  • 2500–1500 BCE: Austronesian migrations created maritime networks linking southern China, mainland Southeast Asia and the Indonesian archipelago.
  • 1st millennium BCE: metal technology and wet-rice cultivation spread through these networks; by the second half of the millennium they reached India. Southeast Asian sailors were as important as South Asian ones; Southeast Asia exported tin and gold and imported semi-precious stones and glass; Indian craftsmen in Southeast Asia made Indian-style goods.
  • 1st c. CE: Funan (Mekong delta, southern Vietnam and Cambodia) rose as a "buffer" between India and China; its ports, especially Oc Eo, trans-shipped cargo between "these two giant markets" (until about the mid-5th century).
  • India: Mauryas (c.324 BCE), then the Kushans, Guptas, Satavahanas, Cheras and Cholas sustained trade. Indo-Roman trade (1st c. CE): Roman coins, beads and glass in Tamil Nadu and Andhra, in exchange for black pepper and textiles.
  • China: under the Qin and Han, rhinoceros horn, ivory, pearls and incense reached the ports of Jiaozhi (north Vietnam), Hepu (Guangxi) and Panyu (Guangzhou). Sima Qian and Ban Gu describe Guangzhou as a rich trading outpost; glass, amber and agate appear in Han elite tombs; by the Jin (265–420) even commoners wore foreign jewellery. With these traders, Buddhist preachers, texts and ritual items came to China.

Sen: the debate is "irrelevant"There is no archaeological evidence of Buddhist monasteries in either eastern Central Asia or maritime Southeast Asia before the 4th century, by which time Buddhism was already in China. The evidence points to both routes: late-2nd-century Buddhist carvings at Mount Kongwang (coastal Jiangsu) suggest the sea; Buddha figures in Han tombs in Sichuan suggest the land. So the transmission came by multiple routes, not one conduit.
In simple words: "long-distance" vs "contact" transmission (Zürcher) Contact transmission works like a relay race: Buddhism spreads to the next region, settles there and builds monasteries, and that region passes it on to the next. Long-distance transmission works like a long-haul flight: monks and texts travel straight from India to China, and the places in between are just stop-overs. Zürcher argued that Central Asian oasis towns (Kucha, Khotan) became real Buddhist centres only from the mid-3rd century, once farming, population and trade grew. Sen applies the same model to the sea: early Southeast Asia was a stop-over ("transit zone").

Better question (Sen): instead of "which route came first", study the complementary, interconnected and sometimes competitive roles of land and sea. Many monks used both; Mahayana-dominated networks grew on the Central Asian land routes, while maritime networks later featured Theravada. The lecture calls this balanced view (land and sea complemented each other; sea did not "replace" land) one of the article's greatest strengths.

9.4 Monks and merchants: partners, but not always friends Must know

Reciprocity (the dominant model)

  • James Heitzman: within South Asia, Buddhist monasteries stood near commercial towns and trade routes, in mutual support with merchants.
  • Xinru Liu extended this to India–China: an interdependent, reciprocal relationship between monks and merchants.
  • Merchants: carried monks; met the demand for ritual items; financed monasteries and missions; circulated relics and carried manuscripts.
  • Monks and monasteries: met merchants' spiritual needs; protected merchants and gave lodging; created trusted commercial environments; introduced new items into trade.
  • Saptaratna ("seven jewels", Ch. qibao): offerings of precious objects to monasteries created demand for pearls, lapis lazuli and coral exported from India.

Lecture's point: religion did not merely accompany trade; it became embedded within commercial exchange itself. Buddhism expanded because commercial networks already existed.

Antagonism (Sen's addition)

Traders specialised in particular goods and route segments, owed loyalty to particular patrons, and believed in particular deities. Some were Brahmanical or later Muslim, and even Buddhist merchants may have competed over the lucrative trade in texts and ritual items. So the relationship was "more complex than is usually recognized".

Example: Faxian, 411/412. About a month out from Yepoti (Yavadvipa, Java/Sumatra?), a storm struck. The terrified merchants, whom Faxian calls "Brahmins", blamed the Chinese monk for bad luck and wanted to abandon him on an island. His patron (tanyue, Skt. danapati) threatened to report them to the Chinese ruler, who respected monks, and the merchants "faltered".

Sen calls for more study of Buddhist monks' relations with the Brahmanical and Muslim trader networks that dominated intra-Asian routes after the first millennium.

The Buddhagupta inscription (5th c., from Raktamrttika, now in the Indian Museum, Kolkata) records a sea captain's prayers to the Buddha and confirms the Buddhism–seafaring trader link and Kedah's rise.

Srivijaya (7th–13th centuries) Must know

  • Ruled from Palembang (Sumatra); controlled Sumatra, Java and the Malay peninsula, and so the routes between the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea.
  • Used Buddhism in diplomacy: inscriptions at Nalanda, Nagapattinam (east coast of India) and Nakhon Si Thammarat (south Thailand) record Srivijayan donations to monasteries. (These come back in Session 10.)
  • Yijing (635–713) studied there (Ch. Foshi) on his way to Nalanda: "In the fortified city of Fo-che, there are more than one thousand Buddhist monks… Rules and ceremonies are identical [to India]. If a Chinese monk wants to travel to India… he must stay in Fo-che for one or two years." He returned to Sumatra to write his works. His Biographies of eminent monks who travelled to the Western Regions covers 60 Chinese monks, of whom 36 from China and 2 from Korea went to India by sea.
  • Lecture's point: intellectual authority was no longer monopolised by India; knowledge became distributed across maritime Asia.

Tantric Buddhism in Srivijaya (not mentioned by Yijing)

  • An Old Malay inscription of 684 (Palembang) suggests knowledge of the Gandavyuha sutra (Hiram Woodward); the Kota Kapur and Telaga Batu inscriptions also suggest Tantra.
  • Punyodaya (Nati), 7th c.: travelled between China and Cambodia.
  • Vajrabodhi (670–741): to China via Java in the early 8th century. Amoghavajra (d. 774): through the region in the mid-8th century.
  • Bianhong, a Javanese monk, went to China in 780 and studied under Amoghavajra's student Huiguo.
  • Atisa (982–1054), a prince from eastern India, went to Srivijaya in 1012 to study under Dharmakirti for about 13 years; then Vikramashila; invited to Tibet in 1040; carried Dharmakirti's Durbodha aloka, the oldest surviving Southeast Asian Buddhist commentary. An Indian going to Southeast Asia to learn: the flow reversed.

End of the 12th century: Chinese Buddhism took "its own ideological path", away from Indian Tantra, while Bay of Bengal trade (Myanmar, south India, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia) grew stronger, so Theravada from Sri Lanka shaped Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia and Laos (the "Theravada/Pali" networks).

9.7 Sri Lanka reinterpreted: relics, Tantra and the Bay of Bengal Must know

Lecture: "perhaps the most significant contribution of this article" Most histories describe Sri Lanka simply as the centre of Theravada. Sen shows it was also a transit point from the 4th century, a repository of relics, a staging ground for pilgrims, a centre of Mahayana and Tantric traditions, and a diplomatic actor across the Bay of Bengal. Faxian, Vajrabodhi, Amoghavajra (and, in the lecture, Atisa) all passed through; its monasteries were intellectual crossroads, not isolated Theravada centres.

The relic cult and political power

  • Faxian saw the Bodhi-tree sapling (planted in Ashoka's time, r. c.270–232 BCE) and the annual tooth relic ritual at Abhayagiri led by the king. Also famous: the Buddha's alms bowl and footprint.
  • Candrabhanu, a Southeast Asian king (probably from Tambralinga, Malay peninsula), attacked Sri Lanka in 1247 and 1262 to get relics (Culavamsa).
  • Kublai Khan sent an envoy in 1284, perhaps for the alms bowl (after missions to south India in 1272 and 1275).
  • Zheng He captured a Sri Lankan "ruler" in 1410, probably wanting the tooth relic.
  • Rulers believed relics had magical powers to project political power.

Tantric masters and Abhayagiri

  • Vajrabodhi, told in a vision by Amitabha Buddha, went to Sri Lanka, venerated the tooth relic for 6 months at Abhayagiri, saw the footprint at Lankaparvata, then sailed on a "Persian" ship to Srivijaya (where he may have met Amoghavajra) and reached China 719–20.
  • Amoghavajra (some biographies say Sri Lankan by birth) went in 741 after Vajrabodhi's death, on a Kunlun (Southeast Asian) ship, perhaps because of Xuanzong's 740 expulsion of foreign monks, or (Sundberg) to get the Tantric text Sarvatathagata-tattvasamgraha. The king bathed him; he learned from the Sri Lankan monk Samantabhadra; he returned in 746 with a diplomatic mission and the Prajnaparamita sutra.
  • Abhayagiri had Mahayana leanings from the 3rd century; it had ties with Pala Bihar–Bengal (750–1120), Tamralipti, Mahabodhi and Nalanda. Around 800, the abbot of Vikramashila was the Sri Lankan Jayabhadra. Gunawardana: Tantra was influential at Abhayagiri by the 9th century.
Ratu Boko, Java (792): Sri Lanka's influence in Southeast Asian architecture A Sanskrit inscription from Ratu Boko (central Java), dated 792, reads: "This Abhayagiri Vihara here of the Ceylonese [Sinhalese] ascetics… was established." J. G. de Casparis: this is a second Abhayagiri Vihara, either a replica or a building sharing its form or spirit. Véronique Degroot: it was dedicated to Avalokitesvara, and the Sinhalese influence came only in later stages (e.g. the northern platform has four access points instead of one). John Miksic: similar double platforms joined by a "bridge", a wall, rocky sites on the edge of a capital, pools and simple carving, but differences (an east–west axis in Sri Lanka vs north–south in Java) showing Javanese adaptation. Lesson: local adaptation, not copying.

11th–15th centuries: close links between Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Cambodia and Thailand. When the Cholas invaded Sri Lanka (11th c.), monks took refuge in Myanmar; after Vijayabahu I (r. 1055–1110) liberated the island, Sri Lankan and Burmese monks together restored Buddhism there. In the 12th–13th centuries Sri Lankan rulers sent monks, relics and texts to Myanmar; Sri Lankan monks visited Sukhothai, Chiang Mai and Ava and performed purification ceremonies. Modern echo: Anagarika Dharmapala (1864–1933) travelled by sea to India, Japan, China and the USA to make Buddhism a world religion.

Sen's conclusion in brief Buddhism moved through trade routes, carried by monks, traders and other travellers, over both short and long distances. Maritime routes complemented land routes and created unique networks. Southeast Asia, like eastern Central Asia on land, was first a transit zone. Maritime Buddhist links endured longer than overland ones. Southeast Asia's later contribution to Chinese Buddhism needs more study.

9.9 Evaluating Sen (lecture's critical evaluation) Must know

Strengths and contributions

  1. Connected histories: not separate histories of India, China and Southeast Asia, but interconnected networks.
  2. Maritime history: the sea is not empty space but an active historical arena.
  3. Network theory: ideas spread through networks, not political boundaries.
  4. Economic history: trade and religion are mutually reinforcing.
  5. Buddhist studies: the long-term evolution of networks.
  • Interdisciplinary evidence: archaeology, inscriptions, travel accounts, Chinese chronicles, Buddhist biographies, material culture.
  • Strong comparative framework: land and sea complemented each other.
  • Excellent use of mobility: people, objects, texts, rituals and ideas all travelled together.

Limitations

  1. Heavy dependence on Chinese textual sources (Faxian, Yijing, Chinese Buddhist biographies). Recall Session 2 on source criticism: whose voice survives?
  2. Limited local agency: early Southeast Asians appear as intermediaries, not active producers of Buddhist thought. Recent scholarship stresses local adaptation, not passive reception (cf. Ratu Boko).
  3. Merchants generalised into one category; their different motivations need more attention: Tamil merchant guilds, Arab merchants, Malay traders, Gujarati merchants.
  4. Gender almost absent: apart from the Sri Lankan nuns, women get little attention.

10.1 The lecture's conceptual toolkit Must know

India is not simply a land of "many religions"

  • Religions originating in India (Indic): Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism.
  • Religions that arrived from outside: Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Zoroastrianism, Bahá'í.
  • Study their interaction and their relationship with society and politics.
  • The study of religion has shifted from studying religions only "objectively" (as doctrines) to religion as a social and historical phenomenon.

Plurality ≠ Pluralism

  • Plurality is a fact: many religious traditions exist.
  • Pluralism is a value or practice: communities negotiate their coexistence through accommodation, interaction, borrowing, negotiation, competition and conflict.
  • Key question: How do people live with religious difference?

Why "religion" was misunderstood in India: the Judeo-Christian and colonial lens (lecture's closing slides)
  • Non-Western religions were first studied by Christian missionaries through a comparison model that was "Western" and "Christian". The Judeo-Christian model (one God, one book, one church, clear membership) did not suit South Asia, which had competing models of divinity, diverse practice, regional distinctiveness and inclusive religious identities. Only in the 20th century did the pluralist viewpoint dominate the academic study of religion.
  • From 1871 the British census classified the population by caste and community. Despite the varying status of castes and the fluidity of religious identity from region to region, it used uniform categories. Despite a policy of "non-interference", colonial administration enlivened perceptions of caste and community. The census's philosophical assumptions were problematic for South Asia and led to the hardening of religious identities (Muslim or Hindu or Sikh).
  • Connect: this is the same colonial knowledge problem as in Session 4 (Orientalism, Said, Mill) and the "census" step in Session 1's invention of tradition.

10.2 Madan: India's religions (the Introduction) Must know

Madan's main arguments in brief
  1. India is a land of religious plurality at two levels: between religions (the global level) and within each religion (the intra-religious level of sects and movements).
  2. The distinction between "pluralist" Indic religions and "homogeneous" (fundamentalist) religions of foreign origin is "wholly misleading". Any religion with many regional groups and millions of followers develops internal plurality; Islam and Christianity in India are as diverse as Hinduism.
  3. In India religion encompasses the secular; dharma is not the same as the Latin religio.
  4. Plurality is a fact; whether it produced pluralism as an ideology is a separate question. Traditional pluralism was often hierarchical ("tolerate and rank"). Popular folk pluralism (lived) differs from intellectual or secular pluralism (ideological).

(a) Religion, dharma and the numbers

  • Dharma vs religio: dharma is a moral and cosmic order, duty and the right way of life (including law, caste duties and life stages). The Western idea of "religion" as a separate private sphere of belief does not fit; in India religion has traditionally encompassed the secular (politics, economy, family).
  • Hinduism as a "federation of faiths": no single founder, book, creed or church; a loose union of many sects, gods, philosophies and practices.
  • Dalits (formerly "untouchables") occupy an ambiguous place: some reject Hindu identity (e.g. Kancha Ilaiah's Why I am not a Hindu), and many converted (to Buddhism under Ambedkar, or to Christianity).

(c) Buddhism and Jainism Supporting

Buddhism

  • The Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama, 6th–5th c. BCE). The four noble truths: suffering is universal; its cause is craving and attachment; it can end (nirvana/nibbana); the way is the eightfold path. The three refuges: Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha (monastic order).
  • The second council at Vaishali led to a split into Sthaviras (elders) and Mahasanghikas. The Sthavira line became Theravada, which the Mahayanists called "Hinayana" and which survived in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.
  • Mahayana (the bodhisattva ideal; Nagarjuna's philosophy of emptiness); Vajrayana (Tantric), strong in eastern India and Tibet.
  • Decline in India (by about the 12th–13th c.): absorbed by Hinduism (the Buddha as an avatar of Vishnu), loss of patronage, and destruction of monasteries. Revival: B. R. Ambedkar's mass conversion of Dalits in 1956 (neo-Buddhism).
  • Pluralism in Buddhism (Madan): "even the Buddhas are many" (Mahayana), but doctrinal pluralism did not mean sectarian tolerance; Mahayanists "virtually drove" Theravada out to Sri Lanka.

Jainism

  • Mahavira (6th c. BCE), the 24th tirthankara ("ford-maker"); extreme ahimsa (non-violence) and asceticism.
  • Because farming harms living beings, Jains turned to trade and banking: a merchant community (Gujarat, Rajasthan).
  • Split into Shvetambara ("white-clad") and Digambara ("sky-clad", whose monks go naked).
  • Anekantavada ("many-sidedness"): reality has infinite aspects, so no single view is complete. Hence syadvada (conditional assertion, "in some respect it is…"), qualified by four specifications: substance (sva-dravya), location (sva-kshetra), time (sva-kala) and state of being (sva-bhava).

(d) Sikhism Supporting

  • Guru Nanak (1469–1539), a representative of the north Indian sant tradition; nam simran (remembrance of the divine Name); rejected external formalism (rituals, customs, caste distinctions) and offered a "transcendent third path" beyond Hinduism and Islam.
  • Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth and last personal guru, created the Khalsa ("the pure") in 1699 with initiation by pahul (baptism); Khalsa Sikhs keep their hair unshorn and are distinct from both Hindus and Muslims. Authority passed to the Guru Granth Sahib (gurbani).
  • Types: Amritdhari (initiated), Keshdhari (keep hair, uninitiated), Sahajdhari ("slow adopters" who cut their hair). Codes of conduct (rahitnamas) tightened identity. A lax Sikh is a patit (lapsed).
  • History: the kingdom of Ranjit Singh (early 19th c.); reform sects (Nirankari, Namdhari); the Singh Sabhas (late 19th c.) defined Sikhs as separate from Hindus; the Bhindranwale phenomenon (1980s) showed a fundamentalist turn. Madan: the Khalsa version is exclusivist, not pluralist, and the Sikh vision, like the Hindu and Muslim ones, is hierarchical.

(f) Islam in India Must know

  • Begins with the Prophet's hijra (622); the five pillars (shahada, prayer, fasting, zakat, hajj). Madan notes that jihad is not an obligatory "pillar" for Indian Muslims.
  • Arrival by trade before conquest: Arab traders on the Malabar coast, giving rise to the Mapillas (Moplahs), Muslims of mixed Arab–local descent who adopted local customs. Sind was conquered in 712 (Muhammad bin Qasim); later the Turkish sultanates and the Mughals.
  • Local incorporation: converts kept caste-like groups, local customs and saint veneration.
  • Ulama vs Sufis: the ulama (jurists, e.g. the chronicler Barani, who urged rulers to enforce the sharia) vs the Sufis, mystics whose shrines attracted Hindus and Muslims alike. Islam spread largely peacefully (Sufis and trade), not mainly by the sword; at Partition, less than a quarter of the subcontinent's population was Muslim.

Internal diversity of Indian Islam

  • Sunni majority (mostly the Hanafi school of law) and Shia minority.
  • Sufi orders: Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi. In Kashmir, the Rishi order (Nuruddin, 1379–1442), which drew on local ascetic traditions, alongside Iranian influence (Hamadani).
  • 19th-century movements: Deoband (1867, orthodox seminary); Aligarh (Sir Syed, Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, 1875: modern education); Ahl-i-Hadis (scripturalist); Barelwis (defend saint veneration and shrine practice).
  • Ahmadiyah (Mirza Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian, Punjab; claimed to be the mahdi). Recognised as Muslim in India, unlike in Pakistan; Madan reads this as a "general attitude of tolerance".

"Equal respect for all religions", presented as Indian secularism (vs Western separation). Madan: it "does not go very far"; real pluralism must be built by people of faith, not as passive tolerance. Gandhi: the secular state should leave religion to the people.

Two pluralisms

Folk (people's) pluralism is spontaneous, lived social reality: ordinary Indians acknowledge difference as experienced fact, and "social harmony is built on the basis of difference". Intellectual or secular pluralism is ideological, self-conscious and sceptical of religion. 19th-century elites dismissed folk pluralism as ignorance. There is a "hiatus" between the two.
Connect to the model answer (doctrine vs lived religion): Madan's folk pluralism, Marriott's little traditions, Sufi shrines shared by Hindus and Muslims, Mapilla customs, and the gap between Brahmanical texts and village practice are all lived religion. The census of 1871 and the scripture-based Judeo-Christian model are doctrine-centred lenses that hardened identities.

10.3 From Madan to the Indian Ocean: religion on the move Must know

From "diffusion" to "circulation"

  • Traditional model: India → Southeast Asia (one-way "Indianization").
  • Dynamic model: India ↔ Sri Lanka ↔ Southeast Asia ↔ China.
  • Agents: merchants, monks and teachers, pilgrims, rulers. Media: ships, texts, images, temples and monasteries, merchant guilds.
  • "Religion travelled because people travelled; what travelled was transformed in the process."
  • The movement also ran back toward India; Islam spread to Southeast Asia along the same routes.

Who carried religion? Not one agent but several

  • Merchants created mobility.
  • Brahmans brought ritual and kingship.
  • Buddhist monks and teachers brought monasteries, teachings and pilgrimage.
  • Rulers gave patronage and appropriated religion politically.
  • Artisans made images and material culture.

"Commercial mobility created conditions within which religious mobility became possible." The sequence: merchant → settlement → community → religious presence; resident communities allowed practices to become institutionalised.

Religious encounters: porous boundaries Hindu ↔ Buddhist ↔ Jain ↔ Islamic ↔ Christian interaction can produce borrowing, adaptation, shared vocabulary, shared sacred spaces, new institutions and new identities. Lecture images: a temple in Bali; the Ramakien (the Thai Ramayana); Thai religious practice; Mendut temple in Java; Buddhist Tantra from Odisha to Tibet. Encounter does not necessarily mean conversion: traditions may coexist, compete, borrow, adapt and influence art and politics. The Indian Ocean was a cultural space: the sea connected South India, Sri Lanka, the Bay of Bengal, Southeast Asia and South China, carrying goods, people, ideas, institutions and rituals. Ports were where merchants settled and religious specialists met.

10.4 John Guy: Tamil merchants and the Hindu-Buddhist diaspora Must know

Guy's argument It is generally accepted that Hindu-Buddhist beliefs spread in first-millennium Southeast Asia largely through local rulers' initiatives to recruit Brahman priests and Buddhist monks to secure their political and personal welfare. Yet the medium of transmission was the merchant vessels of the Indian Ocean diaspora. So early traces of Indian (mostly Tamil) merchant activity (inscriptions and religious imagery) reveal the early entry of Indic religion into Southeast Asian states. Tamils were dominant among the earliest Indian traders in insular Southeast Asia, supported by successive south Indian kingdoms. The distribution of inscriptions mirrors the trade routes and commercial nodes: coastal sites on rivers, giving access to hinterland riches.

The key interpretive point (lecture) India was not simply "copied". Indic symbols were appropriated and reworked locally: Indic vocabulary + local political context = a new cultural formation. Purnavarman used Vishnu to boost his own kingship. Brahmanical ritual came to be associated with kingship and statecraft, while Buddhism was more linked to commerce (Guy).

"Lands of gold": Tamil merchants, gold and religion

  • Indian merchants were drawn by spices, resins, forest products and especially gold, and because Southeast Asia was the gateway to China. The Silappadikaram mentions Chinese silk at Pompuhar. Buddhist Jatakas call the region Suvarnadvipa ("Island of Gold") and Suvarnabhumi ("Land of Gold").
  • Wat Klong Thom (Krabi, peninsular Thailand): a goldsmith's touchstone with the earliest Tamil inscription in Southeast Asia (Tamil Brahmi, 3rd–4th c.), belonging to a Tamil calling himself "Perumpatan" ("the great goldsmith"). Excavations at Khao Sam Kheo show settlements that processed gold and glass, going beyond mere trading.
  • Takuapa (west coast of the Thai peninsula), early 9th c.: three large Pallava-style Hindu sculptures (the main figure over 2 m tall, made under Brahmanical direction) and a Tamil inscription on a new tank ("Sri Avani-naranam"), linked by Nilakanta Sastri to the Pallava king Nandivarman III (r. 826–50). A tank implies an existing temple. The work was done by three merchant guilds, including the manigramattar (most active 9th–14th c.) and the sena mugattar (perhaps guild militia, known from Javanese inscriptions). Traces of processed gold are found in the soil.
  • "Takola": a place of riches in Suvarnadvipa named in the Buddhist Mahaniddesa (3rd c. CE), perhaps Takuapa, confirming continuous Tamil activity. Interestingly, the earliest reference is Buddhist but the later archaeology is mostly Hindu, as also in the Bujang valley (Kedah).
  • Lobu Tua, Barus (west Sumatra, rich in camphor, benzoin and gold), dated 1088: a Tamil inscription of the ainnurruvar ("the Five Hundred") guild, whose members set up a charitable fund, enforced by withdrawing trading rights ("to step on the spread cloth") from those who failed to pay the fee in gold. Other Tamil inscriptions come from Aceh and an inland Minangkabau site, showing Tamils settled even in the hinterland.
  • Nakhon Si Thammarat: a 7th-c. Sanskrit Buddhist inscription (Wat Mahaeyong); the Chong Koi rock inscription on a community of Shaivite Brahmans, urged to perform rituals correctly and live harmoniously with their host community.
  • Bujang valley (Kedah): two Buddhist steles (stupa engravings with the Buddhist creed, Grantha script, c.450–500). One names "the great sea-captain Buddhagupta, a resident of Raktamrittika" ("Red Earth", Malay Tanah Merah), praying that he and his fellow travellers be successful in their voyage. It was found by Col. James Low in the 1840s. Hindu shrines, late Tang Chinese ceramics and Islamic glass were also found there.
  • Belitung shipwreck (early 9th c., Java Sea): an Arab dhow built of African hardwoods, active in the China–Persian Gulf trade; the first Arab dhow found in Southeast Asian waters. (Links to Session 7.)
  • Early stupas at Sri Ksetra, Nakhon Pathom and Beikthano (Pyu sites).

Quanzhou's lost Hindu temples

  • Remains were found in 1940 when the Ming city wall was demolished (temple stones had been used as filler).
  • Two late-Chola-style pillars and basement reliefs (lotus and lion mouldings) were reused in the Ming renovation of the Kaiyuan (Buddhist) temple: "a remarkable instance of cultural syncretism".
  • The reliefs show Vishnu (with Sri Devi and Bhu Devi; as Narasimha; young Krishna subduing the naga Kaliya) and Shiva as a mendicant; one scene shows an elephant worshipping a linga. These are localised Tamil myths from the Kaveri region.
  • The carving was done by Chinese-trained stonemasons directed by a Tamil sthapati (architect) recruited by the merchant community.
  • Quanzhou is "a final and spectacular manifestation of a long process" of Tamil presence from Coromandel and Malabar to Southeast Asia and China.

Purnavarman, Balaputradeva, Atisa (lecture case studies)

  • Trade routes become religious routes: Tamil coast → Thai peninsula → Malay peninsula → Sumatra and Java → China (evidence: Klong Thom, the Vishnupada, Indic royal titles).
  • Balaputradeva, lord of Suvarnadvipa (Sumatra), founded a monastery at Nalanda; the Pala king Devapala (c.810–847) granted five villages to support it (the Nalanda copper plate, found 1921). Southeast Asian rulers patronised institutions in India; India provided learning.
  • Atisa Dipankara (982–1054): Bengal (born in Vikrampur near Dhaka) → Suvarnadvipa (studied with Dharmakirti, 1012–24, travelling on a merchant ship with students) → Vikramashila → Tibet (1040/1042), "the father of Tibetan Buddhism". The same ship carried commodities and religious knowledge.

10.5 H. P. Ray: Nagapattinam, a multi-religious maritime landscape Must know

Ray's argument Indian maritime history has usually been treated as trade driven by foreign (Western) demand for "oriental" luxuries, while historians marginalised maritime communities, studying ancient India through agrarian expansion and caste (assumed to forbid sea travel). Ray focuses instead on maritime cultural landscapes: the coast, its communities and their religious environment. She places the Buddhist complex at Nagapattinam on the east coast (127 contemporary Buddhist sites on the Tamil coast, plus the Bengal, Orissa and Andhra coasts), challenging Alexander Cunningham's theory (Cunningham was the first Director-General of the ASI) that Buddhism declined in India around the 7th–8th century. She also rejects explaining the 8th–13th centuries only through "Indian feudalism" (R. S. Sharma) or trade and politics. The sacred landscape was multi-religious, not a sequence of "Buddhism, then Hinduism".

Nagapattinam: the port and the vihara

  • Settled since the Neolithic. It has a 16th-c. dargah (Nagore, the saint Shahul Hamid) and, 10 km away, the 17th-c. basilica of Our Lady of Good Health at Velankanni. The 7th-c. Tamil saints Appar and Sambandar (31 couplets) describe a fortified, prosperous city where large ships called vangam anchored for Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.
  • Chinese records of the 1060s–70s mention missions from the Cholas and from Srivijaya; Jambi sent missions to China in 852 and 871.
  • Cudamanivihara (Chulamanivarma-vihara): founded at the initiative of the kings of Srivijaya (around 1005); construction began under Rajaraja I (985–1016) and was completed under Rajendra I (1012–44). The Larger Leyden Plates (Sanskrit, 111 lines, plus Tamil, 332 lines): in his 21st regnal year Rajaraja gave the village of Annaimangalam to the shrine that Mara Vijayottungavarman of the Sailendra family, ruler of Srivijaya and Kataha (Kedah), had built in his father's name, "towering above Mount Meru". The Smaller Leyden Plates (Tamil) list nine units of land.
  • The 1467 Kalyani inscription of the Burmese king Dhammaceti: shipwrecked Burmese monks worshipped at Nagapattinam.

The "Chinese Pagoda" and its destruction

  • Wouter Schouten (Dutch, 1660s) called a brick tower the "Chinese Pagoda" (repeated by Valentyn, 1724).
  • Sir Walter Elliot (1846, on the steamer Hugh Lindsay): "a four-sided tower of three stories constructed of bricks closely fitted together without cement".
  • Despite local objections, the Governor-in-Council approved its demolition (28 Aug 1867) by French Jesuits expelled from Pondicherry, who wanted to build a college there.
  • Finds: five bronze Buddhas in a concealed chamber; 350 Buddhist bronzes (1856–1930s); the Sellur hoard (2004, 42–45 bronzes, 11th–13th c., including a votive stupa with scenes of the Buddha's life and a tiny Buddha inside).

Hybrid Buddhism: what the bronzes and inscriptions show

  • The bronzes include Buddha images and votive stupas, and also Avalokitesvara, Maitreya, Lokesvara, Jambhala, Vasudhara and Tara. Tamil pedestal inscriptions (11th–13th c.) record Buddha epithets and gifts by monks, nuns and lay donors.
  • Rockefeller Buddha (69.2 cm): "for festival procession(s)" at the image-house of Rajendracolapperumpalli; installed by a monk; donor "the prefect of artisan manufactories for the merchants of the eighteen countries". So the Buddha image was treated like a Hindu deity (processions, epithets of divinity like those of Shiva and Vishnu), showing inter-religious communication.
  • Two monasteries: the Cudamanivihara and the Rajendracolapperumpalli. Pauline Scheurleer: some 9th-c. Javanese bronzes used Nagapattinam models.
  • Peter Schalk's three Buddhisms at Nagapattinam: (1) bronze-pedestal Buddhism so close to Shaivism that the two are hard to tell apart; (2) devotional Buddhism mediated by the sage Agastya (in the 12th-c. Viracoliyam and its commentary); (3) the Buddhism of acaryas (teachers), which barely survives.
  • Woodward vs Davidson: Hiram Woodward argues that a shared world of Mantrayana and Yogini Tantras made Indonesia and India "an integral unit well into the ninth century" (e.g. parallel Lalitavistara and Gandavyuha programmes at Borobudur and Tabo). This counters Ronald Davidson, who, following R. S. Sharma's feudalism, sees esoteric Buddhism as a response to the feudalization of Indian society (the yogin as overlord of a mandala of vassals).
  • Travel as Buddhist self-identity (Robert Buswell): the Gandavyuha (part of the Avatamsaka sutra) tells of Prince Sudhana, sent by Manjusri to visit 53 "spiritual friends" of all walks of life, so enlightenment comes through pilgrimage. It was popular in Indonesia, China and Japan, in an environment of trading guilds and merchant travel.

A multi-religious sacred geography

Hindu

The Kayarohanaswami (Shiva) temple (6th-c. origins, 11th-c. structure). The king of Srivijaya gave ornaments to its silver image Nakaiyalakar; his agent donated lamps; Chinese gold coins were given for worship of an Ardhanarisvara image installed by the king of Kidar (Kedah).

Buddhist

The Cudamanivihara and Rajendracolapperumpalli; bronzes; monks and nuns; links to Srivijaya, Burma, China and Java.

Islamic, Christian, Jain

The Nagore dargah (Shahul Hamid), patronised by maraikkayar ship-owners, Nayak rulers and even the Dutch East India Company; the Velankanni basilica; inland at Tiruchirappalli, Jain ascetics' inscriptions (3rd–11th c.), rock-cut shrines and the 8th-c. mosque of Abdullah ibn Muhammad.

Textual complexity (Anne Monius on the Viracoliyam): Chola-era Tamil Shaiva and Vaishnava literature shows saints defeating ignorant Buddhist monks in debate (Cekkilar imagines Buddhism's end), yet Pali sources of the same period show south India as a bastion of Buddhist orthopraxy, with monks called "Coliya" or "Damila". Recognise cultural complexity.

Merchant guilds across the Bay of Bengal (9th–14th c.) Must know

  • Guilds: the Ainurruvar, Manigramam, Nanadesi and Anjuvannam (the last associated with West Asian merchants), with associated craftsmen (weavers, potters and others). Inscriptions cluster in Karnataka (Dharwad–Bijapur, Mysore) and Tamil Nadu (Thanjavur, Tiruchirappalli, Madurai).
  • They kept private armies, donated to temples (some named after them) and built tanks.
  • Two kinds of assembly record: pattana-dharmam (Kannada) / pagudi (Tamil), "shared contribution" to temples (12th–14th c.), and erivira-pattinam, towns where merchants and soldiers lived (11th–12th c. in Tamil Nadu), showing the rising importance of armed guards.

Tamil inscriptions abroad (Jan Wisseman Christie)

Eight mid-9th to late-13th-century Tamil inscriptions in Southeast and East Asia: 1 near Pagan (Burma); 2 south of the Isthmus of Kra; 4 in north and west Sumatra; 1 on the China coast (Quanzhou, bilingual). Earliest: Takuapa (9th c.: a tank and a military camp). 11.1 Part I: what does "connected space" mean? Must know

The lecture's definition The Indian Ocean was a connected space, not a collection of isolated regions, linking East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, Southeast Asia and China. "Connection does not mean sameness; it means structured interaction across distance."

Five forms of connection

  1. Routes: maritime pathways structured by wind, geography and custom.
  2. Shared commercial practices: common norms of trade, credit and exchange.
  3. Migration: people settled, carrying skills, language and ties.
  4. Religious networks: belief and pilgrimage linked distant regions.
  5. Political and military encounters: conflict and diplomacy were also forms of connection, not its opposite.

Why the Indian Ocean matters in world history

  • One of the oldest and most important arenas of interregional exchange.
  • Long-distance trade long before European empire; it had its own deep history.
  • Movement of goods, people, technologies, languages and religious ideas.
  • Crucial to the histories of Islam, India, Southeast Asia, China and East Africa.
  • Helps decentre Europe: European expansion is only one chapter.

Significant point: Indian Ocean history is not a "prelude" to European expansion; it has its own long historical logic.

Discussion question (lecture)

Was the Indian Ocean connected mainly by trade, or did trade itself depend on deeper connections like climate (the monsoon), religion and migration?

Answer sketch: both. The monsoon made trade possible and seasonal (Session 7); religious and diaspora networks gave trust and credit (Sessions 9–10); but trade paid for and spread those networks. Chaudhuri stresses deep structures; Roy stresses commerce.

11.2 Chaudhuri: unity and disunity of Indian Ocean history (1993) Must know

The problem, in simple words Fernand Braudel's The Mediterranean (1949) wrote the history of a whole sea and its surrounding societies in three dimensions: space, time and structures. Chaudhuri (author of Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean, 1985, and Asia Before Europe, 1990) asks: can we do the same for the Indian Ocean? Does it have unity? And why are there so few studies comparing Islam, Sanskritic India, Southeast Asia and China before European colonialism? The sceptics' answer: the Indian Ocean has no tangible unity, there is no clear chronological coherence from the rise of Islam to the 18th century, and Asia is "too complex and diverse", so they avoid the question ("the problem is resolved by denying its existence").

(a) Unity "in the large" vs similarity "in the small"

  • Borrowing from mathematics and Wittgenstein: the question is about similarity "in the large" (the overall form) vs "in the small" (local details). Example: a Möbius strip, a cylinder and a twisted cylinder look identical if you cut out a small square of any of them (alike "in the small"), but their overall shapes differ (the Möbius strip has only one side and one edge). So matching small details do not prove a shared overall form, and differing details do not disprove it.
  • If you compare the Rig Veda, the Confucian texts and the Qur'an word for word, you find dissimilarities "in the small". But Arab sailors could sail from Suez and Basra to Guangzhou and Japan: at the level of transcontinental trade there was an intrinsic unity, a similarity "in the large" with Braudel's Mediterranean.
  • Lecture formula: "Unity in the large, disunity in the small." The Indian Ocean can be a meaningful historical unit without being uniform: unity at the level of space, time and structures (shared routes, long-distance trade, recurring political patterns), always alongside internal differentiation (food, clothing, housing, authority and religion varied enormously).

Why so few intra-Asian comparisons? A historical reason

In the past, Asian writers were curious about their neighbours' civilisations. In the 19th century, with Europe's military and scientific power, Asian historians began to measure their own histories against Europe. Intellectual orientation changed from a latitudinal (east–west, Asia-to-Asia) direction to a longitudinal axis (Asia-to-Europe). Today most Asian historians relate their regions to European categories like feudalism and capitalism.

Compare Session 4 (Marxist and colonial frameworks applied to India) and Session 3 (Eurocentrism).

(b) The problem of chronology: Arasaratnam's critique

S. Arasaratnam argued that Chaudhuri showed a geographic unity but failed to show a chronological unity "for the period A.D. 750 to 1750". Chaudhuri notes the "unconscious slip": his period begins with the rise of Islam, i.e. 622, not 750. The deeper point is a contradiction: a South Asian historian sees spatial unity but South Asian periodisation does not fit an Indian Ocean chronology, while historians of Islam see a clear temporal unity (622 to the 18th c.) but deny that Asia is a unit of space (the Balad al-Islam was a concept, not contiguous territory). Unity of space and unity of time may not coincide. Such contradictions are built into real structures (e.g. a bumper harvest lowers prices, which can ruin peasants who must sell for cash).

(c) Chaudhuri's theory: six domains, four strands, four conclusions

Six domains + logical space

Historical action is an interaction between: (1) real space, (2) perceived space, (3) real time, (4) perceived time, (5) physical structures, (6) perceived structures. A seventh domain, the perception of the other six, is the "logical space" of history.

Braudel himself said (1985): "For me, a structural phenomenon is a phenomenon that persists", nothing to do with Lévi-Strauss's structuralism. Chaudhuri goes further: he wants to identify structures and the principles that classify, group and divide them. Critics (e.g. Gordon Johnson called him a "latter-day Dr Faustus" drawing randomly on all learning) found it incoherent; Chaudhuri replies that the theory is rigorous, and that a cabinetmaker still needs a saw and chisel.

Four strands of theory

  1. Braudel's social, economic and civilisational history and intuitive interpretation (the longue durée).
  2. Structuralism (mainly French social philosophers: Lévi-Strauss, Foucault, Barthes, Bourdieu…).
  3. Semiotics (analysis of signs).
  4. Mathematical set theory (Cantor) to establish continuities and discontinuities.
ConclusionMeaning, in simple words
1. Structural invarianceA basic underlying structure, the "ground floor of material life" (food, clothing, housing, religious principles, technology, production), stayed invariant while varying within limits. (Roman Jakobson: the search for "invariance amid variations".) A distinction is made between structural change and changes inside a structure. Analogy: stomach-lining cells are replaced every few days, but the stomach's function stays the same.
2. Unity as the sum of four civilisationsThe Indian Ocean's structural unity is the sum of four civilisational identities: Islam, Sanskritic India, Southeast Asian societies and Confucian China. Set theory (integration and differentiation, cardinality, the property that identifies a set) marks their boundaries and filters the invariant core. "Islam" is a set whose members may also belong to African, Indian, Javanese or Chinese sets.
3. Totality and binary actionPolitical authority, social forms, economic activity and art form a totality with an indivisible logical space. But action is a binary decision (to act or not), a simple "thermostatic" function. Economic or political action can be separated from the totality only through a theoretical filter.
4. Dynamics and "perpetual memory"The interaction of the long term and the random, of events and tendencies (e.g. Islam changing from an Arab identity into a complex structure with Iranian and Turkish elements). Societies replicate their structures through a "perpetual memory" (like genetic coding), studied with topological transformation. Dali's painting The Persistence of Memory: even if a watch seems to melt, as long as the dial and hands are intact, time can still be read.

(d) The "ground floor" of material life: food, clothing, housing Must know

Food

  • Every society distinguishes "food" vs "non-food" (a universal syntax), but the elements differ; there are separations in space (Islam vs China) and time (breakfast food; famine food; ascetic, sacred, festive and everyday food mark sacred and profane, rich and poor).
  • Two zones: bread (wheat) vs rice. To be invited to a meal = to eat bread or rice. Not symmetrical: in the Middle East people ate both (rice as a luxury), while rice-eaters rejected bread. Dairy disappears in Southeast Asia and China.
  • Invariance: dishes in the Qur'anic tradition, cookbooks and Javanese inscriptions are still cooked today. Porridge of boiled grain fed farmers from Egypt to China.
  • Change: new plants with early Islam; New World crops (maize, tobacco, potatoes, tomatoes, chilli); hot food may date only from the 16th-c. chilli (before that, black pepper gave pungency).
  • Food maps onto social life, but not isomorphically (taste is habit).

Clothing

  • Clothing maps onto social identity almost one-to-one (isomorphic): tiny variations signal gender exactly.
  • Generic distinctions: unsewn, semi-sewn and fully sewn garments.
  • The white unsewn ihram of Muslim pilgrims on the hajj is structurally isomorphic to the ritual dress of Brahmans at worship: different forms, same ritual meaning. (Ordinary Islamic dress is fully sewn.)

Housing

  • In all societies a house is a three-dimensional projection of the human body, replicating power relations within the family and between civic authority and families (e.g. planning laws).
  • Arid zone (west and north): brick or stone houses, grain farming, semi-fortified villages, a tendency to political fragmentation.
  • Wet zone (east and south): bamboo and wooden houses, sloping roofs, banana and coconut, rice fields and fish ponds; intensive, vulnerable irrigation gave a "terrible power of coercion" to military rulers.

Desert and sown: nomads and settled farmers

Agriculture needed draft animals (camels in the Middle East, oxen and buffalo in India, Southeast Asia and China), often supplied by nomads raising stock on marginal land (paradoxically, the drier the climate, the better the cattle). Desert and sown were held in symbiosis: nomads needed grain, dates, sugar, coffee, tea and cloth, but could also exact them as tribute by force; their monopoly of transport animals let them protect caravans and promote urbanisation. Settled societies faced a chronic threat of nomadic conquest.

Four expansionary movements (618–1757)

From the founding of the Tang dynasty (618) to the Battle of Plassey (1757):
  1. the outward projection of Islam;
  2. the projection of imperial China;
  3. the migrations of Turko-Mongol nomads into the Middle East, China and India;
  4. the maritime expansion of Christian Europe.

(e) Time, theory and "unity as a mental construct" Supporting

  • Temporal frequency: the lower the frequency of events, the higher their explanatory power, and vice versa. The founding of the Islamic umma is a rare event with huge influence; the number of meals a person eats is huge but explains almost nothing (to study meals structurally, we assume food habits stay stationary).
  • Foucault: three domains of action, one real (primary relations) and two mental constructs (reflexive and discursive). He was concerned with discontinuities; historians compulsively seek continuity.
  • Gödel (the "Undecidable Theorem", 1931): human language and logic contain inherent contradictions, so there is no single self-consistent truth. Therefore the spatial unity of the Indian Ocean and the chronological unity of 622–1750 are not objective truths but mental constructs. For some analyses treat the ocean as one unit; for others break it up. The same applies to chronology. "Historians create the unity of their subject."
  • "Asia is the contradiction of Europe": the sign "Asia" has no positive content without reference to "Europe" (Asia Before Europe = not-Europe, before colonialism). Likewise, the sea is the antithesis of the desert and the land mass, so by dialectical exclusion both belong to one set, "the Indian Ocean".
  • Why 622–1750 works: it is a whole life cycle, from the start of two great expansions to their symbolic end at Plassey. Ask people in the Middle East, India, Java or China what Islam, Chinese civilisation and colonialism meant to them. Even Sanskritic Indians learned to call themselves "Hindus" through contact with Muslims; "if Charlemagne is inconceivable without Muhammad", post-1947 South Asia is inconceivable without the umma.
  • Mathematicians: Cantor (set theory, the arithmetic continuum), Wittgenstein (logical space, language games; "what cannot be said must be passed over in silence"), Dedekind (1872: continuity is defined by a "cut", i.e. a limit; so the "present" is an irrational concept, since everything perceived is past or future), Russell and Gödel. Their key ideas: mapping (projecting one set's structure onto another) and isomorphism (exact one-to-one correspondence). Continuity and discontinuity, unity and disunity, depend on how we define limits.

11.3 Roy: the Indian Ocean trade, 1500–1800 Must know

Roy's argument Indo-European trade laid the foundation of a new economic order in Asia and Europe: it created new consumer markets in Europe, shaped the world's money markets (silver), motivated colonisation in the 18th century, and transmitted technology in both directions. There was so much two-way traffic that it cannot be described as simple "European influence" or Indian "incorporation" into a European world economy (contrast Wallerstein, Session 3). The hybrid technological, institutional and political order that followed had momentous consequences for India.

(a) The Indian Ocean world at 1500: India at the centre of the "great arc"

  • India sat in the middle of the "great arc" of Asian trade (Om Prakash's phrase): a hinge between the western and eastern circuits. No single merchant group usually connected the extremities; direct West Asia–China trade, always rare, declined after 1400, so Indian ports grew as places to change ships and restock, linking Aden, Hormuz and Kilwa with Malacca.
  • Indian ports were transit points, markets and production centres. Exports: textiles (silk, muslin), rice, sugar, oil, cotton, indigo. Imports: gold and silver, consumer goods, horses, and spices for re-export (Indonesia → Cambay → West Asia → Europe).
  • Route to Europe: Cambay or Calicut → Aden (Red Sea) or Hormuz (Gulf) → Arabs to Suez/Tor, then caravans to Cairo/Alexandria; or Persians to Basra, then caravans to Aleppo/Tripoli → Venetians (plus Turks, Ragusans, Armenians and Greeks). Southern Europeans even considered digging a Suez Canal c.1500.

Merchant communities and ports

  • Cambay: Gujarati Muslims (who expanded in the 15th c. after Arab–China trade stopped, settling in Malacca and Aden).
  • Malabar: Mapilla Muslims.
  • Coromandel: Chulia merchants and Telugu Chettis (Balija and Komati); the main port was Pulicat (linked to Vijayanagar).
  • East: Oriyas and Bengalis; Saptagram and Chittagong traded with Burma, Indonesia and China.
  • Each combined trade with shipbuilding, credit and mobility.
  • No port was firmly tied to an inland empire: the Samudri (Zamorin) of Calicut, Cambay's king, Konkan chiefs. Merchants adapted to pan-regional forces rather than controlling them.
  • Two new hubs: Masulipatnam (Qutb Shahi Golkonda; Persian merchants; Krishna delta) and Surat (sacked by the Portuguese in 1530; vital after the Mughal annexation of Gujarat in 1576).
Lecture: "Europeans entered a crowded and functioning commercial world, not an empty stage." Key ports were Calicut, Cambay, Surat, Malacca, Aden and Hormuz. Ports were interfaces connecting sea, land and political authority, and cargo passed through many merchant groups.

Why so violent? And why did it fail?

  • To oust Muslim merchants from the Malabar and Sumatra spice trade, violence was "a necessary weapon"; a crusading impulse made it worse. It worked at first: European pepper prices stabilised, profits ran at perhaps several hundred per cent, and Lisbon became an entrepôt (wholesale trade went on to Antwerp). Dutch resentment of dependence on Portugal motivated the VOC (1602).
  • Debate on failure: (1) Niels Steensgaard: the enterprise was "redistributive" and "pre-capitalist", living off others' profits (protection money) instead of making its own trade profitable; (2) others (Pearson, Subrahmanyam): external factors, the resistance of strong territorial states (Mughals, Ottomans, Japanese, Persians).

Bengal: Sebastião Gonzales

Portuguese private traders, mercenaries, artillerymen and shipwrights were in Bengal (Hooghly, Sandwip); the Mughals suspected them of river piracy (expelled from Hooghly). Sebastião Gonzales, a salt trader leading refugees from Arakan, with help from the king of Bakla and a Spanish fleet, seized Sandwip from the Mughals in 1607, cheated Bakla, and set up a customs house; he then betrayed the Arakan king. A Goa fleet failed (a Dutch fleet, an ebb tide), and Arakan took Sandwip in 1616. These Bay of Bengal misadventures sped the Portuguese decline. Lecture caution: the Portuguese changed the ocean but never fully controlled it; their monopoly was partial and unstable, with bases (Goa, Cochin, Hormuz, Malacca) that were footholds, not territorial control.

  • American silver was the key: Spanish conquest made silver cheaper in Europe than in India and China, and Indians had an "almost inexhaustible" demand for silver as a private asset. Money was dearer in Asia (high interest), so trade ran on bullion. New World tobacco and sugar fed the consumer boom; sugar profits built London's docks, banks and insurance.
  • The EIC had about 50% of the textile market by mid-century (French and Dutch shared the rest). A structural break at mid-century (politics); the French and Dutch companies ended, and the English one survived only by reinventing itself as a colonial power.

(e) Partners, employees, agents: Indian agency Must know

Dependence on Indian institutions Om Prakash: "the Europeans had no option but to operate within the given organizational structure": bankers, money-changers, merchant partners, artisans and shipwrights. Asian traders controlled almost everything except the final sale and shipment by sea: overland, inter-regional, river and caravan trade. Pedro Machado: Gujarati traders supplied textiles to Portuguese Mozambique and built an ivory trade. No question of European dominance over trading institutions; the goal was stable partnerships.

Bankers

Converted silver bullion into coin, lent money (high, seasonal, monthly-quoted interest), changed currencies (risky as the Mughal currency fragmented), and issued promissory notes (hundis) for remittance. After 1765 the Jagatseths lost importance, while Gopaldas of Benares and Arjunji Nathji of Surat prospered; bankers' networks even supplied military intelligence.

Coastal communities

Owned boats; loaded cargo at Bombay; pepper at Calicut; timber from Canara to the Trincomalee naval yard. Dhows (50–250 tons, lateen sails) carried coconut and copra; dhoni boats on the Madras–Ceylon route through the shallow Palk Strait. Some were pirates (Tavernier on the Mapillas, 1660s).

Brokers (chief contractors)

Took company advances and distributed contracts to an "army of under-contractors"; held an information advantage (language, supply sources); in the 17th c. often military or political men, in the 18th c. merchants. Relations combined dependence and distrust: fraud, threats and imprisonment.

"Contract": the words "contract" and "breach of contract" had no Indian counterpart for sales; Hindu and Islamic law dealt with debt, not sales, and were tied to religious identity rather than being the law of the land. Large advance-order purchasing was probably new with the companies. Written contracts specified quantity, price, dimensions, quality, advances and wages. Why bother without courts? They made bookkeeping easier and carried moral force (breaking one was "sinful").

(f) From merchants to landlords: the port towns Must know

  • Three English territories: Madras (c.1640), Bombay (c.1660) and Calcutta (c.1690), each acquired amid threatened or actual war (Madras: the Mughal invasion of the Deccan; Bombay: Shivaji's threats; Calcutta: the Mughal attack in Bengal in the 1680s).
  • In Surat, Hooghly and Masulipatnam the English were tenants sharing rights; in the three new towns they were landlords, with their own laws. Each had a fort (with the European population) plus Indian villages as suburbs; the two worlds transacted at arm's length until the 18th century.
  • A schism between London directors and local officers: the founders, Francis Day (Madras), George Oxenden and Gerald Aungier (Bombay) and Job Charnock (Calcutta), paid from private means and were disgraced or chastised. Mutinies posing as royalists: Edward Winter (Madras, 1665) and Richard Keigwin (Bombay, 1679). Result: governors got wide powers, including law-making.
  • Calcutta's fort was on the east bank (navigability; defence against the Dutch, French, Danes and Portuguese on the west bank). Bengali textile dealers migrated in (after Saptagram silted up); in the 1740s, Maratha raids made it a safe haven and its population doubled.

Why the port cities mattered (lecture)

New kinds of political-commercial spaces:
  • More autonomous from inland powers;
  • More ocean-facing from the outset;
  • A greater role for commercial law and contract enforcement (predictable rules attracted capital and merchants, often refugees from decaying states);
  • They became bases for expanding Company power.

Roy treats these cities as a major turning point in both Indian Ocean and Indian history. ("Port city sovereignty" is in the glossary.)

(g) State formation, 1707–1765, and the Company as a state

Transactions in knowledge (two-way)

European calico printers copied Indian techniques (Riello); British spinning drew on Indian cotton (Parthasarathi). Into India: navigation (the telescope and compass were unknown; heavy Indian ships used little iron), stimulating Narsapur Peta (a "syncretic" shipbuilding centre on the Coromandel), European-style anchors made from Indian iron, Dutch ironworks near Palakole, and princes casting cannons with European help.

From trade to empire (lecture's bridge point)

Trade did not mechanically produce empire, but it created the conditions that made empire possible: fortified settlements accumulated power; Company organisation blurred trade and sovereignty (merchant and quasi-state); Mughal decline opened space; commercial cities became staging grounds for conquest. Violence mattered, but Europeans depended on Indian brokers, financiers and shipwrights; empire grew through entanglement, not simple replacement. And Asian trade was not purely peaceful before 1500 either.
Discussion: if Europeans depended so heavily on Indian merchants and producers, what exactly was new about European power? Suggested answer: not superior commerce, since Asians controlled procurement, credit and most trade. What was new was organisational (joint-stock capital, monopoly charters, continuous corporate presence, credible diplomacy), armed (fortified ports, armed shipping, the cartaz system) and above all institutional-territorial: port cities with their own law and sovereignty that attracted Indian capital, and that the Mughal collapse let turn into bases for conquest. Roy's own framing is "a hybrid technological, institutional and political order".

Strengths of the "connected space" framework

  • Breaks national history boundaries
  • Decentres Europe
  • Shows mobility and interdependence

Limits

  • May flatten local differences
  • May privilege merchants over labourers and peasants
  • May understate coercion, slavery and inequality
  • May make connection seem smoother than it was

"Connected for whom? On whose terms?"

Conclusion: the Indian Ocean as a connected but uneven world
  • Unity did not mean sameness: macro coherence coexisted with deep regional difference.
  • Trade was central but not the whole story: ecology, religion, migration and empire also mattered.
  • European expansion transformed, but did not create, Indian Ocean connectivity: long before 1500, the system already worked.
  • It was a connected, contested and constantly re-made historical world.

S.3 Glossary of key terms Supporting

Anekantavada
Jain doctrine of the many-sidedness of reality; with syadvada (conditional assertion). Gandhi credited it for his pluralism.
Ainnurruvar / Manigramam / Nanadesi / Anjuvannam
Medieval south Indian merchant guilds active overseas (Takuapa, Barus 1088).
Cartaz
Portuguese pass that Asian ships had to buy from the Estado da India.
Circulation (vs diffusion)
Multi-directional movement and transformation of religion and culture, not one-way spread from a centre.
Connected histories
Histories that follow links across regions instead of treating nations or civilisations separately.
Cudamanivihara
Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam built by Srivijaya's king with Chola grants (Leyden plates).
Dharma vs religio
Dharma is cosmic and moral order and duty; it encompasses law and society, unlike the Western "religion".
Effective vs symbolic restoration
Hobsbawm: real return to the past (rarely possible) vs restoring symbols of it (Warsaw's rebuilt old town).
Factory
Company trading station (warehouse and residence), not an industrial plant.
Heuristic
The first step of historical method: finding and collecting sources.
Invention of tradition
New practices presented as ancient to legitimise the present (Hobsbawm).
Isomorphism / mapping
Chaudhuri: exact structural correspondence between two sets / projecting one structure onto another (ihram ≅ Brahman ritual dress).
Joint-stock company
Capital pooled from many shareholders with shared risk (EIC 1600, VOC 1602).
Long-distance vs contact transmission
Zürcher and Sen: Buddhism jumped directly from India to China with Southeast Asia only a stopover, vs step-by-step spread.
Longue durée
Braudel: slow-changing long-term structures (geography, climate, material life).
Monsoon
Seasonal reversing winds (from the south-west in summer, the north-east in winter) that made Indian Ocean sailing predictable.
Orientalism
(1) Scholarly study of the East (Jones, Asiatic Society); (2) Said: Western discourse that constructs the East as inferior "Other" to dominate it.
Plurality vs pluralism
The fact of many religions vs the value or practice of negotiating coexistence (Madan, Lecture 10).
Sabao
Chinese-appointed headman of a Sogdian or foreign community (An Jia, Shi Wirkak).
Saptaratna
"Seven jewels" offered to Buddhist monasteries, which created demand for pearls, coral and lapis (Xinru Liu).
Subaltern
Subordinate groups (peasants, workers, tribals, women); from Gramsci; Guha's Subaltern Studies.
Suvarnadvipa / Suvarnabhumi
"Island/Land of Gold": Indian names for Southeast Asia (Sumatra), in Jatakas.
Transit zone
Sen: early maritime Southeast Asia, where monks changed ships, before Buddhist institutions took root (5th c.).
Vishnupada
Sacred footprint of Vishnu; Purnavarman's inscription (c.450) makes the king's footprints Vishnu's.
World-system
Wallerstein: a unit with a single division of labour and multiple cultures; "a world-system, not a system of the world".
Yupa
Vedic sacrificial post; Kutei's seven stone yupas (late 4th c.) are the earliest Indic inscriptions in Southeast Asia.
Zomia
James Scott's term for the stateless uplands of mainland Southeast Asia, extended by van Schendel.

Q. Doctrine vs lived religion in India

Thesis: religion is more than belief (belief → practice → experience → social life). ¶ Doctrine: texts and orthodoxies: Veda and Manusmriti, the Quran and sharia (the ulama, Barani), the Khalsa rahit. ¶ Lived: food, pilgrimage, festivals; Sufi shrines shared by Hindus and Muslims; Mapilla customs; Marriott's little traditions; Madan's folk pluralism; temples as economic institutions. ¶ Why the gap matters: the colonial census (1871) and the Judeo-Christian model read religion through doctrine and hardened identities; lived religion shows porous boundaries (the Nagapattinam bronzes). ¶ Conclusion: study religion through society, not texts alone.

Q. Why "maritime Silk Road" is misleading (Sen)

Richthofen's 1877 term was extended to every route. ¶ Silk was not the main sea cargo (beads, pearls, then incense, pepper, porcelain). ¶ It over-emphasises China (Chinese shipping only from the 10th c.) and erases Southeast Asia, India and the Middle East (Austronesians, Funan/Oc Eo, Indo-Roman trade). ¶ It is also politically loaded today (BRI's 21st-century Maritime Silk Road; Bhattacharya: selective use of the past). ¶ Alternative: a multi-centred maritime Asia.

Q. Hansen's urban lens vs Bhattacharya's regional lens

Both reject one road and centres-only history. ¶ Hansen: Chang'an; archaeology (An Jia, Shi Wirkak, the Hejiacun hoard, pawn tickets); cosmopolitanism; few goods, many people; decline after An Lushan and the bans of 843/845. ¶ Bhattacharya: Bengal as a hinge of land, river and sea; cotton and cowries; colonial restructuring; BRI and Act East. ¶ Different scales reveal different Silk Roads; centres and peripheries are made.

Q. How useful are foreign travellers' accounts?

Travel writing = observation + memory + interpretation. ¶ Advantages: eyewitness, details Indians did not record, comparisons (Faxian's Gupta India; Xuanzang's Harsha and Nalanda). ¶ Limits: language, religious lens (Buddhist pilgrims), hearsay (al-Shahrastani never visited India and wrote from Arabic secondary sources), exaggeration (Marvels). ¶ Method: corroborate with inscriptions, coins and archaeology; al-Biruni as the critical counterpoint. ¶ Conclusion: indispensable but must be read critically.

Q. Chaudhuri's "unity in the large, disunity in the small"

Braudel's model applied to the Indian Ocean (622–1750). ¶ Möbius strip analogy. ¶ Unity: transcontinental trade, the monsoon, an invariant material ground floor, four expansions to Plassey. ¶ Disunity: four civilisations; bread vs rice; arid vs wet. ¶ Unity is a mental construct (Gödel; Arasaratnam's critique). ¶ Lecture: "connected, not uniform".

Q. Indian agency in Indo-European trade (Roy)

A crowded world before 1500 (Gujarati, Mapilla, Chulia and Chetti merchants). ¶ Europeans "had no option but to operate within the given structure" (Om Prakash): bankers (minting, hundis; the Jagatseths), brokers (Kasi Viranna, Amirchand, Ananda Ranga Pillai), coastal shippers, shipwrights (Narsapur Peta). ¶ Relations of dependence and distrust; "contract" was a new institution. ¶ Empire grew through entanglement. ¶ But also coercion of weavers after 1765.

S.6 Essay plans (15 marks, about 600–900 words) Must know

Essay 1: "Indian Ocean connectivity was transformed, not created, by Europeans." Discuss. Intro: define connectivity (structured interaction across distance); thesis: Europeans entered a working system and reorganised it institutionally and violently. ¶ 1. Pre-1500 system: monsoon and segmented trade, Baghdad to Canton, India as hub (Bernstein); Arab/Persian, Gujarati and Tamil merchants; Chaudhuri's unity in the large. ¶ 2. Religion and diaspora: Buddhism at sea (Sen), Tamil guilds and temples from Takuapa to Quanzhou (Guy), multi-religious Nagapattinam (Ray). ¶ 3. The Portuguese: violence, the cartaz, forts, but partial control and decline after 1640 (Steensgaard vs strong states). ¶ 4. Companies: joint stock, charters, forts; dependence on Indian bankers and brokers; textiles and silver. ¶ 5. Transformation: port cities with their own law → state formation 1707–65 → empire (Plassey, Buxar, 1765). ¶ Critique: "connected for whom?"; coercion of weavers; slavery. ¶ Conclusion: continuity of networks and rupture of sovereignty.
Essay 2: Trace the development of Indian historiography from Orientalism to Subaltern Studies (the professor's model essay; see the front matter) Intro: each school answered the colonial claim that "India has no history". ¶ Orientalists: Jones (1784), Prinsep (1834), the "sheet anchor": achievements, but a golden-age and Brahmanical bias (Said). ¶ Colonial: Mill (1818): Hindu–Muslim–British, despotism, the justification of rule; Elliot & Dowson. ¶ Nationalist: golden ages, Hindu Polity (Jayaswal), Rajput heroism: pride but communal tendencies. ¶ Marxist: Kosambi, Sharma (feudalism), Thapar, Habib: economic structures and class. ¶ Cambridge School: elite interest politics. ¶ Subaltern: Guha (1982), history from below; critique of elitism. ¶ Conclusion: historiography reflects the politics of its present (Hobsbawm).
Essay 3: "Religion travelled because people travelled." Discuss with reference to Indian religions in Asia Intro: from diffusion to circulation; Madan's plural and lived religion. ¶ Agents: merchants, Brahmans, monks, rulers, artisans. ¶ Buddhism: monk–merchant reciprocity (Liu, saptaratna), Faxian's voyage, the transit zone (Sen), Srivijaya and Sri Lanka, Atisa. ¶ Hinduism and kingship: Kutei yupas, Purnavarman's Vishnupada (local appropriation), Takuapa guilds, Quanzhou temples (Guy). ¶ Hybridity and reverse flows: Nagapattinam (Srivijayan and Chinese gifts; Schalk's three Buddhisms; Ray); Balaputradeva at Nalanda. ¶ Limits: antagonism (Faxian's "Brahmin" merchants), source bias. ¶ Conclusion: religion was carried, translated and reinterpreted, not copied.
Essay 4: "There is no single Silk Road." Discuss Richthofen's 1877 coinage vs many routes. ¶ Overland: Hansen's Chang'an, Sogdians, archaeology, change over time. ¶ Southern: Bhattacharya's Bengal (cotton, cowries), colonial rerouting. ¶ Maritime: Sen's critique; Bernstein's sea route. ¶ Modern uses: BRI, Act East, BCIM (Hobsbawm's invented past). ¶ Scale and centre/periphery. ¶ Conclusion: multiple, layered, dynamic networks; the lens decides what we see.

Five sentences to drop into almost any answer
  1. "The past is a permanent dimension of human consciousness" (Hobsbawm), and every present rewrites it.
  2. Every source is evidence and interpretation at once; corroboration is the historian's discipline.
  3. The scale we choose (city, region, ocean, globe) decides which history becomes visible.
  4. Asia was connected long before 1500; Europe transformed but did not create that connectivity.
  5. Religion travelled because people travelled, and it was translated, not simply transplanted.
