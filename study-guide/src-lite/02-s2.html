Historical Methods, Sources & Practices

Matthew Lange, Comparative-Historical Methods (2013), ch. 1 · Charlotte Backerra, "Source criticism for cultural history", Rethinking History (2024) + Lecture 2
In one lineHistorians work like detectives: they find sources, check whether they can be trusted (Backerra), interpret them, and explain why things happened, often by comparing cases (Lange).

1. What are historical methods? Must know

  • Historical methods are systematic ways of studying the past. History is analytical, not just descriptive: historians explain why things happened, not only what happened.
  • Steps: ask a question → find evidence → evaluate sources → compare societies → explain change → present an argument.
  • Source vs evidence: a source is any trace left from the past (letter, coin, tomb, photo). It becomes evidence only when a historian checks it and uses it to answer a specific question. Almost anything can become a source if it helps answer a question (Backerra).

The four stages of historical method (lecture table)

StagePurposeKey questionWhat can go wrong
1. HeuristicsCollect evidence"What survives, and where?"Missing evidence; mistaking a copy for the original
2. CriticismCheck if genuine and reliable"Is it real? Is the author believable?"Wrong author, forgery, trusting a biased witness

Types of sources

Primary = made at the time (diaries, letters, court records, newspapers, photos, inscriptions, coins, travel accounts). Secondary = later analysis (scholarly books and articles). Tertiary = summaries (encyclopaedias, textbooks).

Xuanzang's diary is primary; a historian's book on Xuanzang is secondary; an encyclopaedia entry is tertiary.

Practice: oral history and archives

Oral history (Oral History Association): preparation → interview → preserve → give access. The key ethic is informed consent. Archival research (American Historical Association): plan → research on site → cite properly. The key ethic is provenance (where a document came from).

2. Lange: comparative-historical analysis (CHA) Must know

Lange's argument CHA has produced some of the most famous social science (Adam Smith, Tocqueville, Marx, Weber, Barrington Moore, Tilly, Skocpol). From 1986 to 2010 CHA books won a quarter of the American Sociological Association's best-book awards, yet there was no textbook explaining the method. Lange fills that gap. CHA is special because it combines different methods and balances the particular and the general. Its big topic is social change: state building, nationalism, capitalism, industrialisation, revolutions, democratisation, empire, globalisation.

Within-case methods (the "historical")

Look deep inside one case over time:
  • Causal narrative: a detective-style account of causes
  • Process tracing: following the chain that links A to B
  • Pattern matching: testing theories against one case's evidence
How exactly did the French monarchy collapse?
+

Comparative methods (the "comparative")

Compare several cases to find similarities, differences and causes. Types: narrative, Millian, Boolean, statistical. CHA mostly uses small-N (a few cases).Compare the French, Russian and Chinese revolutions.

The 4 defining elements of CHA

  1. Within-case methods
  2. Comparative methods
  3. Social-scientific outlook: believes real causes can be found (postmodern views that deny this are "anti-methodological")
  4. Big units: states, classes, economies, religions. Tilly: "big structures, large processes, huge comparisons"

What is NOT CHA (and why)

  • Said's Orientalism: compares and narrates, but is postmodern (denies social-science insight)
  • Acemoglu et al. on colonial legacies: only statistics, no within-case study
  • Tilly's Contentious French: deep study of France but no comparison

They are excellent works; each just lacks one element.

Key vocabulary (in simple words)

MethodologyThe whole set of rules for gaining knowledge. Michael Mann: really one methodology in 8 steps (problem → variables → hypotheses → sample → measure → data → test → conclude).
MethodA technique for analysing data.
NomotheticExplanation by general laws for many cases (from Greek nomos, law).
IdeographicExplanation of one unique case. Tilly: this is not "a bungled attempt at generalization".
PositivismThe belief that the scientific method is the best route to knowledge (extreme form: social laws exist).
VerstehenGerman, "understanding" (Weber): aim to understand meaning, not to find laws.
EpistemologyThe study of what we can know and how.

Two famous examples

Reinhard Bendix, Kings or People (1978): compared how 6 countries (England, France, Germany, Japan, Russia, China) moved from kings to popular rule; each path was unique (ideographic).

Theda Skocpol, States and Social Revolutions (1979): revolutions happen when the state breaks down AND peasants can rebel, but only for non-colonial agrarian states in a certain period (a balance of general and particular).

Why combining methods matters

Comparison pushes you towards general explanations; deep study of one case pushes you towards the particular. Using both stops you from over-generalising ("all revolutions are caused by X") and from treating every case as totally unique. That is why CHA sits in the middle, and is criticised from both sides.

3. Backerra: source criticism for cultural history Must know

Backerra's argument Source criticism has been the historian's basic method since the 1800s and has barely changed in 150 years. Today's cultural historians ask new questions and use new materials, so they reach different interpretations, often without reflecting on how they are changing the method. The basic analysis of a source stays the same; what changes is the heuristic (the questions and sources) and the interpretation. The last step, presentation, remains "largely unreflected" (not thought about enough).

Droysen's 4 kinds of interpretation

  1. Pragmatic: cause and effect
  2. Conditions: place, time, resources
  3. Psychological: actions come from character
  4. Ideas: events express the ideas of an age

4 forms of presentation

  1. Investigative: follows the question, shows choices
  2. Narrative: tells a story
  3. Didactic: "learn from history"
  4. Discursive: argues with other historians (most common today)

Backerra's case study: early modern diplomatic letters (examples to use)

Old political historians used diplomats' letters only to find out "who said what to whom, when". Cultural historians also ask about rank, ceremony, symbols, space, language and emotions, i.e. "what did it mean?"

Layout and labels

Wider blank margins = higher-ranking reader. Letters were folded and sealed in special ways. "Secret" letters held key information (sometimes in code); "private" meant only the king and ministers could read it, not "personal".

Ceremony and language

Ceremony filled half of a diplomat's instructions (who visits whom first showed rank). The emperor wrote to George II in Italian, the king replied in French, and in German when writing as a vassal. Latin titles were changed to downgrade "majesty".

Networks, space, weather, emotion

Newspapers show the envoy Kinsky fencing and hosting balls in London (social networks missing from official letters). British envoys lived in a house outside Vienna's walls, which let them hold Protestant worship. An envoy writing "I never knew what Cold was" likely meant the frozen negotiations. Displays of emotion are read as performances.

Cultural history widens the sources

Not only government archives but rituals, symbols, emotions, objects, newspapers, personal letters and everyday life. Peter Burke: cultural history is a way of looking, not a separate field. Backerra asks historians to openly say "I" or "we" instead of pretending to be neutral.

Q. "Lange shows how historians explain; Backerra shows how they know." Discuss.

Historical method is both a science of evidence and an art of interpretation, and the two readings cover each side.

Lange explains how historians build causal explanations through comparative-historical analysis. CHA has four elements: within-case methods (causal narrative, process tracing), comparison across cases, a social-scientific belief that causes can be found, and big units like states and classes. Skocpol, for example, found that revolutions need both state breakdown and peasant power. CHA balances unique (ideographic) and general (nomothetic) explanation. This is why works such as Said's Orientalism do not count as CHA.

Backerra explains how historians decide whether evidence can be trusted, through source criticism (Droysen's heuristic, analysis, interpretation, presentation). Cultural historians use the same method with new questions: in diplomatic letters, wide margins showed rank, the choice of language showed status, and ceremony filled half the instructions.

But comparison can over-generalise, and presentation is still neglected. Together they make the full workflow: find → check → compare → explain.
