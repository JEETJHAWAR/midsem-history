India through the Eyes of Foreign Travellers

Tansen Sen, "The Travel Records of Chinese Pilgrims Faxian, Xuanzang and Yijing" (2006) · M.S. Khan, "A Twelfth Century Arab Account of Indian Religions and Sects" (on al-Shahrastani, 1983) + Lecture 6
In one lineForeign travellers fill gaps where Indian records are missing, but every travel account is evidence and interpretation at once, so historians must question it and check it against other sources. Together they show India was connected, not isolated.

1. Why study foreign travellers? Must know

  • Sources for India's past: archaeology (remains), inscriptions (official records in stone or metal), coins (rulers, dates, trade), literary texts, and foreign travellers (outsiders' accounts).
  • Foreigners noticed everyday details (food, dress, city life) that locals took for granted and never wrote down.
  • Travel writing = a description of a society by someone from outside, mixing first-hand experience with their own outlook: geography, economy, cities, religion, customs, trade, politics, food.

2. The Chinese pilgrims (Tansen Sen) Must know

Sen's argument Buddhism's spread from India to China (from the 1st century CE) started huge cross-cultural exchange: monks, traders, artisans and doctors moved across Central and Southeast Asia. The pilgrims brought texts, ritual objects and detailed records. Their writings made China see India as a sacred, even ideal land of "civilised" people. That was rare praise, because Chinese writers usually called foreigners barbarians. Their records are valuable for: (1) Buddhist doctrine, rituals and monasteries; (2) social and political conditions in India; (3) how cultures saw each other; (4) the hardships of travel and the monk–merchant relationship.

Why did they come? To collect scriptures, visit places linked to the Buddha's life, learn Sanskrit and study with Indian teachers, then bring texts home for translation.

Faxian (left Chang'an in 399, aged over 60)

  • Came for Vinaya texts (rules for running monasteries), which were scarce in China.
  • Route: across the Taklamakan desert → Central Asia → Indian holy sites → Sri Lanka → a dangerous sea voyage home via Southeast Asia (sailed from Tamralipti, stayed 2 years in Sri Lanka).
  • Recorded Buddhist legends and relic worship (the Buddha's bowl at Peshawar, the tooth relic in Sri Lanka), which spread such ceremonies to China.
  • Monks and merchants helped each other: monks travelled with caravans and ships; traders profited from demand for ritual goods; monasteries gave traders lodging.
  • One of the earliest outside accounts of Gupta India. His book: A Record of the Buddhist Kingdoms.

Xuanzang (c.600–664), "the greatest Chinese traveller"

  • Unhappy with poor Chinese translations ("when words are wrong, the meaning is lost"), he wanted original texts and Indian teachers.
  • Brought back 657 texts; Emperor Taizong built the Big Wild Goose Pagoda to house them.
  • Met King Harsha at Kanauj (637/638), which led to diplomatic relations between Harsha and Tang China. So he was a pilgrim and a diplomat.
  • Studied at Nalanda; gave India the Chinese name "Yindu"; surveyed caste, education, cities, food and law.
  • His notes help archaeologists today (Bamiyan, Nalanda); R.S. Sharma used his reports of decaying towns.

Yijing (635–713)

  • Travelled mainly by sea (671–695) and studied at Srivijaya (Sumatra) on the way.
  • Wrote about 40 daily practices in Indian monasteries to correct Chinese "errors", and biographies of 56 Chinese monks who went to India.
  • Allowed sensible compromise. Indians don't use chopsticks, but the rules don't forbid them, so Chinese monks may keep them.
  • Showed India within a wider Asian Buddhist world (links to Southeast Asia).

The "borderland complex"

Chinese monks felt China was on the edge ("borderland") of the Buddhist world, far from the Buddha's land. Faxian's companion stayed in India, wishing "not to be reborn in the borderland". At Nalanda, monks asked Xuanzang why he would go back to a land where "Buddhas are never born"; he replied that China was civilised and the Dharma must spread.

The pilgrims tried to recreate an Indian Buddhist world inside China so others need not travel. Yijing: "a second Rajagriha in China".

What the pilgrims teach historians (lecture): Buddhist networks between monasteries, universities (Nalanda), city life, trade routes shared by monks and merchants, diplomacy between courts, and cultural interaction. "Religion became a vehicle connecting Asia."

3. Arab scholars and al-Shahrastani (M.S. Khan) Must know

Why were Arabs interested in India? Trade; the spread of Islam into South Asia; scientific curiosity (Indian maths and astronomy fed the translation movement in Baghdad); and comparative religion, "one of the great contributions of Muslim civilisation".

What he wrote: he divided Indians into groups (Brahmins, worshippers of spiritual beings, of stars, idol-worshippers, philosophers), e.g. the great Mahakala idol at Ujjain, and explained why Brahmins "reject prophets".

Strengths (Khan)

  • Preserves medieval knowledge that might be lost
  • Systematic classification of Indian religions
  • Comparative: places Indian beliefs beside others
  • Fair: reports faithfully, does not insult Indian religions
  • Unique details on Buddhism
  • Understood that Hindus believe God himself comes down (avatar), unlike a prophet who brings a book
but

Weaknesses (Khan)

  • Never visited India; knew no Sanskrit or Pali
  • Copied older Arabic books (secondary sources); didn't even use al-Biruni
  • Errors: "Brahma" as a man's name; Buddhism as a branch of Hinduism; the Buddha dated "5,000 years" before Islam
  • Omits the Vedas and Jainism
  • Judged through Islamic ideas: didn't see that idols can be symbols

Khan's fair conclusion: it is "futile to expect scientific accuracy from an author of the twelfth century"; judge him by what Arabic information was available then.

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. How useful are foreign travellers' accounts as sources for Indian history?

Where Indian records are scarce, foreign accounts are very useful, but travel writing mixes observation, memory, interpretation and opinion, so it is evidence and interpretation at once. Historians must ask why the traveller came, for whom they wrote, what languages they knew, and what they left out.

The Chinese pilgrims were eyewitnesses. Faxian (399–413) described Gupta India, relic worship and his sea voyage home. Xuanzang described Harsha's Kanauj and Nalanda, brought back 657 texts and opened diplomatic ties. Yijing recorded monastery rules and studied at Srivijaya. They also show monks travelling with merchants. But they saw India through a Buddhist lens, and their "borderland complex" made them idealise it.

Al-Shahrastani classified Indian religions systematically and fairly, but he never visited India, knew no Sanskrit, relied on older Arabic books and made errors (e.g. "Brahma" as a man). Al-Biruni, who learned Sanskrit and used original texts, is more reliable, though al-Shahrastani still shows what Muslims of his time believed.

So these accounts must be corroborated with inscriptions, coins and archaeology. Used critically, they also show that India was connected to Asia, not isolated.
