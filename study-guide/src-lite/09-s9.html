SESSION9

Silk Route Connections II: Trade, Religion and Buddhism Across the Sea

Tansen Sen, "Buddhism and the Maritime Crossings" (2014) + Lecture 9
In one lineBuddhism reached China by sea as well as by land, carried by monks on merchant ships. Trade and religion supported each other, Southeast Asia was first only a stop-over, and later Srivijaya and Sri Lanka became major Buddhist hubs.

1. Sen's main argument: 6 claims Must know

  1. Critique of the "Silk Road" focus: scholars studied only the overland routes; one book on Buddhism's spread had not a single chapter on the sea. The term "maritime Silk Road" is misleading.
  2. Buddhism and sea trade were tied together, just as on land.
  3. "Long-distance", not "contact" transmission (Erik Zürcher's idea): in the first centuries CE, maritime Southeast Asia was only a "transit zone" where monks changed ships.
  4. India–China sea networks were cosmopolitan: Indian, Sogdian and Central Asian merchants and monks, Sri Lankan nuns, Southeast Asian kings.
  5. Sri Lanka was not only the home of Theravada; it was also a centre of relics and Tantric Buddhism.
  6. Sea networks lasted longer than land networks (into the 19th–20th centuries).

2. Why "Maritime Silk Road" is a misleading name Must know

Sen's 3 objections

  1. Silk was not the main cargo at sea. Early trade was in beads, precious stones, pearls; later in bulk goods like incense, pepper, spices, porcelain.
  2. It over-stresses China. Ports and ships from Southeast Asia, India and the Middle East were just as important, and the Chinese became active in Indian Ocean shipping only from the 10th century.
  3. It ignores Southeast Asia, whose rise was crucial for the sea route.

Alternative: see maritime Asia as a multi-centred world, not a China-centred extension of the Silk Road.

The sea was busy long before Buddhism

  • 2500–1500 BCE: Austronesian migrations linked south China, Southeast Asia and Indonesia
  • 1st millennium BCE: metal and wet-rice farming spread; Southeast Asian sailors were as important as Indian ones (they exported tin and gold)
  • 1st c. CE: Funan (Mekong delta), with its port Oc Eo, trans-shipped goods between India and China
  • Indo-Roman trade: Roman coins in Tamil Nadu, in exchange for pepper and textiles
  • Han China's ports (Jiaozhi, Hepu, Guangzhou) received ivory, pearls and incense; with these traders came Buddhist preachers and texts

3. Land or sea first? Sen's way out of an old debate

The old debate

Sea first: Liang Qichao, Paul Pelliot.
Land first (traditional view): Tang Yongtong, Rong Xinjiang.
→

Sen: the debate is "irrelevant"

There were no monasteries on either route before the 4th century, by which time Buddhism was already in China. Evidence shows both: Buddhist carvings at Mount Kongwang (coast) point to the sea; Buddha figures in Sichuan tombs point to land. Multiple routes, not one.
"Long-distance" vs "contact" transmission (Zürcher) Contact transmission works like a relay race: each region adopts Buddhism, builds monasteries, then passes it on. Long-distance transmission works like a long-haul flight: monks and texts go straight from India to China, and the places in between are only stop-overs. Zürcher showed that Central Asian towns became Buddhist centres only from the mid-3rd century; Sen applies the same idea to the sea.

Better question (Sen): study how land and sea were complementary, connected and sometimes competing. Many monks used both. Mahayana grew on the land routes; Theravada later on the sea routes. The lecture calls this balanced view (sea did not "replace" land) one of the article's greatest strengths.

4. Monks and merchants: partners, but not always friends Must know

Lecture: religion did not just travel alongside trade; it became part of trade itself. Buddhism spread because trade networks already existed.

Partnership (the usual view)

James Heitzman: in India, monasteries stood near trade towns and routes, in mutual support with merchants. Xinru Liu applied this to India–China.

The Buddhist "seven jewels" (saptaratna) offered to monasteries created demand for pearls, coral and lapis lazuli from India.

Antagonism (Sen's addition)

Merchants had their own gods and loyalties; some were Brahmanical or later Muslim, and they might compete over the trade in relics and texts.

In a storm in 411, "Brahmin" merchants on Faxian's ship blamed him for bad luck and wanted to leave him on an island. His patron threatened to report them to the Chinese ruler, and they backed down.

5. The India–China sea network (3rd–6th c.) Must know

Mount Kongwang (Chinese coast): Buddhist carvings mixed with Daoist ones, and donors in Central Asian dress. Why Central Asians at sea? The Kushan empire joined land and sea trade networks; trade, diplomacy and religion were intertwined.

The shipping route shifted (5th c.)

Earlier, Funan's port Oc Eo was the key stop. From the 5th century Java, Sumatra and Kedah (Malay peninsula) became the main centres, so later monks passed through the Indonesian islands.

Theravada networks with Sri Lanka

Evidence for the transit zone (lecture): no monasteries before the 4th–5th centuries; Buddhist inscriptions appear late; Buddhist institutions grow alongside sea trade. Sea traffic between India and China even helped establish Buddhism in Southeast Asia.

Srivijaya (Palembang, Sumatra)

  • Controlled the sea route between India and China
  • Used Buddhism in diplomacy: gifts to monasteries at Nalanda, Nagapattinam and Thailand (S10)
  • Yijing: over 1,000 monks there, following the same rules as India; Chinese monks should study there 1–2 years before going to India. His book lists 60 Chinese monks, many travelling by sea.
  • Lecture: India no longer had a monopoly on Buddhist knowledge; it was spread across maritime Asia.

Tantric Buddhism in Srivijaya

  • Inscriptions (e.g. 684) show Tantric ideas
  • Tantric masters Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra passed through to China (8th c.)
  • Atisa (982–1054), an Indian prince-monk, went to Srivijaya in 1012 to study under Dharmakirti for about 12–13 years, then taught at Vikramashila and went to Tibet (1040s). Knowledge flowed back to India.

7. Sri Lanka reinterpreted Must know

Lecture: "perhaps the most significant contribution of this article" Most histories call Sri Lanka simply the centre of Theravada. Sen shows it was also a transit point (from the 4th c.), a store of relics, a staging point for pilgrims, a centre of Mahayana and Tantric Buddhism, and a diplomatic actor. Faxian, Vajrabodhi, Amoghavajra and Atisa all passed through. Its monasteries were crossroads, not isolated.

Relics = power

Faxian saw the Bodhi tree sapling and the tooth relic festival. Kings believed relics had magical and political power:

  • Candrabhanu, a Malay king, attacked Sri Lanka (1247, 1262) to get relics
  • Kublai Khan sent an envoy (1284), perhaps for the alms bowl
  • Zheng He captured a Sri Lankan ruler (1410), probably wanting the tooth relic

Tantra and the Abhayagiri monastery

Vajrabodhi worshipped the tooth relic for 6 months at Abhayagiri before sailing to China (719–20). Amoghavajra went there in 741 to collect Tantric texts and returned with a royal mission (746). Around 800 the head of Vikramashila (India's top Tantric monastery) was a Sri Lankan monk, Jayabhadra.

Ratu Boko, Java (792): copied, but adapted A Javanese inscription names "the Abhayagiri Vihara of the Sinhalese", a second Abhayagiri in Java (de Casparis). Its platforms resemble Sri Lanka's (Miksic) but face a different direction (north–south, not east–west), showing local adaptation, not simple copying (Degroot).

Later Theravada networks (11th–15th c.): when the Cholas invaded Sri Lanka, monks fled to Myanmar; after King Vijayabahu I freed the island, Sri Lankan and Burmese monks restored Buddhism together. Close ties followed with Myanmar, Thailand and Cambodia. Modern echo: Anagarika Dharmapala (1864–1933) travelled by sea to spread Buddhism worldwide.

Early (1st–5th c.)India → China; Southeast Asia is a transit zone

13th c.Land routes become dominated by Islamic exchange, but sea Buddhism continues

9. Evaluating Sen (lecture) Must know

Strengths

  1. Connected history, not separate national histories
  2. The sea as an active space, not empty water
  3. Networks matter more than political borders
  4. Trade and religion reinforce each other
  5. Uses many kinds of evidence (archaeology, inscriptions, travel accounts, chronicles)
  6. Balanced: land and sea complement each other
  7. Shows people, objects, texts and ideas travelling together
but

Limitations

  1. Relies heavily on Chinese texts (Faxian, Yijing)
  2. Little local agency: Southeast Asians appear only as middle-men, while recent research stresses local adaptation (e.g. Ratu Boko)
  3. Treats merchants as one group (Tamil guilds, Arabs, Malays and Gujaratis differed)
  4. Gender almost absent (only the Sri Lankan nuns)

10. Old routes, new politics

  • China's OBOR → BRI: Xi Jinping's main foreign policy; capital flows into Central Asia and serves Chinese steel and coal industries. India has not joined.
  • The "Southern Silk Road" (Yunnan–Myanmar–India) is back in political talk, though local trade there "never ceased".
  • Three takeaways (from S8): it was a network, not one road; colonialism restructured mobility; modern states use the past selectively ("the past is mobilised, not neutrally remembered").
  • Groupings: BIMSTEC, the Asian Trilateral Highway, BCIM, BBIN.

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. How did trade and religion support each other in the maritime spread of Buddhism? (Sen)

Tansen Sen argues that Buddhism reached China by sea as well as by land, and that this spread was closely tied to trade. He rejects the label "maritime Silk Road", since silk was not the main cargo and the label wrongly makes China the centre.

Merchants gave monks passage, funded monasteries and carried relics and texts; monasteries gave merchants lodging, trust and blessings. Offerings of the "seven jewels" created demand for pearls and coral (Xinru Liu). The Indian ship-owner Nandin, who carried Gunavarman and Sri Lankan nuns to China, shows this link. But the relationship was not always friendly: "Brahmin" merchants on Faxian's ship wanted to abandon him in a storm.

Following Zürcher, Sen shows that Southeast Asia was first only a transit zone; the first Buddhist inscription there was set up by a sea captain, Buddhagupta. Later Srivijaya (1,000 monks, according to Yijing) and Sri Lanka (relics and Tantric teachers like Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra) became hubs, and knowledge flowed back to India with Atisa.

Limitation: he relies on Chinese sources and gives little voice to local people or women. Still, he shows that religion became part of trade itself.
