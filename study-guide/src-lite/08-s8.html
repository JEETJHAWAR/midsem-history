Silk Route Connections I: Two Lenses on the Silk Road

Valerie Hansen, The Silk Road, ch. 5 (Chang'an) · Jayati Bhattacharya, "Old Routes, New Dreams" (2021, Bengal–China) + Lecture 8
In one lineThere was no single "Silk Road" but many routes. Hansen looks through a city (Chang'an); Bhattacharya looks through a region (Bengal). The lens you pick decides which Silk Road you see.
Start here: what is the "Silk Road"? The name was invented in 1877 by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen. He drew it as one line from China to Europe. In reality it was a web of many routes, few people travelled the whole way, and goods passed from hand to hand. Much more than silk moved: people, religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism), ideas and many goods.

Hansen: the CITY lens

Chang'an (modern Xi'an), capital of the Tang dynasty, was a cosmopolitan city of about 1 million people, home to foreigners, monks, merchants and refugees.

Main point: few goods travelled the whole way; people and ideas mattered more.

Evidence: archaeology (tombs, hoards, documents).

Bhattacharya: the REGION lens

Bengal was not a "periphery" (edge) but a hub linking rivers, mountains and sea to China and Southeast Asia (the "Southern Silk Road").

Main point: centres and peripheries are made by politics, not by nature.

Evidence: trade goods like cotton and cowrie shells.

Hansen: Chang'an (key examples)

Sogdians (Central Asian traders)

Merchants from Samarkand who settled in China. Their leaders, called sabao, were headmen of foreign communities.

The tomb of An Jia (d. 579) is Chinese in shape but has a Zoroastrian fire altar: mixing of cultures ("hybridity").

Hejiacun hoard (treasure found in 1970)

Over 1,000 items, but only a few imported ones; most were made in China by migrant craftsmen.

This proves Hansen's point: skills and people travelled more than goods. Fewer than 50 Roman coins have been found in all of China.

Rise and fall

Chang'an grew under the Tang, then suffered: the An Lushan rebellion (755), followed by attacks on Sogdians; foreign religions banned (843–845); rebels burned the city. Trade shifted to the sea.

Everyday life too

Pawnshop tickets show ordinary people (a quarter of them women) pawning clothes, not grand luxury trade.

Bhattacharya: Bengal (key examples)

Cotton, not just silk ("Cotton Road")

Bengal's fine cotton cloth (muslin) was sold to China for centuries.

So why do we say "Silk Road" and not "Cotton Road"? Because Bengal was later imagined as a "periphery".

Cowrie shells (money)

Small shells used as cheap money from the Maldives through Bengal to Yunnan in China. Where cowries spread shows how far the Southern Silk Road reached.

Faxian, Xuanzang and Ibn Battuta mention them; in Bengal a chicken cost about 448 cowries.

Colonial rule changed the routes

The British restructured trade (railways, tea, new borders). Then 1947 (Partition) and the 1962 India–China war closed old routes, and Bengal became a "border" zone.

Today: old routes, new politics

China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI, 2013) and India's Act East Policy (2014) both use the memory of old routes, but selectively, for their own goals.

This is Hobsbawm's "use of the past" (S1) in action.
Big lessons (use in any Silk Road answer)
  1. No single road: many routes, by land, river and sea.
  2. Scale matters: a city view and a regional view show different things (S3).
  3. Centres and peripheries are made: Bengal was called "peripheral" only from Delhi's or London's point of view.
  4. Connectivity changes over time: it grows, shrinks, reroutes and gets "revived" for politics.

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. "There is no single Silk Road." Discuss with reference to Hansen and Bhattacharya.

The term "Silk Road" was coined by Richthofen in 1877 as one line from China to Europe, but the readings show a web of routes carrying people, religions and many goods.

Hansen uses a city lens. Through archaeology in Chang'an (the Sogdian tomb of An Jia, the Hejiacun hoard) she shows a cosmopolitan city where foreigners mixed cultures, but where few goods travelled long distances. People, skills and ideas mattered more. The city declined after the An Lushan rebellion, and trade moved to the sea.

Bhattacharya uses a regional lens. Bengal was a hub linking rivers, mountains and sea, with trade in cotton and cowrie shells reaching Yunnan. It was later made "peripheral" by colonial borders, Partition and the 1962 war.

Today, China's BRI and India's Act East use the Silk Road memory selectively, a political use of the past. So the Silk Road was multiple and changing, and what we see depends on our lens.
