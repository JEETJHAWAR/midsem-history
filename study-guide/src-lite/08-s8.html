Silk Route Connections I: Overland Networks ("Comparing Two Lenses")

Valerie Hansen, The Silk Road: A New History, ch. 5 (Chang'an / Xi'an) · Jayati Bhattacharya, "Old Routes, New Dreams: the Southern Silk Road and Bengal–China Connectivities" (2021) + Lecture 8
In one line"There is no single Silk Road, only many histories of connection." Hansen looks through a city (Chang'an); Bhattacharya looks through a region (Bengal). The lens you choose decides which Silk Road you see.

1. What is the "Silk Road"? Must know

Popular view vs reality

Popular view: one overland road from China to the West, mainly for silk and luxuries.

Britannica (lecture): about 6,400 km from Xi'an, along the Great Wall, around the Taklamakan desert, over the Pamirs, through Afghanistan to the Mediterranean. Few people travelled the whole way; goods passed from middleman to middleman.

The name was coined in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, who drew it as one line on a map (he was planning a railway).

The readings show: many routes carrying people, religions, ideas, goods, states and ecologies.

How it worked (lecture)

  • Goods moved by trade but also as gifts and dowries. Han rulers kept peace by giving silk to the Xiongnu nomads.
  • Cosmopolitan centres: Chang'an, Samarkand, Dunhuang
  • Religions spread: Buddhism, Christianity, Manichaeism, Zoroastrianism
  • Art: the Dunhuang caves, Sogdian paintings in Samarkand

Shared argument: both reject the narrow "silk-for-the-West" story and "only capitals matter" history; both stress people on the move and the cultural and political meaning of routes. "Connectivity is not just movement; it is how spaces become meaningful through movement."

3. Hansen: Chang'an, the cosmopolitan end of the Silk Road Must know

Hansen's argument Chang'an was a truly international city, home to monks, merchants, craftsmen, officials and refugees. But few bulk goods came overland; trade volumes were smaller than popular memory suggests. What mattered most was the movement of people, skills and ideas. Her evidence is mainly archaeological.

The city

  • Capital of the Han, Sui and Tang (618–907) dynasties; about 1 million people
  • A planned grid of 109 walled wards with a night curfew
  • Two markets: Eastern (local goods) and Western (foreign goods, inns, Sogdian traders)
  • The Tang Code banned high officials from markets (trade was seen as "polluting"); officials set prices
  • Law for foreigners: same nationality → their own law; different nationalities → Chinese law

Foreigners and religions

  • Foreigners came through treaties (Turks, 631), as hostage princes, or as refugees (Persian princes after the Muslim conquest of Iran)
  • Zoroastrian temples near the Western Market
  • The Nestorian stele (781) records a Christian monk, Aluohan, arriving in 635
  • The Big Goose Pagoda (for Xuanzang's texts) survives

Archaeological evidence (her key examples)

Sogdian tombs: mixing of cultures

An Jia (d. 579), a sabao (headman of the foreign community): his tomb is Chinese in shape but has a Zoroastrian fire altar and a stone bed instead of a coffin. The carvings show diplomacy, not commerce.

Shi Wirkak (d. 579): a stone "house" with a bilingual Sogdian–Chinese epitaph and the soul crossing the Zoroastrian Chinwad bridge to paradise.

Lesson: migrants adapted Chinese practices and mixed them with their own ("hybridity").

Pawn tickets and the Hejiacun hoard

Pawnshop tickets (660s–680s, later reused to make a figurine): ordinary people pawned clothes at 5% a month, and about a quarter of the customers were women. This is everyday life, not grand trade.

Hejiacun hoard (found 1970): over 1,000 items, but only a few imports (gems, a glass bowl, a Persian coin); most were made in China by migrant Sogdian craftsmen.

Fewer than 50 Roman coins found in all of China (many used as charms); silk was often used as money (to pay soldiers). Objects correct the myth of endless luxury caravans.

Decline: change over time

  • An Lushan rebellion (755–763), led by a Sogdian-Turkish general. Afterwards the Tang blamed Sogdians; in one account people with "big noses" were killed, the first time a Chinese ruler targeted a minority.
  • Tibetans looted Chang'an (763); in 787 minister Li Mi cut support for 4,000 foreign envoys who had settled there.
  • Stereotype vs reality: tales show Sogdians as fabulously rich jewel merchants, but most were soldiers, farmers, envoys and craftsmen. A stereotype is not proof of a rich trade.
  • 843–845: foreign religions (Manichaeism, Buddhism, Zoroastrianism, Christianity) banned. Land routes declined, and travel shifted to the sea. Huang Chao's rebels killed foreigners at Guangzhou (878) and burned Chang'an; the Tang ended in 907.

4. Bhattacharya: Bengal and the Southern Silk Road Must know

Bhattacharya's argument New policies, India's Look/Act East and China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), have revived memories of old routes once called "frontier" or "periphery". She argues Bengal was central: a hub linking the delta, rivers, Himalayas, Tibet, Burma (Arakan) and Yunnan (China) with the sea. It was "peripheral" only from the viewpoint of rulers far from the sea (Delhi, Beijing). The "Silk Route" was a web of land, river and sea paths (a "Southern Silk Road" or even a "Cotton Road").

Useful concepts she uses

  • The periphery is constructed (Yang Bin, van Schendel)
  • Zomia (James Scott): the stateless uplands of Southeast Asia
  • Tirthankar Roy: Bengal as "parallel globalizations"
  • Braudel's longue durée (long term) vs short "episodic" political history
  • Wallerstein put South Asia in the "semi-periphery"; Washbrook, Bayly and Ludden criticised this as Eurocentric

Bengal's history in "episodes"

  • Buddhism flourished under the Palas (Tamralipti port, visited by Xuanzang)
  • The Senas revived Brahmanism
  • Ilyas Shahi sultans (from 1342) broke from Delhi: two centuries of autonomy
  • Zheng He's fleets reached Pandua (1415) and Gaur (1432)
  • Colonial era: Plassey 1757, Calcutta as "London of the East", partitions in 1905 and 1947

Four old trade corridors: (1) Tibet–Bhutan–Nepal–Bengal; (2) Yunnan–Arakan–Bengal; (3) Ganga river networks; (4) Assam–Bengal. Horses came through Himalayan passes; Tibet traded gold, salt, musk and wool. China is "Cina" in the Mahabharata, and the Arthashastra mentions Chinese silk; the Chinese envoy Zhang Qian noted India–China trade before the 2nd century BC. Main goods: silver, horses, cotton cloth, cowries.

Case 1: cotton, the "Cotton Road"

Chinese writers praised Bengal's fine cotton (muslin), and China kept buying Indian cotton even after growing its own (Stephen Dale). Chinese names for the route include the "Road of the Shu Cloth".

Why "Silk Road" and not "Cotton Road"? Richthofen's name stuck, and Bengal was imagined as a periphery.

Case 2: cowrie shells

Small shells used as cheap money from the Maldives through Bengal to Yunnan, mentioned by Faxian, Xuanzang, Yijing, Ibn Battuta and Tomé Pires. In Bengal a chicken cost about 448 cowries. Where cowries spread maps how far the Southern Silk Road reached.

The professor said you may skip: the detailed lists of route towns (Bhamo, Myitkyina and so on), the 15th-century Chinese sources on Bengal, the post-1947 transition paragraph, and the conclusion's details on Siliguri and Nathu La.

Colonial rule restructured the routes

  • Colonialism restructured mobility rather than simply expanding it: new institutions, railways, steamships, borders
  • Warren Hastings sent missions to Bhutan and Tibet (Bogle, 1774) to tap trade; the British annexed the Duars (1841–65)
  • Tea in Assam; the Permanent Settlement; Bengal's textile industry declined, and wealth flowed out to Britain
  • The Younghusband mission to Tibet (1904); the Ledo (Stilwell) Road to China (1942)
  • Moving the capital to Delhi in 1912 made Bengal "peripheral"

After 1947: walls go up

Partition (1947), the 1962 India–China war, Communist China and the Cold War built "huge walls". Regional groups such as ASEAN (1967) and SAARC (1985) split South and Southeast Asia. "The cowrie connections were soon chapters of a forgotten history."

21st century: old routes, new politics Must know

India

Look East Policy (1990s, PM Narasimha Rao) → Vajpayee's car rally (2004) → Act East Policy (PM Modi, 2014). Focused on the North-East; partly a hedge against China.
vs

China

Belt and Road Initiative (Xi Jinping, 2013): a land "belt" and a 21st-century "Maritime Silk Road". Big investment in Myanmar, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. India has not joined BRI.

Groupings in the Bay of Bengal: BIMSTEC (1997; the Kaladan project and the India–Myanmar–Thailand highway); BBIN (Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal); BCIM (Bangladesh–China–Myanmar–India; the Kolkata–Kunming car rally, 2013).

Her critical conclusion: the political use of the past States use history selectively (facts and legends) for diplomacy. BRI treats the "Silk Route", which "at no point in history was a single route", as one road led by China, pushing South Asian routes to the margins. Act East also treats the North-East as a periphery run from Delhi. There is a gap between policy and real history. This is Hobsbawm's symbolic restoration and invented past (S1).

Scale

City, region, empire or ocean: each gives a different Silk Road. Always ask "at what scale?"

Centre and periphery are made

Chang'an's greatness depended on "outsiders"; Bengal was later labelled peripheral by politics.

Connectivity has a history

It grows, shrinks, reroutes and gets reimagined. Are BRI and Act East reviving the past or inventing useful pasts?

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. "There is no single Silk Road." Discuss with reference to Hansen and Bhattacharya.

The term "Silk Road" was coined by Richthofen in 1877 as one line from China to Europe, but the readings show a web of routes carrying people, religions and many goods, with few people travelling the whole way.

Hansen uses a city lens. Through archaeology in Chang'an (An Jia's Chinese tomb with a Zoroastrian altar, the Hejiacun hoard, pawn tickets) she shows a cosmopolitan city where foreigners mixed cultures, but where few goods travelled far. Most items were made locally by migrant craftsmen, so people and skills mattered more. After the An Lushan rebellion (755) and the bans of 843–845, the city declined and trade moved to the sea.

Bhattacharya uses a regional lens. Bengal was a hub linking rivers, mountains and sea to Yunnan and Southeast Asia, shown by trade in cotton (a "Cotton Road") and cowrie shells. Colonial rule restructured these routes, and 1947 and 1962 closed them, making Bengal "peripheral".

Today, China's BRI (2013) and India's Act East (2014) use the Silk Road memory selectively, a political use of the past. So the Silk Road was multiple and changing, and what we see depends on the lens and scale we choose.
