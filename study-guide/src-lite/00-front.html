History Midsem: Exam Essentials

The short, simple version. Only what you can actually use in exam answers, explained in plain words with an example for every idea. Sessions 1–4 and 6–11 (Session 5 is not in the syllabus).

How to use this guide (read in 1 minute)

Each session has the same 5 parts

  1. In one line: the whole session in one sentence. If you remember nothing else, remember this.
  2. Picture it: a diagram or map.
  3. Key ideas: keyword → what it means in simple words → an e.g.
  4. Names & dates: only the ones worth writing in an answer.
  5. Write it like this: a ready model answer you can adapt.

The exam (Lecture 16)

  • Part A: 10 MCQs × ½ mark = 5
  • Part B: answer 3 of 5 short questions × 10 = 30
  • Part C: 1 essay out of 2–3 × 15 = 15
  • Total 50 marks, 2 hours, closed book.

The professor wants: paragraphs (not bullet points), the name of the scholar, at least one example (a place, person or date), and a short critical point ("but…").

The 4-step formula for every answer 1. Say what the scholar argues in one line ("Hobsbawm argues that…"). 2. Explain 2–3 points, each with an example. 3. Add one "but" (a limitation or criticism). 4. End with one line linking to the big course idea (e.g. "this shows that India was connected long before Europe arrived").

The whole course on one page

Read top to bottom: first how history is done, then who wrote India's history, then how India was linked to Asia.
Five ideas that appear again and again (use them in any essay)
  1. People use the past for present needs (Hobsbawm). Nationalists praised ancient India; China's Belt and Road uses the old "Silk Road" name.
  2. Every source has a point of view, so check it against other sources. al-Shahrastani never visited India, so trust al-Biruni more.
  3. The scale you choose changes the story. Vasco da Gama looks different from Indian, Indian Ocean and world viewpoints.
  4. Asia was connected long before Europeans came. Arab, Gujarati and Tamil merchants traded from Africa to China.
  5. Religion travelled with traders and changed on the way. Buddhist monks sailed on merchant ships to China.
