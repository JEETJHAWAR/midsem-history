Religions in India: Encounters, Adaptations and Transregional Influences

T.N. Madan (ed.), India's Religions, Introduction · John Guy, "Tamil Merchants and the Hindu-Buddhist Diaspora in Early Southeast Asia" · H.P. Ray, "A 'Chinese' Pagoda at Nagapattinam" + Lecture 10. The professor's model short answer comes from here.
In one lineReligion in India is plural, diverse inside, lived and mixed into society (Madan). When people travelled across the sea, religion travelled with them and changed on the way (Guy, Ray).

Part 1: How to think about religion in India (lecture + Madan) Must know

Religions born in India

Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism
+

Religions that came from outside

Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Zoroastrianism (Parsis), Bahá'í

The study of religion has shifted from looking only at doctrines to seeing religion as a social and historical thing.

Plurality ≠ Pluralism

Plurality = a fact: many religions exist. Pluralism = a value or practice: communities negotiate living together, through accommodation, interaction, borrowing, competition and even conflict.

Key question: how do people live with religious difference?

A town with a temple, a mosque and a church has plurality; neighbours sharing festivals shows pluralism.

Every religion is diverse inside

No religion is one single thing. Each has schools, sects, rituals, regional versions and rival authorities. Boundaries exist but are not fixed or uniform.

Hinduism: Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta, bhakti. Islam: Sunni, Shia, Sufi. Sikhism: Khalsa and Sahajdhari.

Religion as lived experience

Religion is more than belief: belief → practice → experience → social life (food, worship, pilgrimage, death rites, festivals, family customs). Keep apart religion as doctrine (texts) and religion as lived practice.

Texts draw sharp lines, but Hindus and Muslims both visit Sufi shrines.

Religion and society are intertwined

Religion is tied to caste, community, family, gender, economy and politics.

A temple is also an economic institution, a centre of redistribution, a seat of power, an employer and a marker of identity.

Tradition is not static

Tradition → adaptation → innovation. Traditions reinterpret old practices, create new movements and meet other traditions.

Bhakti, the Kabir Panth, the Ramakrishna Mission, Ambedkar's turn to Buddhism.

The colonial lens hardened identities

Missionaries studied Indian religions through a Judeo-Christian model (one God, one book, clear membership), which did not fit India (many ideas of God, regional variety, overlapping identities). From 1871 the British census put everyone into fixed boxes.

Fluid identities became "Hindu or Muslim or Sikh". Only in the 20th century did scholars adopt a pluralist view.

Part 2: Madan's main points Must know

Madan's argument
  1. India has religious plurality at two levels: between religions and within each religion (sects and movements).
  2. Calling Indian religions "tolerant and plural" and foreign ones "uniform and rigid" is wholly misleading: any religion with millions of followers across regions becomes internally plural (Islam and Christianity in India too).
  3. Dharma ≠ "religion": dharma means duty, moral and cosmic order, a whole way of life (law, family, society). In India religion traditionally includes the secular.
  4. Plurality is a fact; whether it produced pluralism is a separate question. Traditional "pluralism" was often a ranking.

Census 1991: Hindus about 82%, Muslims about 12%, Christians 2.3%, Sikhs 2%, Buddhists 0.8%, Jains 0.4%. Minorities are regionally concentrated (Muslims in J&K, Sikhs in Punjab, Christians in the North-East hill states and Kerala).

Quick guide to each religion (Madan)

Hinduism: a "federation of faiths"

  • No single founder, book or church
  • Vedas (from c.1500 BCE): shruti ("heard", revealed) vs smriti ("remembered": law books, epics, Puranas)
  • Manusmriti: varna-ashrama-dharma, duties by caste and stage of life
  • Epics and Puranas: gods like Vishnu (avatars, e.g. Rama, Krishna), Shiva and the Goddess
  • Bhakti (loving devotion) began in the Tamil south and spread north
  • Great teachers: Shankara (non-dualism), Ramanuja, Madhva
  • Many sects: Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta (Goddess); Tantra

Buddhism and Jainism

  • Buddhism: four noble truths, the eightfold path; the sangha (monks); split into Theravada, Mahayana (Nagarjuna) and Vajrayana (Tantric); declined in India; revived by Ambedkar's mass conversion of Dalits (1956)
  • Jainism: Mahavira; strict ahimsa (non-violence), so many Jains became merchants; Shvetambara and Digambara sects; anekantavada (truth has many sides)

Sikhism

  • Guru Nanak (1469–1539), from the sant tradition: devotion to God's name; rejected empty ritual and caste; a "third path" beyond Hinduism and Islam
  • Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa (1699), with initiation and unshorn hair, making a separate identity
  • Later: Ranjit Singh's kingdom; Singh Sabhas; the Bhindranwale period. Madan: the Khalsa form is exclusivist, not pluralist

Christianity

  • By tradition, St Thomas came to Kerala in the 1st century (the Syrian Christians)
  • The Portuguese in Goa: Francis Xavier (1542), the Inquisition; Jesuits at Akbar's court; de Nobili lived like a Brahmin sannyasi
  • Protestant missions (Ziegenbalg); the Church of South India (1947) and of North India (1970)

Islam

  • Came first through Arab traders on the Kerala coast (the Mapillas, who kept local customs), then conquest (Sind, 712)
  • Spread mostly peacefully, through Sufis and trade; the ulama (law scholars) vs the Sufis (mystics)
  • Sunni and Shia; Sufi orders (Chishti); Kashmir's Rishi order mixed with local traditions
  • Modern movements: Deoband (orthodox, 1867), Aligarh (modern education), the Barelwis, the Ahmadiyah

Lived mixing (examples to use)

  • Sufi shrines visited by all faiths
  • The Mapilla Muslims' local customs
  • Marriott: ideas flow both ways between the "great" Sanskrit tradition and "little" village traditions

Madan's key conclusion: two kinds of pluralism Folk pluralism = ordinary people naturally living with difference: "social harmony is built on the basis of difference". Intellectual or secular pluralism = an idea promoted by elites and the state, e.g. sarva dharma samabhava ("equal respect for all religions"), which Madan says "does not go very far". There is a gap between the two.

Part 3: Religion on the move Must know

The bridge (lecture): "If religion is plural, lived, adaptive and socially embedded… how does it change when people move?"

Old view: "diffusion"

India → Southeast Asia, one way. India gives, others copy.
→

New view: "circulation"

India ↔ Sri Lanka ↔ Southeast Asia ↔ China. "Religion travelled because people travelled; what travelled was transformed."

Who carried religion?

  • Merchants created the movement
  • Brahmins brought rituals and royal kingship
  • Buddhist monks brought monasteries and teaching
  • Kings gave patronage and used religion politically
  • Artisans made images

Merchant → settlement → community → temple. "Commercial mobility made religious mobility possible."

Encounter ≠ conversion

Traditions met and borrowed: shared words, shared holy places, new identities. They could coexist, compete, borrow and adapt.

Temples in Bali; the Ramakien (the Thai Ramayana); Mendut temple in Java.

The sea connected, not separated, South India, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and China.

John Guy: Tamil merchants and the Hindu-Buddhist world

Guy's argument Local Southeast Asian kings invited Brahmin priests and Buddhist monks for their own political benefit. But the medium was merchant ships, mostly Tamil. The inscriptions they left map the trade routes: coastal river sites linked to rich hinterlands.
EvidenceWhat it shows
Kutei, Borneo (late 4th c.)Seven Sanskrit yupa (sacrificial post) inscriptions of King Mulavarman: the earliest Indian-style inscriptions in Southeast Asia; Brahmins performed Vedic sacrifice at court
Purnavarman, West Java (c.450)Sanskrit inscription with Vishnu's footprints, which are also the king's: the first local king to identify himself with an Indian god. Indian ideas + local politics = something new
Klong Thom, Thailand (3rd–4th c.)A goldsmith's touchstone with the earliest Tamil inscription in Southeast Asia, owned by "Perumpatan" (great goldsmith). Southeast Asia was the "Land of Gold" (Suvarnabhumi)
Takuapa, Thailand (9th c.)Big Hindu statues and a Tamil inscription on a temple tank built by merchant guilds (the Manigramam)
Barus, Sumatra (1088)A Tamil inscription of the Ainnurruvar guild: a fund for members; those who failed to pay lost trading rights
Kedah (5th c.)The sea captain Buddhagupta's Buddhist stone prays for a safe voyage
Guangzhou (750)A Japanese monk saw three monasteries of Indian Brahmins and ships of Indians, Persians and Malays

Guy: Brahmanical ritual was linked to kingship, while Buddhism was more linked to commerce.

H.P. Ray: Nagapattinam, one port, many religions

Ray's argument Indian sea history is usually told as trade driven by Western demand, ignoring coastal communities (historians assumed caste stopped Indians from sailing). Ray studies maritime cultural landscapes: coasts as places where religions met. The Tamil coast had 127 Buddhist sites, which challenges Cunningham's idea that Buddhism died out in India around the 7th–8th century.

The Buddhist monastery (Cudamanivihara)

  • Built at Nagapattinam by the king of Srivijaya (Sumatra), with villages granted by the Chola kings Rajaraja I and Rajendra I (the Leyden copper plates, c.1005)
  • Burmese monks visited (1467)
  • The Dutch called its tower the "Chinese Pagoda"; it was demolished by Jesuits in 1867
  • 350 Buddha bronzes found (and more at Sellur in 2004)

Buddhism mixed with Hinduism

  • Some Buddha statues were carried in festival processions like Hindu gods, with titles similar to Shiva's and Vishnu's
  • One image was paid for by a guild official of "the merchants of the eighteen countries"
  • Schalk: one form of Buddhism there was so close to Shaivism it was hard to tell apart

A multi-religious coast

A Shiva temple at Nagapattinam got jewels from Srivijaya and gold coins from China; later a Sufi dargah (Nagore) and a church (Velankanni); inland, Jain inscriptions and an 8th-century mosque.

Not "first Buddhism, then Hinduism", but many religions side by side.

Merchant guilds and Tamil inscriptions

Guilds (Ainurruvar, Manigramam, Nanadesi) had private armies and funded temples. Eight Tamil inscriptions are known from Burma to Sumatra and China. Ray also doubts the famous Chola naval raid, which rests on one boastful inscription (source criticism, S2).

The direction also reversed

Religion did not only flow out of India: Srivijaya funded Nagapattinam and Nalanda (King Balaputradeva built a monastery at Nalanda; the Pala king Devapala granted villages); Chinese donors gave to Tamil temples. India was a node in a network, not just a source.

Atisa: knowledge on a merchant ship

Atisa (982–1054) went from Bengal to Sumatra on a merchant ship to study, then to Vikramashila and Tibet. The same ship carried goods and religious knowledge.

The lecture's overarching argument (memorise) Premodern religion was mobile, networked, adaptive, plural and reciprocal. Indian religions became important across Asia not because they stayed the same, but because they could be carried, translated, appropriated and reinterpreted in new places. Madan → religion is social and plural · Guy → it travelled through merchant networks · Ray → it created multi-religious landscapes.
Write it like this: the professor's short answer (10 marks)
Q. Distinguish religion as doctrine from religion as lived practice in India.

The lecture and Madan argue that religion is more than belief: it moves from belief → practice → experience → social life. So historians must separate doctrine from lived religion.

Doctrine means the official teachings in sacred texts and from religious authorities: the Vedas and Manusmriti, the Quran and sharia (upheld by the ulama), or the Sikh Khalsa code. Doctrine tends to draw clear boundaries between religions.

Lived religion is what people actually do: food rules, festivals, pilgrimage, local saints. In practice boundaries are porous. Hindus and Muslims visit the same Sufi shrines, the Mapilla Muslims of Kerala kept local customs, and Madan calls this everyday mixing "folk pluralism". Religion is also embedded in society: a temple is an economic and social institution.

The difference matters. The colonial census (from 1871), using a text-based Judeo-Christian model, put people into fixed boxes and hardened identities. Studying lived religion shows that Indian religions were plural and changing, as at Nagapattinam, where Buddha images were carried in processions like Hindu gods.
