History of the Indian Ocean as a Connected Space

K.N. Chaudhuri, "The Unity and Disunity of Indian Ocean History… 622–1750", Journal of World History (1993) · Tirthankar Roy, India in the World Economy, ch. 4, "The Indian Ocean Trade, 1500–1800" (2012) + Lecture 11
In one lineThe Indian Ocean was connected but not the same everywhere: "unity in the large, disunity in the small" (Chaudhuri). Europeans entered and changed this working system, depended on Indian partners, and slowly turned trade into empire (Roy).

1. What does "connected space" mean? Must know

"Connection does not mean sameness; it means structured interaction across distance." The Indian Ocean linked East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, Southeast Asia and China.

Five forms of connection

  1. Routes: shaped by wind (monsoon), geography and custom
  2. Shared trade practices: common norms of credit and exchange
  3. Migration: people settled and carried skills and languages
  4. Religious networks: belief and pilgrimage
  5. Political and military encounters: war and diplomacy are also connection

Why it matters in world history

  • One of the oldest arenas of exchange
  • Long-distance trade long before European empires
  • Goods, people, technology, languages and religion moved
  • Central to Islam, India, Southeast Asia, China and East Africa
  • Decentres Europe: Indian Ocean history is not a "prelude" to European expansion

Discussion question: was the ocean connected mainly by trade, or did trade depend on deeper links like climate, religion and migration? (Answer: both. The monsoon made trade possible, and religion and diasporas gave trust and credit.)

2. Chaudhuri: unity and disunity (622–1750) Must know

The question Fernand Braudel wrote the history of the Mediterranean (1949) as one sea with its societies, in terms of space, time and structures. Chaudhuri (author of Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean, 1985, and Asia Before Europe, 1990) asks: can we do the same for the Indian Ocean? Sceptics said no: it has no real unity, no clear time frame, and Asia is "too complex and diverse", so they avoided the question.
Chaudhuri: similar at the big level, different at the small level.

Unity in the large vs similarity in the small

From maths (Wittgenstein): a Möbius strip, a cylinder and a twisted cylinder look the same if you cut out a small square of each ("alike in the small"), but their overall shapes differ. So matching small details don't prove a shared overall form, and differing details don't disprove it.

Word by word, the Rig Veda, the Quran and Confucian texts differ. But Arab sailors could sail from Suez and Basra to China, so at the level of transcontinental trade the ocean had real unity.

Why so few Asia-to-Asia comparisons?

In the past, Asian writers were curious about their neighbours. In the 19th century, with Europe's power, Asian historians began comparing themselves with Europe (feudalism, capitalism). Their outlook turned from latitudinal (Asia ↔ Asia) to longitudinal (Asia ↔ Europe).

The time problem: Arasaratnam's critique

S. Arasaratnam said Chaudhuri proved geographic unity but not chronological unity for "750–1750". Chaudhuri replied that his period starts at 622 (the rise of Islam), not 750. The deeper point: unity of space and unity of time may not match. South Asian periods don't fit an ocean timeline; Islamic historians see a time unity but deny that "Asia" is a unit.

His theory (in short)

History is an interaction of six domains: real and perceived space, real and perceived time, real and perceived structures, plus a 7th, the "logical space". He mixes four strands: Braudel, structuralism, semiotics (study of signs) and set theory (maths). Critics called him a "latter-day Dr Faustus" drawing randomly on everything.

Chaudhuri's four conclusions

ConclusionIn simple words
1. InvarianceThe "ground floor" of daily life (food, clothes, houses, religious principles, technology) stayed basically the same while varying within limits. Stomach-lining cells are replaced every few days, but the stomach keeps the same function.
2. Four civilisationsThe ocean's unity is the sum of Islam, Sanskritic India, Southeast Asia and Confucian China. Set theory helps mark their boundaries (one person can belong to several sets, e.g. Muslim and Javanese).
3. TotalityPolitics, society, economy and art form one whole; but each action is a simple yes/no choice (to act or not).
4. "Perpetual memory"Societies copy their structures over time, like DNA. Dali's melting watches (The Persistence of Memory): even if the watch melts, if the dial and hands survive you can still read the time.

The "ground floor" of daily life (good examples) Must know

Food

  • Every society separates "food" from "non-food", but what counts differs; there are also famine food, festive food and sacred food
  • Two zones: bread (wheat) vs rice. To be invited to eat = to eat bread or rice. Middle Easterners ate both; rice-eaters rejected bread. Dairy disappears in Southeast Asia and China
  • Dishes in the Quran and old Javanese inscriptions are still cooked today (invariance)
  • Change: New World crops (maize, potatoes, tomatoes, chilli); spicy-hot food may date only from the 16th-c. chilli (before, black pepper gave the heat)

Clothing and housing

  • Clothes match social identity almost exactly (tiny differences show gender and status). Unsewn, semi-sewn and fully sewn garments
  • The white unsewn ihram of Muslim hajj pilgrims has the same ritual meaning as a Brahmin's unstitched worship dress (different form, same structure)
  • A house is a 3-D projection of the human body and reflects power in the family and society

Dry zone vs wet zone

Dry (west and north): brick or stone houses, grain farming, fortified villages, a tendency to political fragmentation.

Wet (east and south): bamboo and wooden houses, sloping roofs, coconut, rice fields and fish ponds. Fragile irrigation gave rulers great power to coerce.

Nomads and farmers

Farmers needed animals (camels, oxen, buffalo) often bred by nomads. Nomads needed grain and cloth, and could take them as tribute by force; they also protected caravans. A two-way "symbiosis", with a constant threat of nomad conquest.

Time: rare events explain more

The fewer times something happens, the more it explains. The founding of Islam happened once but changed history; the thousands of meals you eat explain almost nothing.

Unity is partly in the historian's mind

Using Foucault and Gödel (logic always has contradictions), Chaudhuri says the unity of the Indian Ocean or of 622–1750 is a mental construct: historians create the unit that fits their question. "Asia is the contradiction of Europe" ("Asia" makes sense only against "Europe"). Even "Hindu" as a name came through contact with Muslims.

Skip: the maths details (Cantor, Dedekind, transfinite numbers). Just write "he used structuralism, semiotics and set theory".

3. Roy: Indian Ocean trade, 1500–1800 Must know

Roy's argument Indo-European trade built a new economic order: it created new consumer markets in Europe, shaped world money markets (silver), pushed Europeans to colonise in the 18th century, and moved technology both ways. There was so much two-way traffic that it was not simply "Europe influencing India" or India being "absorbed" into a European world economy (contrast Wallerstein, S3).

(a) The Indian Ocean in 1500: a crowded world

India in the middle of the "great arc"

  • No single merchant group covered the whole arc. After 1400, direct West Asia–China trade declined, so Indian ports grew as stops to change ships.
  • Ports were transit points, markets and production centres.
  • Exports: textiles (silk, muslin), rice, sugar, cotton, indigo. Imports: gold, silver, horses, and spices for re-export.
  • Route to Europe: Cambay or Calicut → Aden or Hormuz → Suez or Basra → caravans to Cairo or Aleppo → Venetian merchants.

Merchant groups and ports

  • Gujarati Muslims (Cambay), Mapillas (Malabar), Chulias and Telugu Chettis (Coromandel), Bengalis and Oriyas; each linked to shipbuilding
  • No port was tied to a big inland empire (e.g. Calicut's small Samudri king)
  • New hubs: Masulipatnam (Golkonda) and Surat (Mughal from 1576)
Lecture: "Europeans entered a crowded and functioning commercial world, not an empty stage."

(b) The Portuguese: violent but limited

Why so violent?

To push Muslim merchants out of the spice trade, plus a crusading spirit. It worked at first: pepper prices in Europe stabilised and profits were huge (much trade went on via Antwerp).
vs

Why did it fail? (debate)

Steensgaard: it was "redistributive", living off others' profits (protection money) rather than making its own. Others: strong Asian states (Mughals, Ottomans, Persians, Japan) resisted.

Bengal example: the adventurer Sebastião Gonzales seized Sandwip island from the Mughals (1607), betrayed his allies, and lost it to Arakan (1616). Lecture: the Portuguese changed the ocean but never fully controlled it.

(c) The East India Companies

What was new: organisation

  • Joint-stock finance (many investors, shared risk)
  • Monopoly charters
  • Forts, factories (trading stations), armed ships
  • A single company could send credible diplomatic missions to Indian courts
Roy's strongest point: organisation mattered as much as military force.

Three problems of the English Company

  1. Monopoly hard to enforce: rival merchants and "interlopers"; bribes to local kings
  2. Private trade by its own employees, which it could not control ("the more capable the officer, the bigger the conflict of interest")
  3. Embassies of limited use: Mughal orders didn't reach the south; local governors blocked them; disputes over customs and the right to mint coins

What was traded: spices (early 1600s) → Indian cotton textiles, saltpetre, Chinese silk (1650–1750) → Chinese tea (late 1700s), paid for by Indian opium. Indian cloth also paid for spices in Indonesia and slaves in Africa. Trade ran on American silver, cheaper in Europe and in great demand in India. By mid-century the EIC had about 50% of the textile market.

(d) Europeans depended on Indians

Indian agency Om Prakash: Europeans "had no option but to operate within the given" Asian system. Asian traders controlled almost everything except the final sale and sea shipment: overland, river and inter-regional trade.

Bankers

Turned imported silver into coins, lent money, changed currencies and sent money by promissory notes (hundis). The Jagatseths of Bengal; bankers even supplied military intelligence.

Brokers

Took company advances and gave out cloth orders to many sub-contractors; knew the market and language. Relations mixed dependence and distrust.

Kasi Viranna (Madras); Kola Venkadri, jailed by the English for secret talks with Golkonda; Amirchand, who plotted against Siraj ud-Daula (1756) and was punished by Clive; Ananda Ranga Pillai's diary describes the hard job.

"Contract" was new

Hindu and Islamic law dealt with debt, not sales contracts, so the word "contract" was probably brought by Europeans. Written contracts fixed quantity, price, quality and advances; they helped bookkeeping and carried moral force.

Coastal shipping and technology

Indian communities owned coastal boats (dhows, dhonis). Technology moved both ways: Europeans copied Indian cloth printing; India learned from European navigation (shipyards like Narsapur Peta; European-style anchors).

(e) Port towns: Madras, Bombay, Calcutta

From tenants to landlords

In Surat and Masulipatnam the English were only tenants sharing rights. In Madras (c.1640), Bombay (c.1660) and Calcutta (c.1690) they were landlords with their own laws, courts and forts. Indian merchants and artisans moved in for safety.

Founders like Francis Day, Aungier and Job Charnock clashed with London directors; local officers rebelled (Winter in Madras, 1665; Keigwin in Bombay, 1679), so town governors got the power to make laws.

Why they mattered (lecture)

New political-commercial spaces: more independent of inland powers, ocean-facing, with commercial law that attracted capital, and bases for growing company power. Roy calls them a major turning point.

Maratha raids in the 1740s made Calcutta a safe haven; its population doubled.

(f) From trade to empire

1707Aurangzeb dies; the Mughal empire breaks into regional states
1740–60Three Carnatic wars: English vs French (linked to European wars); the English win
1757–64Bengal: a dispute over taxing Europeans → Plassey (1757), Buxar (1764); the EIC becomes ruler of Bengal (1765)
1772 / 1813War debts → a loan from Parliament and closer control / end of the Company's trade monopoly
Trade → empire (the "bridge point" of the lecture) Trade did not automatically create empire, but it created the conditions: forts piled up power; companies blurred trade and sovereignty (merchant + state); Mughal decline opened space; port cities became bases for conquest. Violence mattered, but Europeans still depended on Indian brokers, bankers and shipwrights: empire grew through entanglement, not replacement. (And Asian trade before 1500 was not purely peaceful either.)

Discussion: if Europeans depended on Indians, what was new? Not better commerce, but organisation (joint-stock companies, charters), armed shipping and forts, and above all port cities with their own law, which, as the Mughals collapsed, became bases for conquest.

4. Chaudhuri vs Roy, and the limits of "connected space" Must know

ChaudhuriRoy

Connection as theoryConnection as commercial practice

They are complementary, not opposed.

Strengths of the idea

Breaks national history boundaries; decentres Europe; shows mobility and interdependence.
but

Limits

May flatten local differences; focuses on merchants over labourers and peasants; may hide coercion, slavery and inequality; makes connection look smoother than it was. "Connected for whom? On whose terms?"
Conclusion (lecture) Unity did not mean sameness. Trade was central but not the whole story (ecology, religion, migration, empire). European expansion transformed, but did not create, Indian Ocean connectivity. It was "a connected, contested and constantly re-made world", significant "not because it erased difference, but because it connected different worlds in durable, unequal and transformative ways".

Write it like this (essay outline, 15 marks)
Q. "European expansion transformed, but did not create, Indian Ocean connectivity." Discuss.

Intro: "Connected space" means structured interaction across distance, not sameness. The Indian Ocean was connected long before 1500.

Before Europe: monsoon trade from Baghdad to Canton with India at the centre (Bernstein); monks on merchant ships (Sen); Tamil temples from Java to Quanzhou (Guy). Chaudhuri shows "unity in the large" across four civilisations from 622, with a shared ground floor of daily life.

Europeans arrive: the Portuguese (1498) used violence and the cartaz, but never fully controlled trade (Steensgaard vs strong Asian states). The EIC and VOC brought joint-stock organisation, but depended on Indian bankers and brokers such as Kasi Viranna and Amirchand (Roy).

Transformation: port towns (Madras, Bombay, Calcutta) with their own law; Mughal decline after 1707; Plassey and Buxar turned trade into empire.

But: connection was unequal (force, slavery, coerced weavers). "Connected for whom?"

Conclusion: Europe changed who held power, but the network itself was much older.
