Indian Schools of Historical Thought

E. Sreedharan, A Textbook of Historiography, chs. 14–16 + Lecture 4 (Said, Cambridge School). Very likely essay topic.
In one lineIndian history has been written by different groups, each answering the one before: Orientalists recovered ancient India, colonial writers called it backward, nationalists defended it, Marxists looked at economy and class, and Subaltern historians looked at ordinary people.

Picture it: the chain of schools (learn this order)

Each school reacted to the one above it. The Cambridge School and the Subaltern school both challenged nationalist history, from opposite sides.

1. Orientalists (Indology) Must know

Problem they solved: the British thought ancient India had "no history" (no proper written history books). So history had to be rebuilt from texts, languages, inscriptions, coins and ruins.

What they achieved

  • William Jones founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784).
  • He found that Sanskrit is related to Greek, Latin and English (the Indo-European language family).
  • He matched the Greek "Sandrocottas" with Chandragupta Maurya, giving the first firm date, 325 BC (the "sheet anchor" of Indian chronology).
  • Prinsep read the Brahmi script of Ashoka's inscriptions (1834); Cunningham started archaeology (ASI).

The problems

  • From "shared language" they jumped to a shared "Aryan race", which was later used to justify British (white) superiority and an Aryan–Dravidian divide.
  • Exaggeration ("Sanskrit more perfect than Greek").
  • Edward Said (Orientalism, 1978): this knowledge was also power. It helped Britain classify and rule India. Translating Hindu law books helped British courts govern Indians; Jones himself was a judge.

2. Colonial / imperialist history Must know

James Mill, History of British India (1818)

He never visited India, knew no Indian language, and used only hostile sources. He called Hindu society one of the "rudest" states of mind.

He split history into Hindu, Muslim and British periods (still used today). His book was the textbook for British officers at Haileybury College.

A perfect example of bad source criticism (S2).

The colonial "story" in 3 myths

  1. Static India: it never changed.
  2. Oriental despotism: cruel kings and powerless villages.
  3. So Britain brings progress, which justifies colonial rule.

Others: Elphinstone (fairer, "Tacitus of India"), Elliot & Dowson (attacked Muslim rule), Vincent Smith (textbook writer).

3. Nationalist history Must know

What they did (strengths)

  • Defended Indian civilisation and showed a "golden age".
  • K.P. Jayaswal, Hindu Polity (1918): ancient India had republics, not just despots.
  • R.C. Dutt and Naoroji: the drain of wealth to Britain, which began economic history.
  • Started regional history (e.g. south India by Nilakanta Sastri).
but

Problems (weaknesses)

  • Exaggeration and chauvinism: e.g. claims of aeroplanes in the epics.
  • Used sources "like a lawyer trying to win a case" (Basham on Jayaswal).
  • Thapar: glorified the past as "compensation for a humiliating present".
  • Hindu-centred, which fed communal history.

4. Marxist "new history" Must know

Big idea: not new sources, but new questions about the old ones: Who produced what? Who owned land? Who worked for whom?

D.D. Kosambi (the founder)

History = "successive developments in the means and relations of production", i.e. tools, and who controls work.

The iron plough cleared forests, tribes broke up, and caste grew as a new way to organise farm labour.

R.S. Sharma · Romila Thapar · Irfan Habib

Sharma: "Indian feudalism" (land grants to Brahmins and officials, declining towns). Thapar: Ashoka's dhamma was a social and political policy; no massive "Aryan invasion". Habib: Mughal peasants were crushed by the land-revenue demand.

Why it was an advance

From kings and battles to ordinary people, the economy and society; used archaeology and other disciplines; destroyed the "static India" and "Aryan race" myths.
but

Criticism

Class can become a "master explanation" for everything, ignoring religion, culture and ideas; heavy jargon; Marxists themselves disagreed.

5. Cambridge School vs Subaltern Studies Must know

Cambridge School (Anil Seal, Gallagher)

The freedom movement was mainly Indian elites competing for power, jobs and patronage, through local politics and factions.Critics: this ignores ideals and mass anger against British rule.
vs

Subaltern Studies (Ranajit Guha, 1982)

"Subaltern" (from Gramsci) = people of low rank. Peasants, workers and tribals had their own politics; they were not just followers of leaders.Peasant revolts in Awadh; how peasants saw Gandhi as a miracle-worker (Shahid Amin).

Guha's point: both colonial and nationalist history are "elitist". One says the British made India; the other says Indian leaders freed it. Neither gives credit to ordinary people.

Write it like this: the professor's essay (15 marks)
Q. Was the shift from colonial and nationalist history to Marxist "new history" a major advance?

Intro: Each school of Indian history reflected the politics of its time (Hobsbawm: the past is used for present needs).

Colonial history (James Mill, 1818) described India as static and despotic to justify British rule, and used biased sources. Nationalist history (Jayaswal's republics, Dutt's drain of wealth) answered with pride and opened economic history, but often exaggerated and glorified a "golden age".

Marxist history was an advance because it asked new questions of old sources: production, land and class (Kosambi on the plough and caste; Sharma on feudalism; Thapar on Ashoka). It moved history from kings to ordinary people and used archaeology and other disciplines.

But it could make class a rigid master key, ignoring culture and religion. The Cambridge School stressed elite politics, and Subaltern Studies (Guha) argued that even Marxists missed peasants' own politics.

Conclusion: it was a real advance in method and scope, but not the final word. History keeps being rewritten.
