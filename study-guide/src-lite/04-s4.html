Indian Schools of Historical Thought

E. Sreedharan, A Textbook of Historiography, chs. 14–16 + Lecture 4 (adds Said and the Cambridge School). Very likely essay topic; the professor's model essay is from here.
In one lineIndian history has been written by different groups, each reacting to the one before: Orientalists recovered ancient India, colonial writers called it backward, nationalists defended it, Marxists studied economy and class, the Cambridge School studied elite politics, and Subaltern historians studied ordinary people.

Picture it: the chain of schools (learn this order)

1. The starting problem: ancient India "without history"? Must know

  • For medieval India there were Muslim court chronicles, so the British knew it fairly well. But ancient India seemed to have no proper history books, so it had to be rebuilt from indirect sources: literature, inscriptions, coins, monuments, sculpture.
  • Sreedharan: the traditional Hindu view did not treat everyday history as "useful knowledge"; there was a vague, exaggerated sense of age. In 1808 the pundit Mrtyunjay Sharma wrote a Bengali history with kings ruling for lakhs of years.
  • Early British writers: Robert Orme (1764, military history; mocked Hindu gods), Francis Gladwin (Mughal history), William Robertson (collected Greek and Roman mentions of India). Ancient India stayed "a land of marvel and mystery".

Exam point: Indian history had to be "built from fragments". This is the problem Indology tried to solve.

2. Orientalists / Indology Must know

Indology = the systematic study of India's history, languages, literature, religions and culture.

Why did the British study India? (5 influences)

  1. Enlightenment: differences between peoples come from culture, not nature, which led to tolerance and curiosity (Voltaire admired India and China).
  2. Romanticism: fascination with ancient, mysterious places.
  3. A new scientific, critical way of handling sources.
  4. Comparative scholarship.
  5. Ruling needs: officials had to know Indian languages, law and culture. Governor-General Warren Hastings was the great patron: he learned Persian and loved the Gita.

Early British Indologists

  • Nathaniel Halhed: Gentoo Laws (1776), Bengali grammar
  • Charles Wilkins: first direct English translation of the Bhagavad Gita (1784)
  • Gladwin: abridged Ain-i-Akbari
  • Jonathan Duncan: encouraged Hindu learning

Indology came from a mix of curiosity, administrative need, patronage and imperial rule.

William Jones (1746–1794) and the Asiatic Society

A Welsh language genius, judge of the Supreme Court in Calcutta (1783). In January 1784 he founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal (modelled on the Royal Society, London), which made Indian research organised and collaborative. He translated Shakuntala (1789) (which thrilled Goethe and the German Romantics), the Gita Govinda and Manusmriti.

After Jones: two lines of work

(1) Studying ancient texts

  • Colebrooke: Essay on the Vedas (1803); pictured the Vedic age as a golden age (no sati, no caste, no idols)
  • Anquetil Duperron: translated the Upanishads (via Dara Shukoh's Persian); admired by Schopenhauer
  • Burnouf: made Buddhism known in the West
  • Max Müller: edited the Rig Veda; Sacred Books of the East (50 volumes)

Problem: texts had to be dated and used carefully.

+

(2) Inscriptions, coins, archaeology

  • Epigraphy = study of inscriptions; numismatics = study of coins
  • James Prinsep (Calcutta Mint) read Brahmi in 1834, turning the legendary Ashoka into a historical emperor. "Reading one script can open a whole historical world."
  • Alexander Cunningham: father of Indian archaeology, head of the ASI (Sarnath, Sanchi, Taxila)
  • Colin Mackenzie collected 8,000+ south Indian inscriptions; later Harappa showed civilisation existed before the Vedic Aryans

Achievements

  • Brought modern methods (source criticism, philology, epigraphy, archaeology, coins) to Indian history
  • Fixed a chronology (325 BC)
  • Revealed Indian literature and philosophy to the world, a "Renaissance in the East"
  • Irony: as India was conquered, British scholars showed it was a great civilisation
but

The "seamy side" (Sreedharan)

  • The language → race link was false, yet "white Aryan superiority" became a justification for British rule
  • Aryan invasion theory: superior Aryans conquered "inferior" Dravidians and created caste, an Aryan–Dravidian divide
  • Everything good in India was credited to European (Aryan) origins
  • Exaggeration (Jones: "Sanskrit more perfect than Greek"), later fuelling Indian chauvinism

Reading Orientalism through Edward Said (lecture) Must know

Said, Orientalism (1978): European scholarship on "the East" was not just study; it was part of a relationship of power. "The Orient" was not simply discovered; it was constructed by European knowledge.

3 meanings of "Orientalism"

  1. An academic field (studying Asian languages and cultures)
  2. A style of thought (dividing the world into "West" and "East")
  3. A discourse of power (knowledge that helped Europe rule the East)

How knowledge helped rule

  • Translating Hindu law → British courts could apply "native law"
  • Learning Sanskrit and Persian → officials could govern
  • Classifying castes and religions → society became "readable" for the state
  • Archaeological surveys → India's past catalogued under colonial authority
The balanced verdict (use this) The Orientalists genuinely helped recover ancient India, but this knowledge was produced within colonial power, and the same person was often scholar, judge and ruler (Jones was a judge). So the result was double-edged: India was revealed, but also classified and represented through European categories.

3. Colonial / imperialist history Must know

After Plassey (1757), three British groups argued over how to rule India:

GroupWhoView
Conservative / OrientalistHastings, Jones, Colebrooke; later Elphinstone, MunroRespect Indian civilisation; change slowly
EvangelicalCharles Grant, missionariesIndian society is "hideous"; cure it with Christianity and Western education
UtilitarianBentham, James MillRemake India through laws, education and strong (despotic) government

James Mill, History of British India (1818)

  • A utilitarian who never visited India and knew no Indian language. He decided his conclusions before writing and used only hostile witnesses (missionaries, travellers), ignoring favourable evidence (a failure of source criticism, S2).
  • He called Hindu society one of the "rudest" states of mind; even feudal Europe was superior; British rule was best.
  • He divided history into Hindu, Muslim and British periods (still used!).
  • Influence: Macaulay praised it; it was the textbook at Haileybury College (1805–55), where British civil servants were trained, so they arrived with "imperial dogmatism".
  • Critics: H.H. Wilson said it destroyed sympathy between rulers and ruled; R.C. Majumdar said it "does not deserve the name" of history.

Others in this school

  • Mountstuart Elphinstone (1841): fairer and more sympathetic; built a sound chronology from Chandragupta; laid the foundation of cultural history ("Tacitus of India"); yet still said Hindus lacked "veracity".
  • Elliot & Dowson (8 volumes, 1867–77): translated Persian chronicles but attacked Muslim rule, so Indians would value "the mildness of our rule".
  • Ideas of the age: race superiority, Social Darwinism, Kipling's "White Man's Burden" (1899), the "civilising mission".

Administrator-historians (c.1870–1940): all concluded British rule must continue

The 4 assumptions of imperialist history (memorise)
  1. Static India: it never changed (Mill), so Hegel placed India outside world history.
  2. Oriental despotism: cruel kings over self-contained villages with no private land. Marx turned this into the "Asiatic Mode of Production".
  3. Purpose: if India never changed, Britain could claim to be the agent of change, which justified its rule.
  4. Political bias: moral judgements, focus on the British period, written for English readers. Morley told Gokhale that a fur coat needed in Canada was not needed in the Deccan (no self-rule for India).

4. Nationalist history: defending Indian civilisation Must know

Context: educated Indians wanted to become "more Indian and less English" and needed a proud history. Bankim Chandra: "There is no Hindu history. Who will praise our noble qualities if we do not praise them ourselves?" They reacted against Mill, Elphinstone and Smith, and against claims that Indians borrowed everything (e.g. that the sine in trigonometry must be Greek; Needham showed India used it from the Gupta age). R.C. Majumdar: a nationalist historian is not necessarily "a propagandist or a charlatan".

AreaWhat nationalists argued

Strengths

  • The greatest stimulus to historical research in India: scholars went to the sources
  • Moved history from collecting facts to interpretation
  • Regional history (Bengal, Maharashtra, the south: Nilakanta Sastri)
  • Birth of economic history: Naoroji's drain theory, Dutt's Economic History of India (1904)
  • Cultural history (Tagore, Panikkar)
but

Weaknesses

  • History made to serve present politics. Basham: Jayaswal used sources "like a barrister trying to win a case"
  • Chauvinism. Thapar: glorifying the past was "a compensation for the humiliating present"
  • Contradictions: military glory vs non-violence; spiritual vs worldly
  • Hindu-centred stories of Rajputs, Marathas and Sikhs vs Muslims fed communalism

Names the lecture stresses: R.G. Bhandarkar (careful, impartial "dry truth"), R.C. Majumdar (editor of the 11-volume History and Culture of the Indian People; "History, divorced from truth, does not help a nation"), K.P. Jayaswal, H.C. Raychaudhuri (Political History of Ancient India, 1923). Also Jadunath Sarkar (Mughals; ruthless with sources), K.M. Panikkar (India and the Indian Ocean), Nilakanta Sastri (south India, the Cholas).

5. Marxist "new history" Must know

What made it "new" Not mainly new evidence, but "a re-reading of the sources with a different set of questions": class relations, modes of production, land and farming, surplus (who takes the extra produce), caste and economy, state formation. History became a social science.

D.D. Kosambi (the founder)

  • History = "the presentation, in chronological order, of successive developments in the means and relations of production" (tools, and who controls work).
  • Even without dates, we can know how people lived. He used archaeology, fieldwork, tribes, language and coin weights: India is full of "living survivals" of the past.
  • From tribe to caste: the plough (spread by Brahmin settlement) broke up tribes, and caste became the new way to organise labour.
  • Buddhism and Jainism had economic roots: new traders and landowners wanted peace; ahimsa saved cattle needed for ploughing.

R.S. Sharma · Romila Thapar · Irfan Habib · Bipan Chandra

  • R.S. Sharma: Indian Feudalism (land grants to Brahmins and officials, decline of towns and trade after the Guptas); Sudras in Ancient India; Urban Decay (from archaeology).
  • Romila Thapar: Ashoka's dhamma was a social and political policy to hold a diverse empire together; no massive "Aryan invasion" (small groups spread the language); From Lineage to State.
  • Irfan Habib: Mughal empire's heavy land-revenue demand crushed peasants, who revolted; the empire was "its own grave-digger".
  • Bipan Chandra: economic nationalism; attacked the Cambridge School (nationalism was a people's movement); communalism was a product of colonialism.

Why it was an advance

  • From kings and battles to society and ordinary people
  • Interdisciplinary: archaeology, anthropology, statistics
  • Strong on origins: how states formed in the Ganga valley
  • Explanation, not just narrative; processes over events
  • Destroyed old myths: "static India", Oriental despotism, the "Aryan race"
but

Criticism

  • Same theory, different conclusions (Sharma and Thapar disagreed on Buddhism and slavery)
  • Weak proof (e.g. how many cattle were really sacrificed?)
  • Jargon ("exercises in obscurity")
  • Lecture: class can become a rigid "master explanation", ignoring culture, religion, region and ideas

6. Cambridge School vs Subaltern Studies Must know

Cambridge School (1960s–80s)

Anil Seal, John Gallagher, Gordon Johnson, C.A. Bayly. The freedom movement was mainly about local politics, patron–client networks, elite competition, factions, and bargaining with the British. Nationalism was the "response of the Indian elite to British stimuli".Critics (Bipan Chandra, Guha): it ignores ideals, mass politics and real anti-colonial feeling.
vs

Subaltern Studies (Ranajit Guha, 1982)

"Subaltern" (from Gramsci) = people of lower rank (by class, caste, gender). Both colonial and nationalist histories are "elitist": one credits the British, the other credits Indian leaders. Neither can explain mass uprisings (1919 anti-Rowlatt, 1942 Quit India). Peasants, workers and tribals had their own autonomous politics; a peasant rebelled consciously when petitions failed.

Write it like this: the professor's essay (15 marks)
Q. Was the shift from colonial and nationalist history to Marxist "new history" a major advance?

Intro: Each school of Indian history reflected the politics of its time (Hobsbawm: the past is used for present needs). Marxist history was a real advance in method and scope, but not the final word.

Colonial history: James Mill (1818), who never visited India, used hostile sources and described India as static and despotic, which justified British rule. His Hindu–Muslim–British periodisation trained Haileybury officials. Maine and Smith repeated these assumptions.

Nationalist history answered with pride: Jayaswal's ancient republics, Mookerji's "fundamental unity", and Dutt's and Naoroji's drain theory, which began economic history. But it often glorified a golden age "as compensation for the humiliating present" (Thapar) and fed communal history.

Marxist history asked new questions of old sources: production, land and class. Kosambi showed how the plough turned tribes into caste society; Sharma studied feudalism; Thapar saw Ashoka's dhamma as policy and rejected the Aryan invasion; Habib showed peasant revolts against Mughal revenue. It moved history from kings to society, used archaeology and destroyed colonial myths.

Limits: class could become a rigid master explanation and the jargon was heavy. The Cambridge School stressed elite bargaining, and Subaltern Studies (Guha, 1982) showed that even Marxists missed peasants' own politics.

Conclusion: a major advance in scope and method, but history keeps being rewritten from new angles.
