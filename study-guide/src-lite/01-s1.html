Why Study History?

E.J. Hobsbawm, "The Social Function of the Past: Some Questions", Past & Present (1972) · Hobsbawm, On History (1997), ch. 3 (covered through the PPT)
In one lineEvery society uses its past. The real question is how: as a pattern to copy, a model to choose from, or a process that explains how we got here. The past is often selected, reshaped or even invented for present needs.

Who is Hobsbawm? A famous British Marxist historian who wrote on class, nationalism, capitalism, revolution and empire. His 1972 essay started as a conference paper in 1970. It mostly raises questions rather than giving final answers ("It is easier to formulate questions than answers").

1. The core argument Must know

"The past is a permanent dimension of the human consciousness" In simple words: no person or society can escape its past.
  • Everyone is aware of a past because we all live with people older than us (parents, grandparents).
  • Every society has a past. Even brand-new colonies are made of people from old societies.
  • To belong to a community means placing yourself in relation to its past, even if you reject it.
  • So the question is not whether societies have a past, but how they use it: as authority, a moral guide, a political weapon, an identity, or a way to understand change.
  • The historian's job is to study this "sense of the past" and how it changes. The past is not just inherited; it is organised and used.

"We swim in the past as fish do in water, and cannot escape from it."

2. Picture it: three ways a society uses its past

The faster a society changes, the more it moves from left to right.

3. Past as PATTERN: traditional societies Must know

  • For most of history, the past was the pattern for the present. Each generation tried to copy the one before and felt it had failed if it did not.
  • Old customs, ancestors' authority, sacred texts and old law were treated as rules. History here is not a story of progress but a storehouse of precedents ("what was done before").
  • In societies without writing, law = custom, and old documents gain almost spiritual authority.
A village claims its common lands because "we have always used them", or even cites an old colonial court decision as proof. The past works like a charter (licence) for the present.

How does a traditional society change? (key point)

Traditional societies are NOT frozen Hobsbawm calls the idea that traditional society never changes "a myth of vulgar social science". Change does happen, but it is absorbed in ways that do not openly challenge tradition.

Four ways change sneaks in

  1. Gradually: so slowly that nobody notices.
  2. Hidden (concealed): done without being admitted.
  3. Renamed: "new wine in old bottles". Nomads who traded camels began trading lorries and saw it as the same old business.
  4. Stretching the framework: old myths and rituals are stretched to fit the new thing.

Three mechanisms for innovation without breaking tradition

  1. The official "past" is only a selection of everything remembered, so there are gaps (interstices) where new things can enter unnoticed.
  2. Technology is the most flexible area. Tradition-bound peasants happily took up horses or new weapons without feeling they were breaking with the past.
  3. Really big innovations are made acceptable by disguising them as a return to a forgotten past, or by a higher authority such as a religious revelation or a prophet.

Tradition is strong not because it blocks all change, but because it can absorb change while seeming to stay the same.

What if change is too fast?

This kind of tradition needs slow change. If change is too fast, tradition survives only by:

  • Isolation: the Amish in the USA cut themselves off.
  • Accepting one huge forced step at great human cost: the forced conversion of Mexican Indians to Catholicism after the Spanish conquest.

"Tradition" itself changes

What "has always been done" changes over time without people noticing.

What was "traditional" for Bulgarian peasants in 1850 was very different from earlier centuries, yet they still felt traditional.

Such a past fits ideas of cycles or decline, but not the idea of continuous progress.

4. Past as MODEL: when change speeds up Must know

  • When change becomes too fast, the past "must cease to be the pattern of the present, and can at best become the model for it".
  • Key difference: a pattern is copied automatically; a model is consciously chosen, selected and adapted.
  • The past becomes a "mask for innovation": calls to "go back" actually produce something new.
  • Big insight: when a society starts loudly demanding restoration, continuity has already broken. The past has moved from lived tradition to a conscious reference point.

Restoration, and the problems with Zapata (key point)

Hobsbawm is sceptical of the word "restoration", because you can never simply bring back the past. It always involves selection, rebuilding or reinventing.

Example: Zapata and the peasants of Morelos (Mexico)

The peasants led by Zapata wanted to restore their society to what it had been about 40 years earlier, before the rule of Porfirio Díaz took their lands. Why it could not work as a real restoration (3 problems):

  1. Memory problem: they had to reconstruct things nobody could remember exactly (e.g. the precise boundaries of the old common lands), so they ended up imagining "what ought to have been".
  2. It could not be cut out: the change they hated was not "a bullet lodged in the flesh" that could be surgically removed. It was part of a much wider change in society.
  3. Bigger effects than intended: the movement released forces far larger than its aim. The peasants became a revolutionary power, and the attempt to turn the clock back became a social revolution that helped create "a historically unprecedented new Mexico".

(Hobsbawm uses John Womack's biography of Zapata, 1969.) Lesson: trying to go back to the past can produce something completely new.

Effective restoration

Tries to bring back the actual old practices and institutions.Calls to restore "old-time religion" (everyone in church); 19th-century British radicals wanting to bring back small peasant farmers.
vs

Symbolic restoration

Brings back only the feeling, symbol or meaning, not the real old world (often because what people want back, like "greatness" or "freedom", is too vague).Rebuilding Warsaw's bombed old town after WWII; pulling down the Stalin monument in Prague; Churchill insisting the bombed Parliament be rebuilt exactly as before.

5. The invented past: artefact or "fabrication" Must know

  • If the past people appeal to is very remote, it becomes just a language for present wishes: an artefact or, "in less flattering terms, a fabrication".
  • Such a past is only weakly linked to real history but is politically useful: it can be arranged into a story that justifies the present. Even recent, made-up traditions can be very powerful.

Examples of remote / invented pasts

  • English radicals' "free Anglo-Saxons before the Norman Yoke" and "Merrie England".
  • "Charlemagne" used as a symbol for European unity since Napoleon, though nobody wants 9th-century Europe back.
  • The new country "Ghana" took the name of an old empire from a different place.
  • Zionism's "return" to pre-diaspora Israel in practice denied 2,000 years of actual Jewish history.

Nationalism: the clearest case

Renan: nations "get their history wrong". Their goals are new, but they describe them as historic (especially claims to territory), and they revive old things on purpose.

Welsh "druids"; Hebrew revived as a spoken language; the Nazis' medieval-style castles. Hobsbawm: "History is the most easily worked raw material" for making nations. (Indian nationalists' "golden age", S4.)

Contrast: in truly traditional societies, restoring a remote past is meant literally. Peruvian peasants (to the 1920s) rose up to restore the Inca Empire; for them the Incas were "yesterday".

6. Past as PROCESS: modern societies Must know

  • People reject the past only when innovation is seen as unavoidable and good, i.e. as "progress". In modern culture "new" and "revolutionary" (as in adverts) mean "better".
  • People accept technological change far more easily than social or moral change (family, sex, religion). "Has there ever been a serious Luddism against bicycles or transistor radios?" Popular religion accepted cheap printed holy pictures but fought changes to holy texts.
  • The past now works as a process: the story of development and evolution that explains how the present came to be. Bagehot's Physics and Politics (1872); modern "modernisation" theory.
  • Even conservatives start thinking historically ("even conservative thought becomes historicist").
  • Paradox: "the more change dominates social life, the more important history becomes" as a tool for understanding it.
The problem of the future Even people designing a totally new future look for examples in the past. Socialists pointed to "primitive communism" as a precedent. But history shows tendencies, not exact outcomes. Marx was sure socialism would replace capitalism but said almost nothing about what socialism would look like. So "History ceases to be of use at the very moment when we need it most." We then dip into the past for useful precedents. What happened to the poor displaced by 19th-century railways helps us think about 20th-century motorways; medieval "student power" helps us think about modern universities.

7. Genealogy: searching for ancestors Must know

Genealogy means more than family trees: groups place themselves in a line of ancestors to gain continuity, prestige, legitimacy and comfort for their insecurity. New nations, new elites and revolutionary movements all want heroic ancestors, even symbolic ones.

Revolutionaries claiming Spartacus, Thomas More and Winstanley as ancestors (although Marxist theory says Roman slave revolts could not lead to communism); schoolbooks teaching Boadicea, King Alfred and Joan of Arc; newly rich people buying aristocratic pedigrees; Stalin-era claims that Russians invented everything.

8. Chronology and historical thinking (key point) Must know

For modern historians, dates matter

Chronology = putting events in the right order, with dates and duration. Modern history assumes history is about change in one direction, so order matters.

Anachronism (putting something in the wrong time) is an "alarm signal" for historians.

Macbeth acted in modern dress shocks us today, but did not shock audiences in Shakespeare's time.

In traditional uses, dates don't matter

When the past is a pattern, model or moral lesson, the moral or symbolic meaning of a story matters more than when it happened.

Romans and Moors marching together in Spanish Easter processions; only pedants care whether Horatius came before Mucius Scaevola; Masada and the Maccabees matter to modern Israelis for their meaning, not their dates.
  • When real chronology is brought into a sacred or mythical past (e.g. scholarly dating of Homer or the Bible), it disturbs society and is a sign of transformation.
  • Yet dates still matter for practical purposes (law, administration, ritual). A lawyer looks for a precedent to use now; a historian cares about how different past circumstances were.

9. On History (1997), ch. 3: what can history tell us about today? Must know

Link between the two texts: the 1972 essay asks how societies live with the past; On History asks how historians use the past to explain the present. You cannot understand the present without its history. History is a tool of demystification (removing false beliefs).

History CAN

  • Show the roots of present institutions
  • Show how nations, identities and traditions were built
  • Reveal that many "new" things are part of longer developments
  • Separate real breaks from recycled rhetoric
vs

History CANNOT

  • Give mechanical lessons
  • Predict exactly what happens next
  • Replace political judgement
  • Give a ready-made programme for action

Write it like this (10 marks)
Q. Explain Hobsbawm's idea of the past as pattern, model and process.

Hobsbawm (1972) argues that "the past is a permanent dimension of human consciousness": every society uses its past, but how it uses it depends on how fast it changes.

In traditional societies the past is a pattern: people copy inherited customs, and law equals custom. Yet these societies are not static (static tradition is "a myth of vulgar social science"). Change is absorbed gradually, hidden, or renamed, as when nomads treated lorries as part of their camel trade. Innovation enters through gaps in the remembered past, through technology, or disguised as a return to a forgotten past. If change is too fast, tradition survives only through isolation (the Amish) or at great cost (the forced conversion of Mexican Indians).

When change accelerates, the past becomes a model, consciously chosen. Loud calls for "restoration" show that continuity has already broken. Zapata's peasants tried to restore their lands of 40 years earlier, but they had to imagine what they could not remember, the change could not be cut out "like a bullet", and their movement became a revolution that created a new Mexico. Restoration may also be only symbolic, like rebuilding Warsaw.

In modern societies the past becomes a process of progress that explains the present; "new" means "better", and even conservatives think historically.

But the past is often selected or invented, especially by nationalism. History cannot predict the future, though it explains how the present was made.
